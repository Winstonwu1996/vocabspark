# The Russian Revolution · 1917

> **Through-line**: war-collapse (WWI broke the empire) + the double face of revolution (liberation AND a new authoritarian state, born together)
> **Difficulty**: 4/5 (the hardest Rule-0 balance in the set + careful Old Style / New Style date math)
> **Expected duration**: 35-42 min (13-15 turn dialogue)
>
> **Calendar note**: In 1917 Russia still used the Julian calendar (Old Style). It ran **13 days behind** the Western Gregorian calendar (New Style). This file gives New Style dates, with Old Style in parentheses. The names "February Revolution" and "October Revolution" come from the Old Style months — but by the New Style they actually happened in **March** and **November**. That 13-day gap is the easiest thing to get wrong in this lesson.

## 0. Open Your Textbook First (Textbook Anchor)

> **AP World History Modern — Unit 7 (1900-present)**, Topics 7.3-7.4 (the conduct and consequences of WWI, plus the Russian Revolution). **APUSH Period 7 (1898-1945)** — this is not U.S. history, but 1917 is where the 20th-century rival the United States would face in the Cold War was born.
>
> Your textbook will tell you:
> - **WWI put huge strain on Russia.**
> - **In 1917 there were two revolutions, and the Bolsheviks took power.**
> - **Russia became the first communist state.**
>
> **What your textbook may flatten** (this is why we need this lesson):
> - **There were TWO revolutions, not one.** The February Revolution (March, New Style) was a broad, mostly leaderless uprising that toppled the Tsar. The October Revolution (November, New Style) was one tightly organized party seizing power. They were very different events.
> - **The "storming of the Winter Palace" is mostly a myth.** The real night was almost without fighting. The famous heroic image comes mainly from a 1928 film. (Section 10 busts this.)
> - **The revolution that shouted "Peace" led straight into a four-year civil war** that killed millions.
> - **This is the hardest case in the whole course to judge.** It was both a real liberation push by millions of ordinary people AND the start of a state that shut down its own elected assembly. We will hold both. We will not tell you the verdict.
> - **And it directly shaped modern China** — the October Revolution catalyzed the May Fourth Movement (1919) and the founding of the Chinese Communist Party (1921). For a Chinese-American student, this is the moment "foreign history" becomes the source of your own.

## 1. The Hook (Three Views at Once)

In one year, the Russian people tore down a dynasty that had ruled for 300 years. In that same year, power passed to a small group of professional revolutionaries. One revolution shouted "Peace, Land, Bread" — and out of it grew a state that would shut down its own parliament and crush its rivals. In one hungry city (Petrograd), in one year, three people stood in three completely different places:

- **A Petrograd woman textile worker** (a composite character; receiving-end; **the default view**). Her husband is at the front. Every morning before dawn she stands in the freezing bread line. On March 8, 1917 (Old Style February 23), International Women's Day, she walks out of the factory shouting "Bread!" — and lights the first spark of the February Revolution.
- **A young Bolshevik factory-committee organizer** (a composite character; the actor who makes it happen). Twenty-something, a worker who reads the pamphlets and runs the messages. He rides the Red Guard energy all the way to the night the Bolsheviks take the Winter Palace.
- **Alexander Kerensky** (the lonely mediator; 1881-1970). Head of the Provisional Government. He wants a Russia with freedom but no Tsar and no one-party rule — a road down the middle. He is caught between the old order (Tsarists, the future Whites) and the Bolsheviks. Both sides treat him as the enemy. He loses everything.

The anti-Whig core: "workers rising up" (liberation) and "the seed of an authoritarian state" (one-party rule) are **not two separate stories**. They are **two hands of the same revolution**. This lesson does not decide for you whether the revolution was "worth it." It lets you hear both sides at once.

## 2. The Stage (Land / Climate / Society / System / Events)

- **Land (L0)**: The Russian Empire was the largest country on Earth, stretching across Europe and Asia, with about 170 million people — most of them peasants. The capital was **Petrograd** (formerly St. Petersburg; the name was changed after WWI began because the old name sounded too German). It sits on the Baltic coast. Winters are brutal.
- **Climate / scene (L1)**: The winter of 1916-17 was unusually cold. WWI had been grinding on for almost three years. Most of the city's men were at the front. The trains broke down, and food could not reach the city. In early 1917, women had to stand for hours in below-zero wind for a chance at one loaf of bread — and often the bread ran out before their turn. **The bread line** is the real stage of this revolution.
- **Society (L2)**: At the bottom were tens of millions of peasants, freed from serfdom only in 1861, but still landless while landlords held the soil. "Land" was their centuries-old hunger. In the cities were factory workers: long hours, low pay, crowded rooms. At the top were the Tsar, the nobles, and the Church.
- **System (L3)**: Russia was one of Europe's last **absolute monarchies**. Tsar **Nicholas II** held almost total power. A 1905 revolution forced him to create a parliament called the **Duma**, but he could dissolve it whenever he wanted. In 1915 Nicholas made a fatal choice: he **personally went to the front to command the army** (headquarters at Mogilev). Now every defeat was blamed directly on him. Meanwhile the capital was left to the Tsarina Alexandra and her trusted "holy man," Rasputin — which hollowed out the crown's prestige even more.
- **Events (L4)**: March 8, 1917 (OS Feb 23), women's bread strike → within days a citywide general strike and the capital garrison switching sides → March 15 (OS March 2), the Tsar abdicates and the Romanov dynasty ends → the Provisional Government and the Petrograd Soviet stand side by side ("dual power") → April, Lenin returns and issues the April Theses → the July Days → the Kornilov Affair (Aug-Sep) → November 7 (OS Oct 25), the Bolsheviks take the Winter Palace → January 1918, the Constituent Assembly meets for one day and is dissolved → March 1918, the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk pulls Russia out of WWI → 1918-1922, the Russian Civil War (Reds vs Whites).

## 3. The People

> Three views (receiving-end / actor / lonely mediator), including at least one powerless party.

### The Petrograd Woman Textile Worker (receiving-end; default view) — a composite character

She is a composite — not one named woman, but a stand-in for the thousands of women workers who walked out that March day. (Her name, age, and family are narrative fill; what the record confirms is the event itself: on Women's Day, the textile women struck first, and what they shouted was bread.) She is around thirty. She lives in a crowded workers' room in Petrograd. Her husband was taken for the army two years ago and has not come back.

Her day starts in the bread line. She stands in the freezing wind before dawn, for hours. Often the bread is gone before her turn, and she goes home empty-handed to children who are waiting to eat. She cannot read. She has never read a revolutionary pamphlet. But she knows the price of bread, the length of the line, and which child went to sleep hungry last night.

March 8, 1917 (Old Style February 23) was International Women's Day. That morning the textile women did not bow their heads and work. They walked out. They went into the streets shouting "Bread!" They banged on the doors of the metal factory next door and called the men out. No party sent her. She had simply had enough. And it was exactly her walking out that lit the first spark of the February Revolution. In that moment, this receiving-end woman became an actor — the one who **receives** history suddenly became the one who **starts** it.

### The Bolshevik Factory-Committee Organizer (actor) — a composite character

Also a composite (name and age are narrative fill; what the record confirms is that Petrograd's factory committees and Red Guards were the real muscle of the October seizure). A worker in his early twenties at a big Petrograd plant. He can read. A few years ago he secretly read the Bolshevik pamphlets. In 1917 he goes all in.

After the February Revolution, the factories form **factory committees** — workers electing their own people to run things. He is one of the active ones. He works by day, then meets, prints leaflets, and runs messages at night. When Lenin returns in April shouting "All Power to the Soviets," he feels, for the first time, that someone has said his own thoughts out loud. He is not a theorist behind a desk. He is the one at the factory gate pulling people in, standing watch on the corner, putting rifles into the hands of his fellow workers. The **Red Guard** is exactly this kind of factory militia.

On that November night in 1917, it is he and thousands like him who take the bridges, the telegraph office, and the Winter Palace. He is the hand that turns an abstract slogan into a real state. This view lets you feel, from the inside, how a revolution goes from a sentence to a country — and it also makes you face this: what he did both tore down an old world AND turned the first screw on a new state that would swallow dissent. Two things. The same pair of hands.

### Alexander Kerensky (the lonely mediator) — 1881-1970

A real person. Born 1881, a lawyer by training, a powerful speaker, a moderate socialist. After the February Revolution he became a unique figure: he held a seat in **both** rival power centers at once — in the Provisional Government (mostly liberals) AND in the Petrograd Soviet (workers and soldiers). At first this made him look like the glue that could hold the two sides together.

After the July Days in 1917, he became head of the Provisional Government. He faced an impossible spot. The old order's generals (the future Whites) thought he was too far left. The Bolsheviks thought he was too far right — and hated that he kept fighting a war no one wanted. What he wanted was a Russia with freedom, a parliament, no Tsar, and no single-party rule — a road down the middle.

But the middle road had no ground to stand on in 1917. He made one fatal call: he insisted on staying in WWI (he even launched a disastrous offensive in June 1917), when stopping the war was exactly what front-line soldiers and city workers wanted most. In August he first called in General Kornilov to crush the left, then at the last second begged the Bolsheviks to help him stop Kornilov — angering both sides and leaving himself alone. When the Bolsheviks struck in November, he could barely find one loyal unit. He fled Petrograd, spent most of his life in exile, and died in New York in 1970. The man who wanted to hold a free Russia together — and held nothing.

## 4. The Story (Plot Beats)

1. **The war breaks the empire (1914-1917).** Russian losses in WWI were enormous — about 1.7 million dead, millions more wounded or captured. The front ate men; the home front went hungry. In 1915 Nicholas II went to command the front himself, leaving the capital to the Tsarina and Rasputin. The crown's prestige collapsed.
2. **The bread strike lights the fire (March 8 / OS Feb 23, 1917).** On International Women's Day, Petrograd's textile women walked out shouting "Bread!" The next day 200,000 were on strike; the day after, nearly 400,000 — a general strike.
3. **The garrison switches sides (March 12 / OS Feb 27).** The Tsar ordered troops to fire on the crowds. But the capital's soldiers would not shoot their own people. They went over to the uprising and opened the armories. This was the decisive step: with no army, the Tsar was finished.
4. **The Tsar abdicates (March 15 / OS March 2).** On a train at Pskov, nearly every front commander urged Nicholas II to step down. He did — giving up the throne for his son too. The 300-year Romanov dynasty was over.
5. **Dual power.** After the abdication, two centers of power stood side by side. One was the **Provisional Government** (mostly liberals; the government in name). The other was the **Petrograd Soviet** (a council of elected worker and soldier deputies, which held the real loyalty of the factories and the army). Neither could swallow the other.
6. **Lenin returns + the April Theses (April 16 / OS April 3).** After years in exile, Lenin returned to Petrograd's Finland Station in a "sealed train" arranged by Germany. He immediately issued the **April Theses**: no support for the Provisional Government, "All Power to the Soviets," and an immediate exit from the war. The slogan boiled down to three words: **Peace, Land, Bread.** It hit exactly what everyone wanted most.
7. **The July Days (July 16-20 / OS July 3-7).** Workers and soldiers in Petrograd took up arms on their own. The Provisional Government cracked down and blamed the Bolsheviks. Lenin fled. The party looked finished.
8. **The Kornilov Affair (Sep 10-13 / OS Aug 28-31).** Kerensky first called in General Kornilov to crush the left, then panicked that Kornilov would seize power, and begged the Bolsheviks and the Soviet to help stop him. The Bolsheviks armed up and stopped Kornilov — and that turned the humbled party of July back into "the defenders of the revolution." Their popularity shot up.
9. **The October Revolution (Nov 7 / OS Oct 25).** Led by Lenin and Trotsky, the Bolshevik Red Guards took the bridges, the telegraph office, and the stations by night, and finally "took" the Winter Palace — the seat of the Provisional Government. The next day they declared power to the Soviets.
10. **The Constituent Assembly is dissolved (January 1918).** Even after the seizure, the planned national election went ahead. The Bolsheviks won only about a quarter of the vote; the Socialist Revolutionaries won a majority. In January 1918, the Constituent Assembly met for one day (about 13 hours) and was forced shut by Bolshevik-controlled forces. This is the dividing line between the "liberation" story and the "one-party" story: Russia's only freely elected national assembly lasted one day.
11. **Leaving the war: Brest-Litovsk (March 3, 1918).** To deliver "Peace," the new state signed the harsh **Treaty of Brest-Litovsk** with Germany, giving up huge lands (about a third of the empire's people, over half its industry) in exchange for leaving WWI.
12. **The Civil War (1918-1922).** Leaving the foreign war bought a civil war. The **Red Army** (Bolsheviks) fought the **White Army** (a loose mix of Tsarists, liberals, and other anti-Bolshevik forces), and foreign powers sent troops too. It lasted four years. The Reds won — but at the cost of millions dead from combat, repression, and famine. The Soviet state was forged in that blood.

**The irony**: a revolution that shouted "Peace" fell within a year into a bloodier civil war; an assembly that was elected was shut after one day "in the name of the people"; the woman who lit the fire wanted only a loaf of bread — and the people who ended up on top were a small group of professional revolutionaries she had never met.

## 5. Reading the Documents (Primary Source)

### A. The core slogan of Lenin's April Theses
> Original (common English): "Peace, Land, Bread" + "All Power to the Soviets!"
> Plain reading: peace, land, and bread — and let the Soviets hold the power.
> Read it from many sides: why were these three words so powerful? Because in one breath they answered the three deepest pains of three kinds of people — front-line soldiers wanted **peace** (stop the fighting), peasants wanted **land** (split the landlords' soil), and city people wanted **bread** (stop the hungry lines). Kerensky could not shout this, because he insisted on staying in the war. Whoever offered to stop the war caught everyone's longing.

### B. The street cry on Women's Day, March 1917
> Recorded cry: "Bread!" — the loudest, most common demand that day.
> Plain reading: the most-shouted word that day was not a grand political slogan. It was "bread."
> Read it: revolutions often do not start with big ideas. They start with "my child has nothing to eat." The anti-Tsar, anti-war slogans were laid on top later. The very first cry came out of hunger.

### C. Nicholas II's abdication statement (March 15 / OS March 2, 1917)
> Original (excerpt): "...we have thought it right to abdicate the Throne of the Russian State and to lay down the Supreme Power."
> Plain reading: we think it is right to step down and give up the highest power.
> Read it: a dynasty that ruled for 300 years ended in a train car, on a signed sheet of paper. There was no dramatic storming of a palace — the front commanders together urged him out. The fall of an absolute throne can be quieter than you'd think.

### D. The Bolshevik logic after the Constituent Assembly was dissolved
> Position (not a direct quote): the Bolsheviks held that the Soviets (worker-and-soldier councils) represented "the real people" better than a one-person-one-vote parliament — so dissolving the Assembly was, to them, justified.
> Plain reading: they said the parliament didn't count; the Soviets did.
> Read it: this is the key tension of the whole lesson. The same phrase, "for the people," can be used to **open** freedom (overthrow the Tsar) and to **close** it (dissolve the Assembly). Which one is "the people"? After January 1918, that question could no longer be debated freely in Russia.

## 6. Consequences (Short / Medium / Long Term)

- **Short term (1917-1922)**: the Romanov dynasty ended; the world's first communist-led state was born; Russia left WWI, then fell into four years of civil war and famine, with millions dead.
- **Medium term (1920s-1991)**: Soviet Russia became the USSR (founded 1922) and one pole of the 20th century. The tension planted in 1917 — that "in the name of the people" could both free and repress — reached its extreme under Stalin (the purges, the Gulag). At the same time, it became, for oppressed people around the world, a model of "rising up."
- **Long term (to today)**: the October Revolution directly catalyzed the **global communist movement** and is one starting point of the entire Cold War. For China, its influence ran especially deep (see §8). And to this day, "was 1917 the liberation of workers or the start of an authoritarian state?" remains a genuinely contested question — which is exactly why this lesson draws no verdict for you.

## 7. AP DBQ Thinking Practice

- **Stakeholder grid**: front-line soldiers / city workers / peasants / liberals (the Provisional Government) / moderate socialists (Kerensky) / Bolsheviks / old nobles and generals (the Whites). What did each want in 1917, and what cards did each hold? Why did "Peace, Land, Bread" catch the first three at once?
- **Continuity & Change**: from Tsarist autocracy (one man holds all) to the Bolshevik state (one party holds all) — what changed (the dynasty gone, landlords' land split, exit from WWI), and what **stayed the same** (still highly centralized, still no room for opposition, the parliament still powerless)? Did the revolution really remove "autocracy," or just swap one kind for another?
- **February vs. October**: two very different revolutions. February was a **broad, spontaneous** toppling of the Tsar (women, soldiers, liberals, socialists together). October was a **single tightly organized party** seizing power at the right moment. A common AP point: do not blur them into "one revolution."
- **Compare the French Revolution**: 1789 France and 1917 Russia both started from a bread crisis, both shouted freedom, and both fell into an inner spiral of violence (the Jacobin Terror / the Russian Civil War). "The revolution devours its own children" held in both — but the endings differed, so the comparison is rich.

## 8. Meanwhile in China (Structural Comparison; the Rule-0 Veto Dimension) — KILLER BRIDGE

In 1917, China was in the **warlord era**. The Qing dynasty had already fallen in 1912 (the Xinhai Revolution), and the Republic of China was founded — but it quickly fragmented. The Beiyang government in the north was the central authority in name only; in reality, regional warlords carved the country up and fought. This was a "shattered" China: the old order had collapsed, and a new order had not yet formed.

**The October Revolution's direct influence on China (this is historical fact; the influence can be verified)**:

- **The May Fourth Movement (1919).** After WWI, the Paris Peace Conference handed Germany's rights in Shandong to Japan. When the news reached China, Beijing students marched on May 4, 1919, and the protest grew into a nationwide patriotic and intellectual movement. After May Fourth, a layer of intellectuals turned seriously toward Marxism — and what pushed them there was the shock of Russia's October Revolution: for the first time, a real, working model of the oppressed "rising up" stood in front of them.
- **The founding of the Chinese Communist Party (1921).** Li Dazhao was among the first Chinese intellectuals to publicly introduce and support the October Revolution (he published "My View on Marxism" in 1919). Among the youth fired up by May Fourth, a group turned decisively to Marxism and Bolshevism. In July 1921, the Chinese Communist Party was founded, just over two years after May Fourth. The famous line — "the salvos of the October Revolution brought us Marxism" — describes exactly this chain of cause and effect.

**Rule-0 note**: to say "the October Revolution shaped modern China" is to **describe** a verifiable chain of historical influence (it genuinely and deeply altered the path of 20th-century China). It is **not** to endorse the revolution's ultimate outcomes, however you judge them. Describing influence ≠ approving results. This bridge is one of the most important China-bridges in the whole course precisely because it ties "Russia 1917" directly to "China's 20th century." For a child in a bilingual Chinese-American home, this is the moment when "foreign history" suddenly becomes the source of your own.

## 9. What Historians Argue About (Historiography)

- **The "from below" reading (social history)**: 1917 was not a plot by a few. It was the real, spontaneous action of millions of workers, soldiers, and peasants — women on strike, soldiers switching sides, peasants seizing land. These "nameless" people drove it.
- **The "coup" reading (from above)**: the October Revolution was, at its core, one tightly organized party seizing power at the right moment; later official accounts overstated the mass base. The dissolution of the Constituent Assembly is this reading's key evidence.
- **The totalitarian-origins reading**: there is a structural line running from 1917 to Stalin — one-party rule, suppression of dissent, state violence — not a later "betrayal," but logic already inside the revolution itself.
- **The revisionist reading**: pushes back on that straight line, arguing the extreme conditions of the civil war, foreign intervention, and economic collapse were what pushed the revolution toward violence; Stalinism was not the inevitable result of 1917.
- **The neutral synthesis**: 1917 was both a real emancipatory drive by millions of ordinary people (the "from below" view holds) AND it did grow a state that shut its parliament and crushed its rivals (the "one-party" view holds). Both are true at once. Do not flatten it into two poles, and do not use one side to cancel the other.

## 10. Common Misconceptions (Myth-Busting)

1. ❌ "The Russian Revolution was one revolution." → ✅ It was **two**: the February Revolution in March (a broad, spontaneous toppling of the Tsar) and the October Revolution in November (the Bolshevik seizure). Very different in nature.
2. ❌ "The October Revolution was a bloody heroic storming of the Winter Palace by tens of thousands of Red Guards." → ✅ The real night was **almost without fighting**. The guard was thin; the attackers walked in through a side gate; casualties were tiny. That "heroic assault" image comes mainly from Eisenstein's 1928 film October (which actually filmed a 1920 re-enactment with thousands of people — and **more people were hurt in the re-enactment than in the real event**). Many famous "iconic scenes" of this revolution were mythologized later. Learn to tell them apart.
3. ❌ "Lenin led the revolution from Petrograd the whole time." → ✅ When the February Revolution broke out, Lenin was still in exile in Switzerland. He returned only in April, and fled again after the July Days. He was not present from the start.
4. ❌ "Right after October, there was an elected people's government." → ✅ The opposite — the elected Constituent Assembly (where the Bolsheviks held only about a quarter) was dissolved after one day in January 1918.
5. ❌ "Once the Bolsheviks took power, the war ended and everyone lived well." → ✅ Leaving the foreign war bought a civil war that lasted four years and killed millions, plus a great famine.
6. ❌ "'Soviet' just means 'the USSR.'" → ✅ A **Soviet** originally meant a "council" — the elected bodies of workers and soldiers. The USSR was a country, founded only in 1922. In 1917 there was no "USSR" yet.
7. ❌ "The Tsar was killed when the palace was stormed." → ✅ The Tsar **abdicated** on a train in March 1917, urged out by his commanders. He and his family were executed in 1918 during the civil war — a separate event.

## 11. Open Questions (No Conclusion Given)

1. The February Revolution was lit by women who only wanted a loaf of bread — and had read no revolutionary theory. Can a revolution that changes the world start from something as concrete as "my child has nothing to eat"? Which moves history more: ideas, or hunger?
2. Kerensky wanted a middle road — freedom, no Tsar, no one-party rule — and ended up hated by both sides and losing everything. In a moment when everyone is pushed to the extremes, does the "moderate in the middle" have any path to survive? If you were him, would you have kept fighting that unwanted war?
3. The same phrase, "for the people," was used to **overthrow** the Tsar (opening freedom) and to **dissolve** the Constituent Assembly (closing it). How can one slogan do two opposite things? How do you judge which time it was really "for the people"?
4. The October Revolution "salvo" directly shaped the May Fourth Movement, the founding of the CCP, and the whole 20th century of China. How can an event in Russia become one of the sources of another country's history? What does that do to how you understand where "your own history" comes from?
5. This revolution was both a real longing for liberation by millions AND the start of a state that would crush dissent. If something begins in liberation and ends in repression — how do you judge it? Or should it not be judged in a single sentence at all?

## Appendix D: Visualization Cues

- A long **bread line**: below-zero wind, women workers, empty baskets — the real start of the revolution.
- A "dual power" split image: on the left, the Provisional Government (liberals in suits); on the right, the Petrograd Soviet (worker and soldier deputies); a crack down the middle.
- "Peace / Land / Bread" matched to three kinds of people (soldier / peasant / city woman).
- An OS/NS calendar band: the same event with two dates (Women's Day March 8 = OS Feb 23; October Revolution Nov 7 = OS Oct 25), marking "13 days apart."
- Myth vs. reality: Eisenstein's dramatic "storming of the Winter Palace" vs. the real "walk in through a side gate, almost no fighting."
- A timeline arrow across borders: Russia's October Revolution 1917 → China's May Fourth 1919 → the founding of the CCP 1921.
