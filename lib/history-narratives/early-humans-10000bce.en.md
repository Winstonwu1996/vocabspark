# Early Humans & the Neolithic Revolution (c. 10,000 BCE)

> **Lock status**: narrative kernel v0 — pending review
>
> **Anchor**: CA HSS Grade 6.1 (opening of the Ancient World strand) / AP World pre-Unit-1
> **Through-line**: the turn from "follow the land" to "make the land circle around us" — and its hidden bill
> **Difficulty**: 3/5 (two time-scales, counter-intuitive causation, but emotionally lighter than war topics)
> **Expected duration**: 35-45 min

---

## 0. Open Your Textbook First (Textbook Anchor)

> This is **the very first lesson** of the whole "Ancient World" strand — **CA HSS Grade 6, Chapter 1**.
>
> Your textbook will tell you:
> - **Modern humans began in Africa** and spread across the world.
> - **The Neolithic Revolution** was the shift from **hunting and gathering** to **farming and settling down**, starting around **10,000 BCE** in the **Fertile Crescent** of the Middle East.
> - **Farming made the first villages, towns, and eventually civilizations possible.**
>
> **What your textbook may NOT make clear** (this is why we need this lesson):
> - **Farming was NOT invented in one place and spread everywhere.** It was invented **independently** in several corners of the earth — Southwest Asia (wheat, barley), **China** (rice on the Yangtze + millet on the Yellow River), Mesoamerica (corn), and others. Nobody copied anyone.
> - **The first farmers were not better off — at first they were worse off.** Ancient skeletons show settled farmers were **shorter**, had **worse teeth**, and caught **more disease** than their hunter-gatherer ancestors. Farming fed *more people*; it did not make *each person* healthier. Those are two different things.
> - **Religion may have come BEFORE farming.** A site called **Göbekli Tepe** (about 9500 BCE) was built by hunter-gatherers who did not yet farm — a thous-plus years *before* agriculture. That flips the old story of how civilization began.
> - **Inequality was born here.** Once grain could be **stored**, whoever controlled the storehouse gained power. The first rich-and-poor gap appears in the very first farming villages.
> - **This lesson is the foundation for your whole year.** Cities, writing, kings, the four ancient civilizations — every piece grows from one source: the **surplus grain** that farming made possible.

## 1. One-Sentence Anchor (Many Situations at Once)

About **12,000 years ago**, on a piece of land in the Middle East curved like a crescent moon (the **Fertile Crescent**), someone did a thing that humans had not seriously done in the previous several hundred thousand years. He did not chase the gazelle. He did not pick the wild grass. He **crouched down, pushed a handful of wild wheat seeds back into the soil — and stayed in place to wait for them to grow.**

That action was almost too small to see. It was also the biggest turn in human history. Before it, **all** humans were **hunter-gatherers** — they followed the seasons and the herds and ate whatever the land gave. After it, over the next few thousand years, a new way of life caught fire — **independently** — in several corners of the planet: wheat and barley in Southwest Asia, **rice and millet in China**, corn in Mesoamerica. Humans began to **settle, farm, herd animals, build houses, and have more and more children.**

But this revolution was squeezed out of several situations pulling against each other:

- **The older hunter-gatherers.** In their eyes, following the herds, eating a wide variety, living free — that was the right way of life, proven over hundreds of thousands of years. Crouching on one patch of ground to nurse a crop looked exhausting and foolish.
- **The first farmers.** They bet everything — they pushed seeds into the soil and had to wait months to learn whether they would starve. They survived. But they became **shorter, more tired, with worse teeth, and caught diseases their ancestors had never known.**
- **The people of the first villages and towns.** When grain could be **stored** for the first time, someone in the village guarded the storehouse, someone stopped working the fields, someone had more than the rest. Humans tasted the sweetness of "settling down" — and, for the first time, the bitterness of **inequality.**

In that same stretch of time, on the other side of the earth in **China**, people by the **Yangtze River** were domesticating a wild grass that grew in water — later called **rice**; and people by the **Yellow River** were domesticating a tough, drought-hardy little grain — later called **millet**. Nobody told anyone. Nobody copied anyone's homework. **Farming was the same idea that humans figured out, separately, in several places at once.**

This lesson is about that turn — from "follow the land" to "make the land circle around you" — and about the bill it sent at the same time: was it paradise, or a new kind of chain?

## 2. The Stage (Geography / Climate / Society / Institutions / Events)

- **Geography**: The human story begins in **Africa** — modern humans (**Homo sapiens**) appeared in Africa about **300,000 years ago** (one of the oldest fossils is from Jebel Irhoud, Morocco). The first scene of the farming revolution is the **Fertile Crescent**: an arc from today's Israel/Palestine, Jordan, and Syria, through southeastern Turkey, down to Iraq and western Iran — shaped like a crescent moon. It had wild wheat and barley, and animals that *could* be domesticated — sheep, goats, cattle, pigs. A ready-made "domestication kit." That luck is a big reason it went first.
- **Climate**: About **11,500 years ago**, the earth ended its last ice age and entered a warmer, more stable era (geologists call it the **Holocene**). The climate became warm, steady, and predictable — and that is the precondition for farming. You have to *believe* "this land will get rain and warmth again next spring" before you dare bet a whole year's life on a seed. The end of the ice age (fewer big game animals, rising population pressure) may also have *pushed* people to try growing their own food.
- **Society**: Before farming, humans moved in small **bands** of a few dozen people, with no fixed home, few possessions, shallow division of labor, and relative equality (you can't carry much, and you can't hoard much grain). After farming, people could **settle** and **store grain**, and villages grew from dozens to hundreds and thousands. **Jericho** held two to three thousand people by about **7000 BCE**, and **Çatalhöyük** gathered several thousand from about **7500 BCE** onward — among the earliest large towns we know. More people plus stored grain meant society began to stack into layers.
- **Institutions**: Farming quietly rewrote three of humanity's base rules. ① **Land became property** — the field you nursed all year, the flock you raised, was "yours"; the idea of private property took root. ② **Grain could be stored → it could be gathered, handed out, and taxed** — whoever controlled the granary held power, and the first headmen / priests / managers sprouted from there. ③ **Division of labor** — with surplus grain, not everyone had to farm; some could specialize in pottery, building, ritual, or war. Every part of "civilization" (cities, writing, kings) grows from this one source: **surplus grain.**
- **Events**: One site **flips the order** of the whole story — **Göbekli Tepe** in southeastern Turkey, built around **9500 BCE** by hunter-gatherers **who did not yet farm.** It is one of the oldest known monumental stone structures on earth: giant T-shaped stone pillars carved with wild animals. It predates agriculture by at least a thousand years. The implication: it may **not** be "first farming, then surplus, then the power to build temples." It may be the reverse — **people came together regularly to hold rituals and raise big monuments, and *that* pushed them to grow more grain nearby.** Belief and gathering may be a *cause* of farming, not only a result.

## 3. The Main Characters

> Three perspectives (actor / lonely witness / receiving-end), including at least one powerless party. Note: this 10,000-year span left **no individual names** — every "character" is a clearly-labeled, factually realistic composite bridging figure standing for a whole type of person. No names are invented.

### The older hunter-gatherer (actor / the side that resisted) — an elder in a roaming band on the edge of the Fertile Crescent

A bridging character, fully realistic in role — this span left no names, so he has none; he stands for a whole generation of "the last hunter-gatherers before farming." What is certain is this life: he belongs to a band of a few dozen people, with no fixed home. He knows a staggering amount — which slope sprouts wild wheat first in spring, when the gazelles pass through which gap in the hills, which root is food and which is poison, how to read clouds, wind, and animal tracks. He may "work" only a few hours a day; the rest he spends walking, telling stories, watching the children. He is not short, his teeth are good, and he barely knows what an epidemic is — because people are few and always moving, so germs cannot pile up.

To him, the young people who have started camping on one patch of ground, bending all day to nurse a crop, are **asking for misery.** Bet your life on one small field, one crop — what if the rain fails? What if one swarm of pests wipes it out? Follow the land, eat a wide variety, live steadily — that is the way proven over hundreds of thousands of years. He may die believing that "settle down and farm" was a foolish gamble.

Why this perspective matters: it forces students to realize that **farming was not "humans finally getting smart." It was a choice full of costs that people at the time had every reason to refuse.** Standing in his place, you ask the sharpest question of all: if farming made people more tired, shorter, and sicker, what were they even after?

### The Göbekli Tepe stone-carver (lonely witness / standing on the hinge) — a worker carving giant stones before anyone farms

A bridging character, but the situation is realistic and hung on a **real site** (Göbekli Tepe, about 9500 BCE, today's southeastern Turkey). He is still a hunter-gatherer — no farm fields, no metal tools, no domestic animals — yet he and hundreds of others are using stone tools to carve T-shaped pillars weighing several tons, cutting reliefs of foxes, wild boars, vipers, and vultures into them, and standing them in ring after ring. He probably spends most of his life hunting, and only at certain times of the year comes with distant bands to this hilltop to haul stone, hold rituals, and feast together.

He stands on the most delicate hinge in human history: **to gather here again and again for rituals this large, you need enough food to feed this many people — and the nearest, steadiest food source is to grow a bit more of the wild wheat close by.** He may have no idea that the vulture-carved stone in his hands is quietly pushing humanity toward settlement and farming.

Why this perspective matters: it flips the old script of "how civilization develops." The old script says "first eat your fill, then you have the leisure for belief." Göbekli Tepe says it may have been the *need to gather and to believe* that forced people to grow more grain. It lets students see that the cause-and-effect of history often runs against intuition.

### The girl in the first settled village (receiving-end · DEFAULT) — a farm girl in an early Fertile Crescent village

A bridging character, realistic in role (this is the DEFAULT perspective, and the type of person who **bore the heaviest cost of this revolution while having the least say**). She has no name — because history left these people no name at all, and that, exactly, is part of the problem. What is certain is her situation: she was born in an early farming village (a place like Jericho or Çatalhöyük), where mud-brick houses press wall to wall and storage jars hold grain inside. She has worked since she can remember — grinding wheat (the stone grinder reshapes her knees and wrists; you can see this on real excavated skeletons), minding younger siblings, tending the sheep in the pen.

She eats far more **monotonously** than her grandmother's generation — mostly a porridge of boiled wheat. She is **shorter** than her hunter-gatherer ancestors, with worse teeth (the starchy porridge wrecks them), and she falls ill more easily — people packed together, living close to animals, drinking from the same water, gave infectious disease its first breeding ground. She has more children than people did in the roaming days (settling shortens the gap between births), but more of them die young. She may never travel more than a few miles in her whole life.

She also sees something new: some households' granaries are fuller, some people have stopped working the fields, some people speak and others must listen. **The word "inequality" gets a body, for the first time, in her generation's village.**

This perspective is the heart of the lesson. The Neolithic Revolution is often told as "humanity's great leap forward" — but **the bill for that "progress" was paid mostly by ordinary settled farmers like her, and especially by women, in a more tired body, worse health, and less freedom.** Standing in her place, you do not simply cheer for "civilization." You see its sweetness and its cost at the same time.

## 4. The Story (Plot Beats)

1. **Out of Africa (c. 300,000 years ago → c. 60,000-70,000 years ago).** Modern humans (Homo sapiens) appeared in Africa about 300,000 years ago. After living there for over two hundred thousand years, a group walked out of Africa around 60,000-70,000 years ago, spread along the southern coast of Asia, reached Southeast Asia and Oceania by about 50,000 years ago, entered Europe about 40,000 years ago, and later crossed into the Americas. **By the time farming appeared, Homo sapiens had left footprints on every continent except Antarctica — and the entire way, they were hunter-gatherers.**
2. **The ice age ends; the stage is set (c. 11,500 years ago).** The last ice age ended and the earth entered the warm, stable Holocene. The climate became predictable — the precondition for daring to bet your life on a seed.
3. **Religion before farming? Göbekli Tepe (c. 9500 BCE).** While no one in Southwest Asia yet farmed, hunter-gatherers were already raising giant stone rings for rituals in southeastern Turkey. A counter-intuitive possibility: the need to "gather and do something big together" pushed people toward growing more grain nearby.
4. **The first seeds (c. 10,000-8000 BCE, Fertile Crescent).** People began deliberately sowing, harvesting, and selecting the best wild wheat (**einkorn** and **emmer**), barley, lentils, and peas — **domesticating plants.** They also domesticated sheep, goats, cattle, and pigs — **domesticating animals.** This was not a single day's invention; it was a slow change spanning over a thousand years.
5. **Settlement and the first towns (c. 9000-7000 BCE).** If you can grow grain, you don't have to move. Jericho held two to three thousand people, ringed by a stone wall, by about 7000 BCE; Çatalhöyük gathered several thousand from about 7500 BCE, its houses packed so tightly you entered through the roof. Humans "settled down" for the first time.
6. **Farming catches fire independently (multiple centers).** The key fact: farming **did not spread from one place in the Fertile Crescent to the whole world.** It was invented **independently** in several corners of the earth — Southwest Asia (wheat, barley), East Asia / China (rice on the Yangtze + millet on the Yellow River, from about 8000 BCE; see §8), Mesoamerica (corn), and also Africa, New Guinea, and South America. Nobody copied anyone.
7. **Surplus grain → division of labor → the beginning of everything.** Once grain could be stored and a surplus existed, not everyone had to farm. Some specialized in pottery, building, priesthood, war, or managing the storehouse. Cities, writing, kings, armies — every part of later "civilization" grows from this one source: surplus grain. This is why this lesson is the foundation of the whole Ancient World strand.
8. **The bill arrives (the costs).** Excavated skeletons show settled farmers were **shorter** than their hunter-gatherer ancestors (one study of skeletons from Greece and Turkey estimates men's average height fell from about 5'10" to about 5'6"), with worse teeth, a more monotonous diet, and more infectious disease (people packed together + living with animals + shared water). **Inequality** appears for the first time: whoever controls the granary holds power, and skeletons show "elites" ate better and grew taller. Farming fed more people — but each person's life was not necessarily better.
9. **No way back (the trap).** Once a village fed hundreds or thousands of people through farming, it could never return to hunting and gathering — that much land could not feed that many people by foraging, and that many people could not scatter back into small bands. Humanity was **locked** by its own success onto the farming road. From here on, it was a staircase up into civilization — and a staircase that could never be climbed back down.

**An irony to keep:** for millions of years, humans lived free, healthy, and equal by "following the land." Yet the very revolution that let us build cities, write, and raise kingdoms cost its first participants a more tired body, worse teeth, and the first rich-poor gap. **The thing that made us "great" and the thing that made those first farmers "suffer" were the same thing.**

## 5. Reading the Documents (Primary Sources)

> Reminder: this 10,000-year span had **no writing** — writing did not appear until thousands of years *after* farming (Mesopotamia, about 3200 BCE). So this lesson has no "written sources." Our "documents" are **physical evidence**: stones, bones, seeds, sites. Reading these "books without words" is the core skill of prehistory.

### A. The T-shaped pillars of Göbekli Tepe (c. 9500 BCE, physical evidence)
> What it is: T-shaped limestone pillars weighing several tons, carved with foxes, wild boars, snakes, and vultures, standing in ring after ring — built by hunter-gatherers who **did not yet farm.**
> Grade 6-7 reading: in an age before anyone farmed, a group of "wild" people moved stones weighing several tons, carved them with animals, and stood them up into a temple — and that fact alone overturns the old saying that "people must eat their fill before they have the leisure for culture."
> Reading: it forces us to flip the cause and effect — **maybe the spiritual need to "gather and do something big together" came first, and *that* pushed people toward settlement and farming.** One vulture-carved stone can rewrite the whole script of "how civilization began."

### B. The "before-and-after skeletons" of the same population (physical evidence, the hardest evidence of the cost)
> What it is: archaeologists place bones from the hunter-gatherer era and bones from the later farming era of the **same region** side by side.
> Grade 6-7 reading: the farming-era bones are **shorter**, with more defects from malnutrition on the teeth, and more marks of iron-deficiency anemia and infection. Bones do not lie.
> Reading: this is the strongest counter-evidence to the gut feeling that "farming = progress." It does not say farming was bad — it says **"feeding more people" and "making each person healthier" are two different things.** Farming did the first, while for a long time sacrificing the second. AP thinking wants exactly this: "the same thing, two opposite truths."

### C. A domesticated grain of wheat vs. a wild grain of wheat (physical evidence)
> What it is: when wild wheat ripens, the seeds "shatter" off on their own (so the plant can re-seed itself); after humans repeatedly selected and planted it, a wheat appeared that **stays put on the stalk even when ripe, waiting to be harvested** — that is the mark of "domestication" on a single seed.
> Grade 6-7 reading: humans did not "invent" wheat. Year after year they kept only the most "obedient, easiest to harvest" seeds to plant, and over centuries they reshaped a wild grass into a crop.
> Reading: domestication was a long negotiation in which **humans and plants reshaped each other** — we reshaped wheat, and wheat reshaped us from roaming hunters into farmers who could no longer live without it. So who domesticated whom? A question worth chewing on.

### D. The streetless houses of Çatalhöyük (physical evidence)
> What it is: in the town of Çatalhöyük, home to several thousand people, the houses press wall to wall with **no streets** — people climbed in and out through **holes in the roof**, ancestors' bones were buried inside, and murals covered the walls.
> Grade 6-7 reading: the earliest "big towns" looked nothing like the cities we imagine — no roads, no plazas, no palace; to get home you climbed the roof.
> Reading: it reminds us not to imagine the earliest settlements as today's "cities." This was a brand-new way of "crowding together to live" that humans were figuring out for the very first time — even "how to live in a house" was still being trial-and-errored.

## 6. Consequences (Short / Medium / Long Term)

- **Short term (the first few thousand years after farming):** Humans **settled down** for the first time, could **store grain** for the first time, populations began to grow, and the first villages and towns appeared (Jericho, Çatalhöyük). At the same time, the costs of settling appeared for the first time: harder labor, a more monotonous diet, shorter stature, new infectious diseases, and the earliest rich-poor gap.
- **Medium term (c. 4000-3000 BCE, toward civilization):** Along several great rivers (the Tigris-Euphrates, the Nile, the Indus, the Yellow and Yangtze), surplus grain piled higher and higher, supporting priests, craftsmen, soldiers, and managers who did not farm, and growing **cities, writing, temples, kings, armies, and taxes** — the "four ancient civilizations" you study later in Grade 6. **This lesson is the foundation for that whole year.** Without this one link, the Neolithic Revolution, nothing later — "why are there kings and pyramids?" — can be explained.
- **Long term (to today):** Almost every bite you eat today — rice, bread, noodles, meat, milk — comes from the handful of animals and plants this 10,000-year-old revolution domesticated. Cities, states, social classes, private property, epidemics — nearly every basic structure of the modern world can be traced back to the moment "someone first pushed a seed back into the soil." **It is the deepest and most irreversible turn in human history — bar none.**

## 7. AP DBQ Thinking Practice

- **Continuity & change (the trump card of this lesson):** humans were hunter-gatherers for millions of years (continuity), then in about ten thousand years completely rewrote their relationship to food, land, and each other (change). What changed (settlement, stored grain, private property, division of labor, inequality, cities)? What stayed the same or got worse (harder labor, worse health)? This is the best topic for practicing "change and continuity at the largest possible scale."
- **Causation (counter-intuitive training):** the traditional chain is "farming → surplus → leisure → civilization (including religion)." But Göbekli Tepe reverses the arrow — maybe it was "the need for religion / gathering → forced people to settle and grow more grain → farming." Having students argue *both* opposite cause-and-effect chains from the same evidence is exactly the higher-order thinking AP wants.
- **Multi-perspective unpacking of "progress" (the core DBQ angle):** was the Neolithic Revolution "progress"? Argue it in layers with evidence — **for the species** (population boom, eventually civilization) it was a huge success; **for the ordinary individual farmer** (shorter, sicker, more tired, more unequal) it was very likely a step backward; **for women** (grinding-injury, denser childbearing, falling status) the cost was especially heavy. What AP graders reward is exactly the argument that "refuses a 0/1 answer and can hold several opposite truths at once."
- **Cross-civilization comparison · multiple origins (connects to §8):** farming appeared **independently** in Southwest Asia, China, and Mesoamerica. Several civilizations with no contact gave the same answer to the same problem of "inventing agriculture" — does that mean farming was "inevitable under certain conditions," or "coincidence"? Comparing Southwest Asian wheat with Chinese rice/millet is an excellent cross-civilization compare-and-contrast prompt.

## 8. China in the Same Era (Structural Comparison · Rule 0 veto dimension)

**This section is the most important part of this course for Chinese-heritage families. It must be concrete, not a throwaway line.**

World-history teaching has one recurring error: telling the story as if farming was "invented in the Fertile Crescent of Southwest Asia and then spread to the whole world." **That is wrong.** Farming was invented **independently** in several corners of the earth — and **China is one of the most important independent centers of origin.**

Specifically in China (using real Chinese terms — this section's special privilege):

- **The Yellow River basin: the home of millet.** In the dry Yellow River basin of northern China, from about **8000 BCE**, people independently domesticated **foxtail millet** (from a wild grass) and **broomcorn millet**. This was a whole farming system suited to the dry north — not Southwest Asian wheat, but a drought-hardy staple Chinese people figured out themselves. Later cultures (Yangshao, Longshan) were built on millet farming.
- **The Yangtze River basin: the home of rice.** In the wet middle and lower Yangtze of southern China, from about **10,000 years ago**, people independently domesticated **rice** (*Oryza sativa*) — reshaping a wild grass that grew in water into a staple that would later feed half of Asia. This was a **second, parallel, independent** Chinese center, separate from the dry millet farming of the Yellow River.
- **The fact of "two centers" is itself remarkable:** within China alone there were **two independent** centers of agricultural origin — the Yellow River (millet) and the Yangtze (rice). They and the wheat-barley center of Southwest Asia arose at close-in times, with no contact, each succeeding on its own.

Why this section matters for Chinese-heritage children (the teaching takeaway):
1. **It places the child's ancestral civilization accurately on the opening map of world history.** American textbooks say "agriculture has multiple independent origins," and China is one of the leads — a child can say with full confidence: "My ancestors invented farming themselves; they did not learn it from anyone."
2. **It corrects an error in both directions:** it corrects the hint in some Western narratives that "civilization spread one-way out of Southwest Asia," AND it corrects an over-nationalist "China was first at everything" — **the truth is "multiple centers, each independent, close in time,"** which is more accurate, and more powerful, than any single-pole story.
3. **It makes "the same era" feel real:** while people in the Fertile Crescent were growing wheat and raising Göbekli Tepe, people by the Yangtze were growing rice and people by the Yellow River were growing millet — **several ends of the earth walked onto the farming road in roughly the same stretch of time.** Humanity did not have one place "wake up first" and the rest catch up later — in similar situations, people made similar choices, separately.

Rule 0: we cannot say Southwest Asia was "first, therefore best," nor that China "proves the Chinese were earliest and strongest." The truth is more equal — and more astonishing: **farming was the same idea our species came up with, in several places, facing similar challenges.** This section only sets the people at several ends of the earth, in the same stretch of time, side by side.

## 9. What Historians Argue About (Historiography)

- **The "farming is progress" school (traditional progress view):** for a long time the mainstream narrative treated the Neolithic Revolution as humanity's great step "toward civilization" — farming fed more people, freed up division of labor, and eventually birthed cities and writing. This was the basic frame when **V. Gordon Childe** coined the term "Neolithic Revolution" in the 1930s (and he was the one who first stressed how "revolutionary" it was).
- **The "farming was the worst mistake" school (revisionist view):** **Jared Diamond**'s famous 1987 essay flatly called farming "the worst mistake in the history of the human race" — using skeletal evidence to argue that farming brought worse health, malnutrition, and infectious disease, along with "the gross social and sexual inequality, the disease and despotism" of later life. This school turns the arrow completely away from "progress."
- **The "domestication ran both ways" school:** other scholars stress that rather than "humans domesticated crops," humans and crops (and humans and livestock) **domesticated and reshaped each other** — wheat made humans settle down to nurse it; in a sense, wheat "domesticated" humanity.
- **The "not a revolution, but a slow change" school:** still other scholars object to the word "revolution" — farming was not a one-night invention but a **slow process** spanning thousands of years, full of trial, error, and reversal; "revolution" overstates its suddenness.
- **A neutral synthesis:** the Neolithic Revolution was both the great turn that made all later civilization possible, AND the turn that cost ordinary farmers heavy health and equality losses for the first few thousand years. Both are true at once — it was a success **for the species** and often a step backward **for the individual** of the time. Do not make it either/or.

## 10. Common Misconceptions (Myth-Busting)

1. ❌ "Farming was invented in one place in Southwest Asia and spread to the whole world." → ✅ Farming was invented **independently** in several places — Southwest Asia, China (rice + millet), Mesoamerica (corn), and more — with no contact. This is the single most important point of the lesson.
2. ❌ "Humans lived outside Africa from the start." → ✅ Modern humans originated in **Africa** about 300,000 years ago and only walked out of Africa about 60,000-70,000 years ago to spread across the world — as hunter-gatherers the whole way.
3. ❌ "Once farming came, people were immediately better off." → ✅ Excavated skeletons show the first settled farmers were **shorter, with worse teeth and more infectious disease** than their hunter-gatherer ancestors. Farming fed more people without making each person healthier — two different things.
4. ❌ "Hunter-gatherers were poor and miserable, always anxious about food." → ✅ The evidence runs the other way — they ate a more varied, more complete diet, may have "worked" fewer hours a day, and had almost no infectious disease or rich-poor gap. "Primitive = wretched" is a prejudice.
5. ❌ "Cities and civilization came first, then religion and big monuments." → ✅ Göbekli Tepe (about 9500 BCE) was a giant stone monument built by hunter-gatherers who **did not yet farm**, over a thousand years *before* agriculture — religion/gathering may have *driven* farming, not resulted from it.
6. ❌ "Humans cleverly 'invented' crops." → ✅ More accurately, humans and plants **domesticated each other** — humans kept only the most "obedient" seeds year after year, taking centuries to reshape a wild grass into a crop, while the crop reshaped humans into settlers who could not live without it.
7. ❌ "The Neolithic Revolution was a sudden invention in one year or one generation." → ✅ It was a slow change spanning **thousands of years**, full of trial and error; "revolution" means "fast" relative to millions of years, not literally overnight.
8. ❌ "Inequality and class were created later by kings." → ✅ The root of inequality was laid in the earliest farming villages (whoever controlled the granary held power) — it appeared almost together with settlement and stored grain.

## 11. Thinking Questions (Open-Ended, No Conclusion)

1. If farming made the first farmers shorter, sicker, and more tired, why didn't they quit and go back to hunting and gathering? Was it that they couldn't go back — or that something else (more children? steadier grain? the safety of gathering together?) was worth staying for?
2. Göbekli Tepe suggests that the urge to "gather and do something big together" may have driven people to settle *before* the need to fill their stomachs did. In your own life, does the need come first and then the conditions, or do the conditions come first and then the need? Does the cause-and-effect of history really always follow intuition?
3. Farming was a huge success for the human **species** (we went from a few million to billions), yet often a step backward for the ordinary **individual farmer** (sicker, more tired, more unequal) of the time. When "good for the group" clashes with "good for the individual," which ruler should we use to judge "progress"?
4. Farming was invented **independently** in Southwest Asia, China, and Mesoamerica, with no copying. Does that mean farming was "bound to happen sooner or later under those conditions," or "several astonishing coincidences"? With a different climate and a different set of domesticable plants and animals, would humans still have walked onto the farming road?
5. This time, you mostly heard one voice — a farm girl, the person who paid the most for "civilization" and had the least name. How would the older hunter who resisted tell this revolution? How would the Göbekli Tepe carver see it? Whose voice have you not heard yet?

## Appendix A: Key Timeline

> Note the two clocks: the top half uses "years ago (ka)", the bottom half uses "BCE."

- c. 300,000 years ago: modern humans (Homo sapiens) appear in **Africa** (fossil evidence such as Jebel Irhoud, Morocco).
- c. 60,000-70,000 years ago: Homo sapiens **walk out of Africa** and begin spreading globally (as hunter-gatherers throughout).
- c. 50,000 years ago: reach Southeast Asia and Oceania.
- c. 40,000 years ago: enter Europe.
- c. 11,500 years ago: the last ice age ends; the warm, stable **Holocene** begins — the climate precondition for farming is in place.
- c. 9500 BCE: **Göbekli Tepe** is built by hunter-gatherers (today's southeastern Turkey), predating agriculture.
- c. 10,000-8000 BCE: the **Fertile Crescent** begins domesticating wheat (einkorn/emmer), barley, lentils, and peas, plus sheep, goats, cattle, and pigs.
- from c. 8000 BCE: **China's Yellow River basin** independently domesticates millet; the **Yangtze basin** independently domesticates rice from about 10,000 years ago.
- c. 8000-7000 BCE: **Mesoamerica** independently domesticates the ancestor of corn (plus squash and beans).
- from c. 7500 BCE: **Çatalhöyük** gathers several thousand people (today's central Turkey).
- c. 7000 BCE: **Jericho** holds two to three thousand people, ringed by a stone wall.
- c. 3200 BCE: the earliest writing appears in Mesopotamia — **the end of prehistory, the start of recorded history** (beyond this course's scope; the start of the next chapter).

## Appendix B: Core Vocabulary

- **Homo sapiens** — modern humans, originated in Africa about 300,000 years ago
- **Out of Africa** — the spread of Homo sapiens out of Africa about 60,000-70,000 years ago
- **hunter-gatherer** — the pre-farming way of life: roaming, gathering, hunting
- **Neolithic Revolution** — the shift from hunting-gathering to settled farming (named by V. Gordon Childe)
- **domestication** — reshaping wild plants/animals into crops/livestock over generations
- **Fertile Crescent** — the crescent-shaped first farming region of Southwest Asia
- **Holocene** — the warm, stable geological era that began about 11,500 years ago
- **Göbekli Tepe** — a giant stone monument built by hunter-gatherers c. 9500 BCE (Turkey)
- **Çatalhöyük** — an early large town from c. 7500 BCE (Turkey)
- **Jericho** — one of the oldest settlements (two to three thousand people by c. 7000 BCE)
- **einkorn / emmer** — the earliest domesticated wheats of Southwest Asia
- **millet / rice** — staples independently domesticated in China's Yellow River / Yangtze basins
- **surplus** — farm output beyond immediate need; the source of division of labor and civilization
- **sedentism** — settling in one place rather than roaming
- **multiple origins** — the fact that farming was independently invented in several places on earth
