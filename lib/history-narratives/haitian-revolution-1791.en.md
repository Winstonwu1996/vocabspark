# The Haitian Revolution · 1791-1804

> **Lock status**: narrative kernel v0 — pending review
>
> **AP anchor**: AP World History Modern, Unit 5.2-5.3 (Atlantic Revolutions)
> **Through-line**: the only revolution started and won by the enslaved themselves
> **Difficulty**: 4/5 (heavy emotional weight + many factions + real atrocities)
> **Expected duration**: 35-45 min

## 0. Open Your Textbook First (Textbook Anchor)

> This belongs to **AP World History Modern, Unit 5 (1750-1900)** — the **Atlantic Revolutions** chain.
>
> Your textbook will tell you:
> - **The Haitian Revolution started in 1791** in a French sugar colony called **Saint-Domingue** (san doh-MANG).
> - **It was part of the "Age of Revolutions":** America 1776 → France 1789 → Haiti 1791 → Latin America 1810s.
> - **Haiti became independent in 1804.**
>
> **What your textbook may NOT make clear** (this is why we need this lesson):
> - **Haiti is the ONLY revolution started and won by enslaved people themselves** — and the only nation in history founded by formerly enslaved people. They were not freed by outsiders. They lit the fire, fought the war, and built the state with their own hands.
> - **It was the richest colony in the world.** Saint-Domingue's sugar and coffee made France enormous wealth — built on about **500,000 enslaved people**, roughly **90% of the island's population.**
> - **The same sentence that started it was not meant for them.** France's 1789 Declaration said "men are born free and equal." French writers did not mean the enslaved Africans in the Caribbean. The enslaved picked up that sentence and forced it to come true.
> - **The AP DBQ angle is "black agency."** Graders reward the argument that the enslaved were **drivers of history, not people waiting to be rescued.**
> - **It was also brutally violent on many sides** — including the 1804 killing of the remaining French (about 3,000-7,000 people). This lesson holds both the greatness and the cost. It does not pick a final verdict for you.

## 1. One-Sentence Anchor (Many Factions at Once)

On a night in **August 1791**, on the northern plain of a French sugar colony called **Saint-Domingue** (today's Haiti), hundreds of thousands of enslaved people lit their torches at the same time. This is the **only large uprising in history that the enslaved won and then turned into their own nation.** But the word "won" was squeezed out from four groups who hated each other:

- **grands blancs** (grahn blahn, "big whites") — rich French plantation owners. They got rich from the world's most profitable sugar, and kept it going with the world's most brutal slavery.
- **petits blancs** (puh-TEE blahn, "small whites") — white overseers, shopkeepers, sailors. Poorer than the owners, but holding tightly to one thing: "I am white."
- **gens de couleur libres** (zhahn duh koo-LUR, "free people of color") — people of mixed African and European ancestry, free under the law. Some owned plantations and enslaved people themselves. Stuck in the middle, accepted by neither side.
- **the enslaved majority** — about **500,000 people**, roughly **90% of the island.** Their sugar fed France's wealth. Their death rate was so high that plantations bought new people from Africa every year just to replace them.

Far away, on the other side of the earth, the same year was the **56th year of the Qianlong** (chyen-LONG) reign of China's Qing dynasty — an empire at its peak. The Atlantic world was being overturned by one "rights of man" revolution after another. The great eastern empire barely felt the wave. Inside the plantation houses, people counted money and kept order. Outside, the sound of a **lambi** (LAHM-bee — a conch shell horn) was moving across the dark cane fields. The person blowing it was about to become a maker of history.

## 2. The Stage (Geography / Climate / Society / Institutions / Events)

- **Geography**: Saint-Domingue was the western third of **Hispaniola** (his-pan-YOH-la) island in the Caribbean — today Haiti plus the Dominican Republic. France took it in 1697. The **Plaine du Nord** (the Northern Plain) and the port city of **Le Cap-Français** (luh KAP, "Le Cap") were the heart of sugar and coffee. It was the richest colony on earth.
- **Climate**: Tropical — hot, wet, and hit by hurricanes. Endless cane fields. Cutting cane and boiling sugar went on day and night beside huge boiling pots. The land also had a hidden killer for Europeans: **yellow fever** (a deadly tropical disease). People born on the island and people from Africa had some resistance. New European soldiers did not. This biology decided how the war ended.
- **Society**: Around 1789 the island held about **520,000 people**. About **500,000 were enslaved** (around 90%). Whites numbered about 30,000-40,000 (split into grands blancs and petits blancs). The gens de couleur libres numbered about 28,000-30,000. The death rate among the enslaved was so high that plantations kept buying new people from Africa. This means many enslaved people were **born in Africa**, remembered freedom, and carried African languages and beliefs (which later blended into **Vodou**).
- **Institutions**: The **Code Noir** (kohd NWAHR, "Black Code," issued by King Louis XIV in 1685) was the legal frame of slavery. It gave owners almost total power over the enslaved, while pretending to require food and clothing (often ignored). It turned people into property on a ledger. Meanwhile, France's own revolution erupted in 1789 with the cry "men are born free and equal." That sentence crossed the ocean and set everyone off: whites wanted self-rule, free people of color wanted equal citizenship, and the enslaved wanted freedom itself.
- **Events**: On the night of **August 14, 1791**, at a place called **Bois Caïman** (bwah kah-ee-MAHN, "Caiman Woods"), a Vodou ceremony led by the priest **Dutty Boukman** and the priestess **Cécile Fatiman** sealed a pact to rise up. On the night of **August 21-22**, the Northern Plain burst into flame at once. That night is seen as the start of the revolution. From that night to independence on January 1, 1804, the war lasted 13 years.

## 3. The Main Characters

> Three perspectives (actor/perpetrator, lonely mediator, receiving-end), including at least one powerless party.

### The grand blanc planter (actor / perpetrator) — a French sugar owner on the Northern Plain

This is a bridging character, but the role is fully real. He is the French owner of a sugar plantation near Le Cap, on the Northern Plain — a **grand blanc** (a "big white"). He has no name, because he stands for a whole class. What is certain is how this class lived: in a breezy two-story wooden house, drinking wine shipped from Bordeaux, with a ledger listing the names and prices of more than two hundred enslaved people. His wealth rested on a math so exact it was cruel. Sugar prices were high. Labor could be bought. The enslaved cut cane until they died, and new people came off the African ships to replace them. He was not specially evil as a person. The whole **Code Noir** system made "using people up as property" into normal business.

When France cried "men are born free and equal" in 1789, he and his class wanted **self-rule** — less control from Paris, their own colonial rules — but never freedom for the enslaved (that would mean burning his own wealth). To him, the free people of color asking for equal citizenship was "insolent," and the enslaved wanting freedom was "unthinkable."

On that night in August 1791, the first thing he heard was not a war cry. It was a sound he could not explain — a horn (a **lambi** conch) cutting across the dark cane fields. Hours later, he watched his whole world catch fire: the sugar mill, the cane, the wooden house, all turned to ash in one night. This perspective lets you stand inside a perpetrator and watch an order he thought was natural collapse in front of him. He may have died never understanding why "property" stood up.

### Toussaint Louverture (lonely mediator) — 1743-1803 · from enslaved to head of a nation, dead in a French mountain prison

A real historical person. He was born around May 20, 1743, on the **Bréda** plantation in the north. He was not ordinary field labor. Because he was good with horses and animals, he was made a coachman and a steward. He could read. He was **freed in the 1770s** and became a **free man**. For a time he even rented land and owned a few enslaved people himself (this is his complicated side — we do not hide it). So when the uprising broke out in 1791, he was already in his late forties, a free man — and he chose to join this revolution of the enslaved.

His real genius was on the battlefield and at the bargaining table. For more than a decade, three empires — France, Spain, and Britain — fought over this island. He played all three against each other: he helped whoever recognized freedom, and fought whoever wanted to bring slavery back. After France abolished slavery across its empire in **February 1794**, he switched to fight for France and conquered the whole island. In **1801** he issued a **constitution**: slavery abolished forever in Saint-Domingue, with himself as governor for life. On paper it still belonged to France. In reality he was the head of a nation. He wanted a new order — multiracial, all groups living together, rebuilt through labor.

But he misjudged one man: Napoleon. In **1802**, Napoleon sent his brother-in-law **Leclerc** with more than 20,000 troops. The true mission was to bring slavery back. Toussaint resisted, then was tricked and arrested. In June 1802 he was put on a ship called *Le Héros* and shipped to France, locked in the cold Alpine fortress of **Fort de Joux** (for duh ZHOO). On **April 7, 1803**, he died sick in that cell, before independence. This perspective puts you in the middle of everyone. He was never fully "one of us" to any side. He tried to grab a future where everyone could live, using talks and a constitution — and was betrayed by the empire he trusted most. This is the tragedy of a mediator.

### The young field worker (receiving-end · DEFAULT) — an enslaved boy on a Northern Plain sugar plantation

A bridging character, with a fully real role (this is the DEFAULT perspective). He has no exact name — because history left **no names** for these people. And that, exactly, is part of the problem. What is certain is his situation: an enslaved boy on a sugar plantation on the Northern Plain. Every day before dawn he went to the fields. His cane knife (**machete**) was sharpened bright. His hands were covered in small cuts from the cane leaves. He may have been born in Africa, remembering the day he was sold onto a ship — or born on the island, never knowing what freedom looked like. He lived not far from the Bois Caïman woods.

On that night, August 14, 1791, deep in the woods there was a ceremony — drums, blood, an oath. A few days later, the signal came: someone blew the **lambi** (the conch shell horn), and the sound passed from plantation to plantation across the dark plain. He picked up a torch. In that moment, the "property" on the ledger did a thing that property is not supposed to do — he made a choice.

This perspective is the heart of the revolution. The AP World DBQ keeps testing **black agency** — the enslaved were not objects waiting to be rescued. They were the ones who lit it, fought it, and founded the nation. This perspective lets you stand in the lowest, most nameless position and turn yourself into a maker of history with your own hands. It also makes you face the hardest part: freedom was bought with real violence — and once violence starts, it does not fall on the oppressor alone.

## 4. The Story (Plot Beats)

1. **The sugar-and-blood machine (1697-1791).** France squeezed the largest colonial fortune in the world from Saint-Domingue. The cost: about 500,000 enslaved people working under an extreme death rate, replaced by constant new arrivals from Africa. The **Code Noir** (1685) was its legal shell.
2. **The echo of the 1789 French Revolution.** France cried "men are born free and equal." In the colony, that sentence lit every group's opposing demand: grands blancs wanted self-rule, gens de couleur wanted equal citizenship, the enslaved wanted freedom. In 1790, the free-colored leader **Vincent Ogé** rose up and was executed. Tensions exploded.
3. **August 14, 1791 — the Bois Caïman ceremony.** In a forest in the north, **Boukman** and the priestess **Cécile Fatiman** led a Vodou ceremony and sealed a pact to rise. This is seen as the spiritual start of the revolution.
4. **Night of August 21-22, 1791 — the Northern Plain uprising.** Thousands, then tens of thousands of enslaved people took up the **machete** and the torch. They burned cane fields, sugar mills, and the owners' houses. Within days the whole Northern Plain was on fire.
5. **The death of Boukman (November 1791).** The early leader Boukman was killed by French forces, and his head displayed in public — the French wanted to break his legend of being "unkillable." But the fire was already lit. Killing one leader could not stop it.
6. **Three empires fight (1793-1798).** France, Spain, and Britain all wanted the island. Toussaint moved between all three and slowly rose into the strongest military force.
7. **February 1794 — France abolishes slavery.** The French National Convention abolished slavery across the empire (February 4, 1794). Toussaint switched to the French side and fought for "freedom."
8. **The 1801 Toussaint constitution.** He united the whole island and issued a constitution: slavery abolished forever, himself governor for life, still nominally part of France. A man born enslaved became, in practice, the head of a nation.
9. **1802 — Napoleon's expedition.** Napoleon sent his brother-in-law **Leclerc** with more than 20,000 troops, with the hidden goal of bringing slavery back. Toussaint was tricked and arrested, shipped to France, and died on April 7, 1803, in the Fort de Joux fortress prison.
10. **Yellow fever destroys the French army (1802-1803).** Yellow fever — which locals resisted and European recruits did not — cut down the French in waves. Leclerc himself died of it in November 1802. This biology was one key reason the revolution could win. When France's plan to bring slavery back became clear, even black and free-colored officers who had joined France turned against it, now united under **Dessalines**.
11. **January 1, 1804 — independence.** Dessalines declared independence and changed the colonial name Saint-Domingue back to the old Indigenous **Taíno** name **Haiti / Ayiti** ("land of high mountains"), honoring the people who lived there before Columbus. It was the first independent nation in Latin America, and the first nation in history founded by formerly enslaved people.
12. **The 1804 massacre.** After independence, Dessalines ordered the killing of the remaining French whites on the island — about 3,000 to 7,000 people (February to April 1804). This is the bloody side of the revolution. It must be faced directly, not softened.

**An irony to keep:** France's own sentence, "men are born free and equal," was never meant for the colony's enslaved. The enslaved picked it up and forced it to come true. And Toussaint, who trusted France most, was betrayed by France and died in a French prison.

## 5. Reading the Documents (Primary Sources)

### A. Declaration of the Rights of Man, Article 1 (1789, France)
> Original (common English version): "Men are born and remain free and equal in rights."
> Plain reading: "People are born free, and stay free, with equal rights."
> Many-sided reading: the French who wrote this did not mean the enslaved Africans in the Caribbean. But once said out loud, it could not be held back. The enslaved of Saint-Domingue aimed it back at the people who wrote it. This was the spark of the whole revolution.

### B. The logic of the Code Noir (Black Code, 1685)
> Original (paraphrased): the Code Noir defined the enslaved as the owner's "movable property" (biens meubles), while requiring owners to give food and clothing.
> Plain reading: the law stated in black and white that these people were property to be bought and sold — while pretending to give them a little "protection."
> Reading: the darkest part is not the open cruelty. It is that "law," "order," and even a little "protection" were used to package "using people up" as normal business. To understand the Code Noir is to understand what the enslaved were rising against.

### C. The 1801 Toussaint Constitution (its spirit)
> Content: slavery is abolished forever in Saint-Domingue; all people are born free and are French citizens; Toussaint is governor for life.
> Plain reading: a man born enslaved wrote a constitution saying this land would never again hold a slave.
> Reading: this is one of the very few founding laws in human history written by "former property" with their own hands. It still pledged loyalty to France — and that careful step was the seed of the later tragedy.

### D. Toussaint's words as he boarded the ship (a famous saying — note: very likely later retelling, NOT an archived quote)
> The saying as it is passed down: "In overthrowing me, you have done no more than cut down the trunk of the tree of liberty. It will spring back, for the roots are many and deep."
> Plain reading: "You knocked me down, but you only cut the trunk of the tree of freedom. It will grow back — because its roots are many and deep."
> Reading: this saying is **very likely a later retelling or reworking** (scholars widely mark it as *apocryphal*, meaning not proven to be his exact words). But it names the truth: Toussaint died in 1803, and in 1804 the tree grew back from its roots into a nation.

## 6. Consequences (Short / Medium / Long Term)

- **Short term (1804):** Haiti became the first independent nation in Latin America, the first nation in history founded by formerly enslaved people, and the second independent republic in the Americas (after the United States). It abolished slavery from day one.
- **Medium term (19th century):** Haiti's victory terrified the whole slaveholding Atlantic world — the U.S. South, the Caribbean, Brazil. The simple fact that "the enslaved could win" was a threat. The U.S. did not recognize Haiti until 1862. In 1825, France forced Haiti to pay a huge "indemnity" (a payment) in exchange for recognition; that debt crushed Haiti for over a century and is one structural root of its long poverty. Having lost his richest colony, Napoleon sold Louisiana to the U.S. in 1803 (the **Louisiana Purchase**) — so the Haitian Revolution indirectly doubled the size of the United States.
- **Long term (to today):** The Haitian Revolution is the first time the language of "rights" was forced — by the very people it tried to exclude — to come true all the way. It stretched the "freedom" of America 1776 and France 1789 from "freedom for propertied white men" into "freedom for all." At the same time, the violence after independence, the foreign debt, and the long instability make Haiti a complex case of "after the revolution, it got harder." The light and the cost share one body.

## 7. AP DBQ Thinking Practice

- **Stakeholder grid:** grand blanc / petit blanc / gens de couleur libres / the enslaved / French revolutionaries in Paris / Napoleon / Spain / Britain — what did each want, and what cards did each hold between 1791 and 1804? Whose interests briefly overlapped?
- **Black agency (the key tested angle):** were the enslaved passive, waiting to be freed — or active drivers of history? Argue with the evidence: Bois Caïman, the Northern Plain uprising, Toussaint's 1801 constitution, and black officers switching sides during the yellow-fever war. This is the argument AP graders reward again and again.
- **Continuity & change:** from the property system of the Code Noir (1685) to independence and abolition in 1804 — what changed completely (legal status, who held power), and what stayed the same or got worse (the wrecked plantation economy, foreign debt, internal power struggles)?
- **Compare with Philadelphia 1787:** the U.S. in 1787 wrote enslaved people into the Constitution as three-fifths of a person to keep the slave states in; Haiti in 1804 was founded by the enslaved themselves and abolished slavery completely. The same "freedom" language went opposite ways in the two places — an excellent cross-topic comparison.

## 8. China in the Same Era (Structural Comparison · Rule 0 veto dimension)

1791 was the 56th year of the Qianlong reign of China's Qing dynasty. The Qing at this moment held a vast territory and a population over 300 million, at the peak of what is called the "High Qing" era — one of the largest and most populous empires in the world.

Structural comparison (side by side, no ranking):
- **Place in time:** the Atlantic world in these decades was overturned by a chain of "rights" revolutions — America 1776, France 1789, Haiti 1791. At the very same moment, the Qing empire stood at the peak of power and barely felt this "rights of man" wave, and did not need to answer it. Its legitimacy rested on the Mandate of Heaven, ritual law, and centralized imperial rule — not on "written contract plus individual rights."
- **"Who counts as a full person":** the heart of the Haitian Revolution was a group of legal "property" forcing the sentence "men are born free" to count them in. The Qing political community also kept the vast majority of common people out of power — just in a different way: not through a "movable-property slave" system, but through a different order of rank. In 1791, neither civilization had solved the question of "who has the right to count as a full political person."
- **Facing outward:** in 1793 (the 58th year of Qianlong), Britain's Macartney mission came to China seeking trade and was politely refused — the emperor's reply was that the empire "possessed all things in abundance" and needed nothing. The Atlantic world was expanding violently outward, trading in enslaved people, and erupting in revolution; the great eastern empire chose self-sufficiency and closure. This is only a juxtaposition, not a ranking.

Rule 0: we cannot say the Atlantic world was "advanced" and the Qing "backward," nor the reverse. Each answered its own situation, each with its own wisdom and its own blind spots. This section only sets the two histories of one year, on two sides of the earth, side by side.

## 9. What Historians Argue About (Historiography)

- **The self-emancipation school (C.L.R. James):** the 1938 book *The Black Jacobins* wrote the Haitian Revolution as the enslaved people's own active revolution — a main story of world history, not a side branch of the French Revolution. This school set the tone for "black agency" studies.
- **The Atlantic-revolutions reading:** places Haiti inside the America-France-Latin America "Age of Revolutions" and stresses how the language of rights traveled across the Atlantic.
- **The structural / material reading:** stresses the sugar economy, yellow fever, and the war among empires — the outcome was decided not only by heroes, but also by disease, trade, and geography.
- **The reckoning-with-violence reading:** recent work does not avoid the 1804 massacre, and argues for holding both "the greatness of liberation" and "the violence of the process," refusing to flatten either side.
- **A neutral synthesis:** the Haitian Revolution was both the first time "rights" was forced all the way by the people it tried to exclude, AND a process full of violence on many sides, paid for at a deep cost. Both are true at once; do not make it either/or.

## 10. Common Misconceptions (Myth-Busting)

1. ❌ "Haiti was freed by France or outsiders." → ✅ The enslaved **themselves** lit the fire, fought, and founded the nation — this is exactly what makes it unique in world history.
2. ❌ "Toussaint was a slave the whole time and fought straight out of the fields." → ✅ He was freed in the 1770s, was a free man, could read, and even owned a few enslaved people for a time. His situation was far more complex than "slave hero."
3. ❌ "Slavery ended the moment the revolution began." → ✅ France did not abolish it until 1794, and Napoleon tried to restore it in 1802 — abolition was a back-and-forth, won over 13 years of war.
4. ❌ "The French army was defeated in straight battle by the Haitian army." → ✅ The armed resistance was real, but **yellow fever** cut down European recruits who had no resistance — one key biological reason the French collapsed.
5. ❌ "The free people of color (gens de couleur) naturally sided with the enslaved." → ✅ They were a separate third party; some owned enslaved people, their demand was "equal citizenship," not "abolition," and their side shifted with events.
6. ❌ "The revolution was a purely bright liberation story." → ✅ It also included the 1804 killing of remaining French and violence on many sides. The greatness of freedom and the blood of the process share one body.
7. ❌ "Haiti was fine once it was independent." → ✅ The huge "indemnity" debt France forced on it in 1825 crushed it for over a century — one structural root of its long poverty.

- An Atlantic-revolutions timeline band: America 1776 → France 1789 → Haiti 1791 → Latin America 1810s, with Haiti highlighted as "the only one founded by the enslaved."
- A "lambi conch horn across the night cane fields" image: three perspectives each hear the same horn (the boy blows it / Toussaint hears the awaited signal / the planter hears an unexplained sound in the dark).
- A "one sentence, two directions" chart: Philadelphia 1787 counting people as three-fifths vs Haiti 1804 founded by the enslaved and abolishing slavery — the same "freedom" language, opposite outcomes.
- An arc image of Toussaint: Bréda coachman → 1801 constitution → the snowy Alpine cell of Fort de Joux.
