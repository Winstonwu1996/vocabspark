# Latin American Independence · 1810-1826

> **Lock status**: narrative kernel v0 — pending review
>
> **AP anchor**: AP World History Modern, Unit 5.2-5.3 (Atlantic Revolutions)
> **Through-line**: a real liberation from Spain — led by, and won mostly for, the creole elite
> **Difficulty**: 4/5 (many factions + the hard truth that the bottom barely changed)
> **Expected duration**: 35-45 min

## 0. Open Your Textbook First (Textbook Anchor)

> This belongs to **AP World History Modern, Unit 5 (1750-1900)** — the **Atlantic Revolutions** chain.
>
> Your textbook will tell you:
> - **The Latin American wars of independence ran from about 1810 to 1826.**
> - **They were part of the "Age of Revolutions":** America 1776 → France 1789 → Haiti 1791 → **Latin America 1810-1826.**
> - **Simón Bolívar** (see-MOHN boh-LEE-var) and **José de San Martín** (san mar-TEEN) were the two great liberators.
>
> **What your textbook may NOT make clear** (this is why we need this lesson):
> - **This was the biggest one in the chain.** The first three toppled one colony or one kingdom. This one toppled an entire empire — the Spanish American empire that had ruled across half the globe for 300 years.
> - **It was led by, and won mostly for, the creole elite.** The leaders were **criollos** (kree-OH-yohs) — American-born whites of pure European blood, rich and educated, but blocked from power by the Spain-born **peninsulares**. They were not the people at the bottom.
> - **The spark came from Europe, not America.** In 1808 Napoleon invaded Spain and seized the king. The legitimate king the colonies had obeyed for 300 years suddenly vanished. That power vacuum lit the fire.
> - **The AP DBQ angle is "who led vs who benefited."** Graders reward students who separate **who lit it** (the vacuum from Napoleon's invasion), **who led** (the creole elite), and **who paid the most but gained the least** (the casta bottom — Indians, the enslaved, mixed-race people).
> - **The bottom of society barely changed.** The political break from Spain was real. But the **casta** racial hierarchy, Indian tribute, and slavery in many places continued. This lesson holds both the real liberation and the unfinished one. It does not pick a final verdict for you.

## 1. One-Sentence Anchor (Many Factions at Once)

In **1808**, on one side of the earth, Napoleon's army marched into Spain, seized the Spanish king, and put his own brother on the throne. A European event that looked like it had nothing to do with the Americas blew open a crack across the Atlantic: the king that Spanish America had obeyed for 300 years was suddenly gone. From Mexico to Argentina, the colonies faced one question: **the king is captured — so who is in charge now?** Over the next dozen or so years (about 1810-1826), the entire Spanish American empire shattered in war, then was pieced back together — in war — into a set of new nations. But the word "independence" was squeezed out from groups whose interests were completely different:

- **peninsulares** (pen-in-soo-LAH-res, "people from the peninsula") — born in Spain itself, sent to the Americas as viceroys and archbishops. The top of the pyramid; they looked down on everyone born in the Americas.
- **criollos** (kree-OH-yohs, "creoles") — American-born whites of pure European blood. Rich, landed, educated — and held down hard under the peninsulares by one fact: they were born in the Americas. They were the real **leaders** of this independence, and its real **winners**.
- **casta** (KAH-sta, the racial hierarchy) — **mestizos** (mes-TEE-zohs, Indian-European mixed), **mulatos** (moo-LAH-tohs, Black-white mixed), free Black people — stuck between the whites and the bottom, the largest group, ranked by a chart precise down to skin color.
- **Indians and enslaved Africans** — the very bottom. They paid tribute, did forced labor in the mines and on the estates. The war needed them to fight and bleed — but after independence, their situation barely changed.

Far away, on the other side of the earth, China's Qing dynasty was sliding down from the peak of the **Qianlong** (chyen-LONG) golden age, and within a few decades would crash into the Opium War. On one side, an old empire (Spain) saw its rule in the Americas collapse and shatter into a cluster of new republics at war with each other. On the other, another ancient empire (the Qing) was pushed by pressure from inside and out toward a modern struggle for survival. Two answers to the same question — **after the old order breaks, how do you rebuild?** — were being written at the same time on two sides of the earth.

## 2. The Stage (Geography / Economy / Society / Institutions / Events)

- **Geography**: Spanish America spanned today's Mexico, Central America, and most of western and northern South America (not Brazil — that was Portugal's). It was cut into isolated regions by the towering **Andes** (AN-deez) mountains, vast grasslands (pampas / llanos), and rainforest. This geography matters enormously: the mountains were too high, the roads too hard, the regions too weakly linked — one physical root of why, after independence, the land could not hold together as one big nation and instead broke into many small ones. Administratively, it was divided into several **Viceroyalties**: New Spain (Mexico), New Granada (around Colombia), Peru, and Río de la Plata (around Argentina).
- **Economy**: For 300 years, Spanish America was the empire's wallet — **silver** (above all from Potosí in Peru and Mexico's mines) flowed back to Spain. The economy rested on three kinds of forced labor: Indian **tribute and labor service**, mine labor, and **African slavery** along the coasts and on plantations. A web of trade rules forced the colonies to trade only with Spain — creole merchants and landowners had long resented the "sell only to the mother country" rule.
- **Society**: Here is the key to the whole revolution — a racial hierarchy called **casta**. At the top: peninsulares (born in Spain). Next: criollos (American-born pure-European whites). Below them: mestizos, mulatos, and other mixes. At the bottom: Indians and enslaved Africans. The skin color and bloodline you were born with mostly decided what you could do, how much tax you paid, whether you could hold office. **The key tension was between the top two layers**: criollos were rich and educated, yet — because they were "born in the Americas" — were kept out of power by the peninsulares forever. That resentment was the core engine of the independence movement.
- **Institutions**: For 300 years the colony's legitimacy hung on one thread — **loyalty to the Spanish king**. The viceroy was the king's agent; the Church was the king's ally; the whole order turned around the king as its center. So when Napoleon seized the king in 1808 and a Frenchman took the throne, that thread snapped: with no legitimate king, colonists began forming their own **juntas** (HOON-tas, governing committees) "to guard the region for the king." This seemingly loyal act was exactly the first step toward independence.
- **Events**: In 1808 Napoleon invaded Spain and put his brother Joseph Bonaparte on the throne, creating a power vacuum. From 1810, juntas formed everywhere from Mexico to Buenos Aires. On **September 16, 1810**, the Mexican priest **Hidalgo** gave the "**Grito de Dolores**" (GREE-toh deh doh-LOH-res, "Cry of Dolores"), launching a mostly bottom-up uprising. In the north, **Simón Bolívar** lost and fought again through the 1810s, then won the **Battle of Boyacá** on August 7, 1819, opening New Granada. In the south, **José de San Martín** crossed the Andes in 1817 to free Chile. After the two liberators met secretly at **Guayaquil** (gwai-ah-KEEL) in 1822, San Martín handed the southern war to Bolívar. The **Battle of Ayacucho** (eye-ah-KOO-choh) on December 9, 1824 ended Spanish rule on the South American mainland.

## 3. The Main Characters

> Three perspectives (actor/driver, lonely mediator, receiving-end), including at least one powerless party.

### Simón Bolívar (actor / driver) — 1783-1830 · freed half a continent, then died in the despair that "America cannot be governed"

A real historical person. Born July 24, 1783, in **Caracas**, Venezuela, into an extremely rich **criollo** family — he is the perfect specimen of the creole elite: pure European blood, born in the Americas, rich, landed, educated, sent to Europe as a young man, reading Voltaire and Rousseau, even watching Napoleon's coronation with his own eyes. He is honored as **El Libertador** ("the Liberator").

His revolution was a revolution seen through creole eyes: he did not want to flip the bottom of society over — he wanted to free the **American-born white elite** from the peninsular ceiling and run things himself. He lost and fought again — his early republics collapsed, and for a time he lived in exile. Exiled in Jamaica in 1815, he wrote the famous **Letter from Jamaica**, analyzing why the Americas must become independent and why they would struggle to unite the way North America had. In 1819 he marched his army over the Andes, struck at Boyacá and won, then built his dream nation: **Gran Colombia** (today's Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Panama).

But he misjudged the land and the people under his feet. Gran Colombia was torn by factions and regional warlords, and he grew more and more powerless — in 1828 he even named himself dictator and survived an assassination attempt that year. In 1830, in despair, he wrote the famous line: "He who serves a revolution **plows the sea**." On December 17 of that year, sick and drained of his fortune, he died near Santa Marta at an estate called San Pedro Alejandrino, at age 47. The continent he had freed with his own hands was shattering into a cluster of warring small nations before his eyes. This perspective lets you watch a driver topple an old empire — and then watch the new order fail to come together.

### José de San Martín (lonely mediator) — 1778-1850 · freed the southern half of the continent, then chose to hand over his army and live out his life far from home

A real historical person. Born in 1778 in Yapeyú in today's northeastern Argentina, he served for many years in the Spanish army as a young man and got formal military training — then turned that skill against Spain itself. He was the liberator of the southern front: in January 1817, he led an army of about 4,000 from Mendoza, Argentina, **across the snow-choked Andes**, and in under three weeks struck the Spanish defenders of Chile by surprise and won — a feat in the history of warfare. He then went north by sea to free Peru.

He and Bolívar were two liberators fighting upward from the south and the north — bound to meet in the middle. In 1822, the two met secretly at **Guayaquil**, Ecuador. What was said there has no complete record and is debated to this day — but the result is clear: **San Martín chose to step aside**, handing his troops and the southern war to Bolívar, and quietly left. He did not fight to be "the greatest liberator," did not start a civil war; instead he sailed for Europe, dying in 1850 in Boulogne-sur-Mer, France, never returning to the land he had freed. This perspective lets you watch a mediator, at the moment when fighting on would mean liberator fighting liberator, choose to yield instead of grab power — was that weakness, or a rare kind of clarity? This is the other kind of mediator's ending: not dead from betrayal, but stepping back by choice.

### The casta-bottom mixed-race soldier / Indian estate laborer (receiving-end · DEFAULT) — the one who bled for someone else's independence, while his own situation barely changed

A bridging character, but the role is fully real (this is the DEFAULT perspective). He has no exact name, because history **rarely** left names for these people — and that, exactly, is part of the problem. What is certain is his situation: he might be a **mestizo** (mixed) or **mulato** young man, recruited into the independence army by creole officers; or an Indian estate laborer on the high Andean plateau, whose family paid tribute and did labor service for generations. On that casta hierarchy chart, his place was at the bottom.

The war needed him. He carried a rifle, marched, charged into the fire at Boyacá and Ayacucho, bled, and maybe died on some hillside no one would remember. While fighting, the creole officers promised "freedom," "equality," "no more oppression by Spain." But when the war was over, the Spanish were gone, and the new republic was founded — he looked down and saw: the estate was still the same estate, the tribute still had to be paid, and the casta color order had a new name but at its core was largely unchanged. In some places, slavery continued for decades more. The people in power had changed from whites born in Spain to whites born in the Americas.

This perspective is the hardest, and most honest, part of the revolution. AP World keeps testing **how the fruits of independence were distributed** — political independence was real, but for the very bottom, social liberation was deeply discounted. This perspective lets you stand in the position that paid the most and gained the least, and see clearly the line — between "changing masters" and "rising up" — that was never crossed. It forces you to face this: a real liberation — why, for some people, did almost nothing change?

## 4. The Story (Plot Beats)

1. **The 300-year silver empire (about 1500-1808).** Spain built a vast colonial order in the Americas resting on silver, forced labor, and the casta racial hierarchy. The criollo elite were rich but blocked from power by the peninsulares — resentment built up over generations.
2. **1808 — Napoleon invades Spain (the spark).** Napoleon's army marched into Spain, forced the king off the throne, and put his brother Joseph Bonaparte on it. The legitimate king obeyed for 300 years was suddenly gone — this was the **catalyst**, coming not from inside the Americas but as a surprise hammer-blow from Europe.
3. **1810 — juntas form everywhere ("guarding it for the king").** From Caracas to Buenos Aires, colonial elites formed self-governing juntas, claiming to "guard the region for the captured king." This loyal pose was actually the first step toward independence — once you taste running things yourself, there is no going back.
4. **Sept 16, 1810 — Mexico's "Grito de Dolores" (a different path).** The Mexican priest Miguel Hidalgo gave a cry for independence and launched a **bottom-up uprising** led mostly by Indians and mixed-race farmers. It was very different from the criollo-led movement in South America — more like a social revolution, and for that very reason it terrified Mexico's creole elite. Hidalgo was executed in 1811.
5. **The losing early years (1810s).** The first wave of independence attempts mostly failed: Spain recovered and struck back, and the early republics collapsed one by one. Bolívar went into exile and in 1815 wrote the Letter from Jamaica, thinking through the Americas' future and its struggles.
6. **1817 — San Martín crosses the Andes.** On the southern front, San Martín led his army over the snow-choked Andes, struck and freed Chile by surprise, then went north to free Peru. The northern and southern liberation wars advanced at the same time.
7. **Aug 7, 1819 — Battle of Boyacá (the northern turning point).** Bolívar led a combined Venezuelan and New Granadan army (with a British legion) over the mountains in a surprise strike and won big at Boyacá, opening the road to Bogotá. This is seen as the real start of independence in northern South America. Late that year he set about building **Gran Colombia**.
8. **June 24, 1821 — Battle of Carabobo.** Bolívar beat the Spanish royal army at Carabobo, essentially sealing Venezuela's independence.
9. **1822 — the Guayaquil meeting (the handoff between two liberators).** Bolívar and San Martín met secretly at Guayaquil. Afterward San Martín withdrew, handed over the southern war, and sailed for Europe. Command of South American liberation was unified under Bolívar.
10. **Dec 9, 1824 — Battle of Ayacucho (the end of the continent's liberation).** On the high Andean plateau at Ayacucho in Peru, a combined army commanded by Bolívar's general **Sucre** crushed the main Spanish force. This battle marked the end of Spanish rule on the South American **mainland** (islands like Cuba and Puerto Rico stayed Spanish far longer). By about 1826, the mainland wars of independence had largely wound down.
11. **Fragmentation and the caudillos (1826-1830s).** Independence was won; unity was not. Geographic isolation, regional interests, military strongmen — Gran Colombia broke apart in factional fighting, formally splitting in 1830 into Venezuela, Ecuador, and New Granada. Waves of **caudillos** (kow-DEE-yohs — strongmen who rose by army and personal prestige) appeared, filling the vacuum left by the Spanish king with the gun rather than with institutions.
12. **The liberator's death (1830).** Abandoned, sick, and drained of his fortune, Bolívar died on December 17, 1830, at an estate near Santa Marta. Near the end he wrote: "America is ungovernable for us... He who serves a revolution plows the sea." The continent he had freed with his own hands was shattering before his eyes.

**An irony to keep:** the colonists first formed juntas under the banner of "loyalty to the Spanish king captured by the French" — they began governing themselves in order to "keep the king." But that one step out led toward a complete break from the king. A movement that opened with "loyalty to the crown" ended with "republic."

## 5. Reading the Documents (Primary Sources)

### A. Bolívar's Letter from Jamaica (1815) — its core judgment
> Content (its spirit): In exile, Bolívar argued that Spanish America must become independent, but that it would not easily unite into one single republic the way North America had — 300 years of colonial rule had made the Americans "neither truly Europeans nor Indians," lacking the experience of self-rule and a common foundation.
> Plain reading: a leader in exile saw a cruel thing ahead of time — we will surely win independence, but we will very likely not win unity.
> Many-sided reading: the letter's prediction came almost entirely true. It shows both Bolívar's foresight and the blind spot of the creole view — the "unity" he worried about was unity among elites, while the "liberation" the bottom wanted was not even part of this conversation.

### B. The logic of the casta (racial hierarchy) system
> Content: the colony used a fine-grained ranking chart to sort people by combinations of European, Indian, and African blood — peninsular, criollo, mestizo, mulato, and on down — deciding your taxes, your access to office, your legal standing.
> Plain reading: the skin color and bloodline you were born with mostly wrote, in advance, how far you could go in life.
> Reading: to understand the casta is to understand who this revolution freed and who it did not. The war overthrew the peninsulares' dominance over the criollos, but it did **not** overthrow the casta pyramid itself — it changed the tip of the pyramid and kept the body.

### C. Bolívar's "plowed the sea" (1830, near death)
> The saying as passed down: "America is ungovernable for us. He who serves a revolution plows the sea."
> Plain reading: "America cannot be governed by us. Whoever serves a revolution is plowing the sea."
> Reading: this is the liberator's deepest despair — he overthrew an empire, then found the new order would not come together: fragmentation, civil war, caudillos. "Plowing the sea" means: you cut a furrow with all your strength, the water closes right back over it, and nothing is left. It names exactly the tragedy of "after independence, it got harder."

### D. The "loyalty to the king" wording of the 1810 juntas (note this irony)
> Content (summary): the juntas formed everywhere in 1810 generally raised the banner of "guarding the region for the legitimate king Fernando VII, imprisoned by the French," still nominally loyal to the Spanish crown.
> Plain reading: at first they did not say "we want independence." They said "we'll manage things for the captured king for now."
> Reading: this "loyalty" coat is very important — it shows independence was not decided overnight, but grew step by step out of a seemingly conservative act. Only after they tasted self-rule, and after the Spanish king returned in 1814 and tried to take power back, was that coat finally thrown off.

## 6. Consequences (Short / Medium / Long Term)

- **Short term (about 1826):** The Spanish American mainland empire — spanning half the globe, ruling for 300 years — essentially collapsed, giving birth to a set of new independent republics. The Atlantic-revolutions chain (America → France → Haiti → Latin America) was now complete, and the "divine-right old empire" exited the entire American mainland.
- **Medium term (19th century):** Political independence did not mean stability. Dreams of big nations like Gran Colombia quickly broke apart, and the mainland shattered into many competing states with frequently disputed borders. **Caudillo politics** became the norm — strongmen who rose by army and personal prestige used the gun in place of institutions, and politics stayed turbulent for a long time. Meanwhile, although the casta racial hierarchy loosened in law, the real situation of those at the bottom (Indians, former slaves, mixed-race people) improved slowly, and land and power stayed concentrated in the creole elite.
- **Long term (to today):** Latin American independence left a deep double legacy. On one side: it ended Spanish colonial rule for good, established the principle that "Americans govern the Americas," and gave birth to today's Latin American nations. On the other: political fragmentation, the caudillo tradition, and the continuation of social inequality became one structural root of the repeated turbulence in Latin America over the next century or two. It is a classic case of "successful political independence + limited social liberation" — the light and the unfinished share one body.

## 7. AP DBQ Thinking Practice

- **Stakeholder grid:** peninsular / criollo / mestizo & mulato (the casta middle) / Indians / enslaved Africans / the Spanish crown / Napoleon / Britain — what did each want, and what cards did each hold between 1808 and 1826? When did the creoles' and the bottom's interests briefly overlap (fighting Spain), and when did they split apart (dividing the fruits after independence)?
- **Catalyst vs leadership vs benefit (the key analytical angle):** separate "who lit it" (the power vacuum from Napoleon's invasion — an external catalyst), "who led" (the creole elite), "who benefited" (the creole elite), and "who paid the most and benefited the least" (the casta bottom, the Indians). This is the layered causation AP graders reward again and again.
- **Continuity & change:** from the colonial order before 1808 to the independent republics after 1826 — what changed completely (the break from Spain, the end of monarchy, the creoles in power), and what stayed the same or only changed in name (the casta color order, concentrated land, bottom labor service, slavery continuing in some regions)?
- **Compare with Haiti and North America:** Haiti (1804) was founded by the **enslaved themselves** and abolished slavery completely — a revolution that overturned society to its base; Latin America (1810-1826) was creole-elite-led with the bottom social order largely intact. On the same Atlantic-revolutions chain, "who led, and how deep the liberation went" were utterly different — an excellent cross-topic comparison. You can also compare North America 1776: why could the thirteen colonies hold together as one federation while Spanish America shattered into many? (Differences in geography, colonial structure, and how united the elite were.)

## 8. China in the Same Era (Structural Comparison · Rule 0 veto dimension)

The years of the Latin American wars of independence (about 1808-1826) were a turning point as China's Qing dynasty slid from peak toward decline — the Qianlong Emperor died in 1799, the Jiaqing and then Daoguang emperors reigned in turn, and the afterglow of the "High Qing" golden age was fading. The White Lotus Rebellion (1796-1804) had just drained the treasury, and the real storm — the Opium War of 1840 — was still waiting a dozen-plus years away.

Structural comparison (side by side, no ranking):
- **Two answers to "after the old order breaks, what now":** Latin America faced an **external collapse first** — Napoleon yanked away the pillar of legitimacy (the Spanish king), forcing the colonies to answer "who is in charge now," and the result was **fragmentation**: one great empire splitting into many warring new republics plus caudillo strongman politics. China faced an old order that **loosened gradually under pressure from inside and out, then was forced into a modern struggle for survival** — its response was not splitting into small states but a painful search for a path to self-strengthening and reform while "holding the great unity together." Both faced "how to rebuild after the old order breaks," but one moved toward splitting apart and reassembling, the other toward fiercely defending integration and remaking.
- **Different roots of legitimacy:** Spanish America's legitimacy hung on one external thread — "loyalty to the Spanish king." Once the king was captured, the thread snapped and the order scattered. China's political legitimacy rested on the internal logic of the Mandate of Heaven, ritual law, and centralized great unity — it did not depend on any one particular monarch's person. So its crisis was not "the king is gone, what now," but "this all-under-heaven order, continuous for two thousand years, how does it meet a modern world of gunboats and cannon?" Two kinds of legitimacy produced two ways of breaking and two paths of rebuilding.
- **Who has the right to rule:** the core conflict in Latin America was the criollos (the locally born elite) seizing power from the peninsulares (the people sent from the mother country) — a fight of "local elite vs outside rulers," after which the bottom stayed at the bottom. China's power structure in the same period ran on another logic (Manchu/Han, gentry, the civil-service exam), and its common people at the bottom were likewise kept out of power, just in a different way. In these decades, neither civilization had solved the question of "how the majority at the base of the pyramid get counted into politics."

Rule 0: we cannot say Latin America's "fragmentation" was failure and China's "integration" was success, nor the reverse. Each answered the situation it was thrown into, each with its own costs and its own wisdom. This section only sets side by side, within the same span of years, two answers to "how to rebuild after the old order breaks" on two sides of the earth — one an empire shattering because its pillar was pulled out from the outside, the other an empire painfully holding integration together under pressure from both sides as it moved toward a modern struggle for survival.

## 9. What Historians Argue About (Historiography)

- **The national-liberation view (patriotic / heroic narrative):** writes independence as a great national liberation led by "founding fathers" like Bolívar and San Martín, a people winning freedom — the mainstream official narrative of Latin American nations, stressing heroes and nation-building.
- **The revisionist / social reading:** stresses that this was essentially a revolution by the creole elite to seize power for themselves, with the bottom's racial and labor order largely continuing — it questions the "liberation of all" narrative and asks, "who was actually freed?"
- **The Atlantic-revolutions reading:** places Latin America inside the America-France-Haiti "Age of Revolutions," stressing the external catalyst (Napoleon's invasion) and the spread of transatlantic ideas (the Enlightenment, republicanism, the language of rights).
- **The structural / geographic reading:** stresses the barrier of the Andes, the divisions of colonial administration, and centrifugal regional economies — the post-independence fragmentation was not just "people not uniting," but was also decided by geography and colonial structure.
- **A neutral synthesis:** Latin American independence was both a real, complete anti-colonial political liberation, AND a complex transformation in which leadership and benefit were highly concentrated in the creole elite while social liberation for the bottom was deeply discounted. Both are true at once; do not make it either/or.

## 10. Common Misconceptions (Myth-Busting)

1. ❌ "Latin American independence was a revolution where the people at the bottom rose up and overthrew their oppressors." → ✅ It was mainly led by, and won for, the **criollos** (locally born white elite); the bottom (Indians, the enslaved, mixed-race people) paid the most but their social situation barely changed. The political independence was real; the social liberation was deeply discounted.
2. ❌ "Bolívar single-handedly freed all of South America." → ✅ Bolívar advanced from the north, San Martín from the south, alongside generals like Sucre and countless soldiers from the bottom; the decisive Battle of Ayacucho was commanded by Bolívar's general Sucre. This was a war of many people, many fronts, over more than a decade — not one hero's solo act.
3. ❌ "The independence movement aimed to break from Spain from the very start." → ✅ The 1810 juntas first raised the banner of "guarding the region for the king captured by Napoleon," still loyal to the crown; complete independence grew step by step, not as a goal set from the beginning.
4. ❌ "Independence brewed and erupted from inside the Americas." → ✅ The direct **catalyst was an external event** — Napoleon's 1808 invasion of Spain, the seizure of the king, and the vacuum of legitimacy. Without that hammer-blow from Europe, the timing would have looked very different.
5. ❌ "After independence, Latin America became free and democratic." → ✅ What followed was long political fragmentation and **caudillo** (strongman) politics — strongmen who rose by army and prestige used the gun in place of institutions, and the turbulence lasted a century or two.
6. ❌ "Latin American independence and the Haitian Revolution were the same kind of thing." → ✅ Completely different. Haiti was founded by the enslaved themselves and abolished slavery completely (society overturned to its base); Latin America was creole-elite-led with the bottom order continuing. On the same revolution chain, the depth of liberation was worlds apart.
7. ❌ "Gran Colombia was a solid, stable great nation." → ✅ It was Bolívar's dream of unity, but it quickly broke apart from geographic isolation and factional fighting, splitting in 1830 — "winning independence but not winning unity" is precisely this revolution's core tragedy.

## 11. Thinking Questions (Open-Ended, No Conclusion)

1. The colonists first formed juntas to "keep the Spanish king captured by the French" — a movement that opened with "loyalty to the crown" ended with "republic." How does a conservative act grow into a complete revolution? Does this tell you the direction of history is designed, or walked into?
2. This independence was led by, and benefited, the creole elite, while the bottom paid the most and barely rose. If a revolution "frees" a country but does not free most of the people in it, is it still a successful revolution? By what standard do you measure?
3. After the Guayaquil meeting, San Martín chose to hand over his army and live far away — not fighting for the title of "greatest liberator," not starting a war of liberator against liberator. Was that weakness, or a rare kind of clarity? In his place, would you yield or fight?
4. Near death, Bolívar said "he who serves a revolution plows the sea." He overthrew an empire but could not piece the new order together. Which is harder — overthrowing an old world, or building a new one? Why?
5. In the same span of years, Latin America's old empire shattered into many small nations because its pillar was pulled out from the outside, while China's old empire fiercely held integration together under pressure from both sides as it moved toward a modern struggle for survival. Looking at these two answers to "how to rebuild after the old order breaks," do you see one as wiser — or two different choices forced by two different situations?
6. This time you mainly heard two positions — the creole liberator and the soldier at the bottom. How would a peninsular viceroy, born in Spain and driven out, tell this history? For an Indian village on the high Andean plateau, was there any difference at all before and after independence? Whose voice have you not heard yet?

## Appendix A: Key Timeline

- c.1500-1808: Spain builds a colonial order in the Americas resting on silver, forced labor, and the casta racial hierarchy.
- 1808: Napoleon invades Spain, forces the king off the throne, installs his brother Joseph Bonaparte as king of Spain — a vacuum of legitimacy (the catalyst).
- 1810: From Caracas to Buenos Aires, juntas form, nominally "guarding the region for the captured king."
- 1810.9.16: Mexican priest Miguel Hidalgo gives the "Grito de Dolores," launching a mostly bottom-up uprising.
- 1811: Hidalgo is executed by Spain.
- 1812: The Cortes of Cádiz in Spain enacts the Constitution of 1812 (a liberal constitution; influential in Latin America but abolished by the restored king in 1814).
- 1815: Bolívar, in exile in Jamaica, writes the Letter from Jamaica.
- 1817.1-2: San Martín crosses the Andes, strikes and frees Chile by surprise.
- 1819.8.7: Battle of Boyacá — Bolívar wins big, the turning point for independence in northern South America; late that year he begins building Gran Colombia.
- 1821.6.24: Battle of Carabobo — essentially seals Venezuela's independence.
- 1822: Bolívar and San Martín meet secretly at Guayaquil; San Martín withdraws and hands over the southern war.
- 1824.12.9: Battle of Ayacucho — a combined army commanded by Bolívar's general Sucre crushes the main Spanish force, ending the liberation of the South American mainland.
- c.1826: The mainland wars of independence largely wind down.
- 1830: Gran Colombia breaks apart (into Venezuela, Ecuador, and New Granada).
- 1830.12.17: Bolívar dies, sick, at the San Pedro Alejandrino estate near Santa Marta, leaving the words "plows the sea."
- 1850: San Martín dies in Boulogne-sur-Mer, France, never having returned to his homeland.

## Appendix B: Core Vocabulary

- **peninsular** — a person born in Spain, sent to the Americas to hold power (the top layer)
- **criollo** (creole) — an American-born white of pure European blood (the elite; led and benefited from independence)
- **casta** — the colonial racial hierarchy ranked by bloodline/skin color
- **mestizo / mulato** — Indian-European mixed / Black-white mixed
- **caudillo** — a strongman who rose after independence by army and personal prestige
- **junta** — a self-governing committee (the first step toward independence)
- **Viceroyalty** — the top administrative division of the colony
- **Gran Colombia** — the unified great nation Bolívar built (dissolved 1830)
- **Grito de Dolores** — the "Cry of Dolores," Hidalgo's 1810 call to uprising in Mexico
- **Cortes of Cádiz** — the Spanish assembly that enacted the liberal Constitution of 1812
- **El Libertador** — "the Liberator," Bolívar's honorific
- **Andes** — the mountain range; one physical root of the fragmentation
