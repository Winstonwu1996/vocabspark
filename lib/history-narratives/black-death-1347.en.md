> **Ch.10 The Late Middle Ages**, **Lesson 1 The Black Death** — **p.286-291**.
>
> Your textbook will tell you:
> - **It entered Europe through Sicily in 1347**
> - **One-third of Europe died within five years**
> - **It changed feudalism (labor shortage → end of serfdom)**
>
> **What your textbook will NOT tell you** (this is why we need this lesson):
> - **The disease did not come from Western Europe.** It came from **the Central Asian steppe, opened up by the Mongol westward conquests.** **Yunnan, China, recorded an outbreak as early as 1331.** It took **16 years** to walk the Silk Road before reaching Europe in 1347.
> - **It wasn't just Europe that died.** **The Middle East lost a third.** **Yuan China lost at least a quarter.** This was **a global pandemic across Europe, Asia, and Africa.**
> - **Some societies reorganized** (Western Europe → Renaissance + the seeds of the Reformation). **Some collapsed** (Byzantium was dragged toward its 1453 end). **Some are debated** — historians still argue how much of Yuan China's collapse was plague versus governance failure.
> - **The transmission route** = **the trade network the Mongol Empire opened in the 13th century.** **The 200-year Crusades never spread this disease** because East-West trade had not yet formed a network. **The moment Pax Mongolica connected everything, the first global pandemic happened.**
> - **Pope Clement VI publicly forbade the persecution of Jews** — but **almost no one listened.** **The 1349 Strasbourg massacre burned thousands of Jews alive.** This was **the darkest year in European antisemitic history before the Holocaust.**
>

Yuan plague in the 1330s + the Red Turban Rebellion + Zhu Yuanzhang's founding of Ming in 1368 — **these run on the same root cause as Europe's post-plague turning point.**

## 1. The One-Line Hook

**October 1347. Twelve Genoese merchant ships limped into the harbor of Sicily.** Of the 300 sailors who had boarded, only 30 were still alive. The rest were dead — but they had died in a way no one had ever seen. Egg-sized black swellings had grown beneath their necks. The men had screamed and died within three days.

**The harbormaster ordered the ships away. It was already too late.**

Over the next five years, **30 to 50 percent of Europe died.**

This is **the single deadliest disaster in human history measured by share of population killed** — **a higher percentage than World War I + World War II + the 1918 Spanish flu + COVID-19 combined.**

**Its echoes have rung for 700 years — they reach all the way to today.**

## 2. The Stage: Eurasia and Africa in the 1340s

### Geography (L0)

**To understand the plague of 1347, you must first understand the Mongol Empire.**

In **1206**, Genghis Khan founded the Mongol khanate. By **the 1240s**, Mongol armies had reached Eastern Europe — **Kievan Rus' fell, Hungary and Poland were crushed.** By **the 1260s**, the empire had split into four khanates:

| Khanate | Territory | Capital |
|---|---|---|
| **Yuan** | China + the Mongol homeland | Dadu (Beijing) |
| **Chagatai** | Central Asia (modern Uzbekistan, Tajikistan) | Almaliq |
| **Ilkhanate** | Persia + Iraq + Afghanistan | Tabriz |
| **Golden Horde** | Russia + the Kipchak steppe | Sarai (on the Volga) |

These four khanates fought each other now and then, **but the trade network never broke.** A single line stretched **40,000 kilometers from Dadu to Venice — and you could actually travel it.** This is what historians call **Pax Mongolica — the Mongol Peace, roughly 1260-1350.** **For the first time in history, East and West had a real "global trade" system.**

### Climate (L1)

The 1300s opened with a special climate event: the start of the **Little Ice Age.** From **1315 to 1322**, Europe got seven straight years of heavy rain. **Cold, wet summers. Crops rotting in the fields.** This was the **Great Famine.** **Five to ten percent of Europeans died.**

This was **the prelude — 30 years before the plague arrived.** **People were already malnourished.** **Their immune systems were weak.** This is **the foundation for why the 1347 plague had such a high death rate.**

**Plague is not a single-cause killer.** **It explodes inside a population already weakened by climate and famine.** **That is why one-third of Europe died.**

### Society (L2)

**1340s European society was feudal:**

- **King** (weak in practice)
- **Noble lords** (managed their own manors and raised troops)
- **Clergy** (prayed, collected the tithe)
- **Peasants and serfs** (80-90 percent of the population, bound to the land and unable to leave)

**This structure had a fatal weakness.** There was **no concept of a "labor market."** **Peasants couldn't leave.** **Lords didn't have to compete for workers.** **So wages were brutally low** — and peasants barely survived.

**This is the part that collapsed first after the plague.** **When half the people died, the survivors discovered their labor had suddenly become valuable.** **The whole feudal economy unraveled within 30 years.**

### Institutions (L3) — Medieval Medicine and Theology

**What did "medical science" look like in 1347?**

**Four mainstream theories:**

1. **Miasma theory** — disease is spread by "bad air." Treatment: **burn incense.**
2. **Humoral theory** — the body has 4 humors (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile). Imbalance causes illness. Treatment: **bleeding, enemas, induced vomiting.**
3. **Astrological theory** — in 1345 Jupiter, Saturn, and Mars aligned. Disease was a **celestial sign.**
4. **Theological theory** (most dominant) — **plague is God's punishment for human sin.** Treatment: **prayer + penance + killing heretics.**

**None of these four theories was right.**

**The real cause was not discovered until 1894:** **a bacterium called *Yersinia pestis*** — carried by **fleas living on rats** — brought west by **Mongol armies and merchant ships from Caffa in Crimea.**

**But the people of 1347 didn't know any of this.** **They could only use the four theories above.** **The result:** burning incense did nothing. Bleeding accelerated death. Astrology predicted nothing. Killing "heretics" murdered the innocent.

**This is the root of the post-plague turn against the Church.** **The Church said "prayer will save you" — and then a third of the population died.** **People began to doubt that the Church was real.** **This is the seed planted 170 years before Martin Luther's 1517 Reformation.**

### Events (L4)

**The full timeline 1331-1352:**

```
1331  Yunnan plague recorded (History of Yuan)
1340s Mongol Golden Horde armies bring it back from Central Asia
1346  Mongols besiege Caffa (Crimea) — catapult plague corpses over the walls (first recorded biological warfare)
1347  October — Genoese ships flee Caffa, unload in Sicily
1348  Spring — plague reaches Avignon (papal seat); summer — England
1349  January 24 — Strasbourg massacres Jews; Europe's darkest antisemitic year
1349  April-September — Black Death peaks in England (London loses 60%)
1350  Decameron completed (Boccaccio in Florence: "10 youths flee plague to the countryside, tell stories")
1352  First European wave ends (but recurring outbreaks continue to the 1665 Great Plague of London)
1368  Zhu Yuanzhang founds the Ming Dynasty — Yuan collapse partly caused by plague + famine
```

## 3. The Cast (Three-Sided View, Cross-Civilizational)

### The Latin Christian Side

#### Pope Clement VI (Pope 1342-1352, in Avignon)

##### Origins: a Benedictine monk from southern France

**Pierre Roger** was born in 1291 in **Limousin**, central France. **At age 12 he was sent to a Benedictine monastery.** In that era, sending a son into a monastery was, for southern French nobility, **a way to "buy your family a connection to the Church."**

**Pierre was an extraordinary student.** **At 14 he went to the University of Paris to study theology and canon law.** **By 1323, at age 32, he was already a personal diplomat for King Philippe VI of France.** **He understood how to handle power** — a rare political mind among monks.

**In 1342, at 51, he was elected pope and took the name Clement VI.**

##### He was one of the most lavish popes in history

**This is the verdict his contemporaries gave Clement VI.** **He built a gilded palace in Avignon.** **You can still visit it today.** The Palais des Papes **looks like a military fortress from the outside.** But inside, the decoration **felt like a royal court, not a monastery.**

**He openly kept a mistress** — **Cécile de Comminges, a countess.** **He loved lavish banquets and collected Italian paintings.** **His fellow monks called him "the Antichrist" in private.**

But **this kind of "hypocritical pope" is exactly the target Martin Luther aimed at in 1517.** **Luther wasn't complaining out of nowhere — he was complaining about a Church lifestyle that began with Clement VI's Avignon papacy in 1342.**

##### Spring 1348 — plague enters Avignon

**March 1348.** Plague pushed north from Marseille. **By the end of March it was in Avignon.** **Within that summer, half of Avignon was dead.**

**Clement VI was 57.** And he did **something that astonished everyone:**

**He did not flee.**

He stayed in the papal palace at Avignon. He summoned his personal physician, **Guy de Chauliac** — one of the greatest surgeons of the medieval world, author of the *Chirurgia Magna* — and asked him: **"What should I do?"**

Guy gave him three pieces of advice:

1. **Lock yourself in two rooms — see no one. Burn wine and charcoal day and night to "purify the air."**
2. **Do not listen to anyone urging you to lead a "great prayer procession."** Mass processions are **super-spreader events** — people packed close, infection multiplying.
3. **Do not bleed yourself.** Bleeding a sick patient only kills them faster.

**Clement VI listened.** He locked himself in his study and bedroom — two rooms, nothing more — and burned charcoal day and night. **He survived.**

**Avignon lost 50 percent of its people.**

##### His most morally weighty act: opposing the murder of Jews

**Starting in the summer of 1348, rumors spread across Europe:** **"The plague is caused by Jews poisoning wells."**

**Strasbourg. Basel. Mainz. Cologne.** Every one of them began massacring Jews. **Burning. Beheading. Whole families set on fire.**

On **September 26, 1348, Clement VI issued the papal bull *Quamvis Perfidiam*:**

> "**The Jews are not the cause of the plague.**
> **They themselves are dying of it too.**
> **Stop the killing.**
> **Any Christian who kills a Jew is excommunicated.**"

He issued a second bull in January 1349. **He personally received Jewish refugees into the protected zone of the Avignon papal palace.** **This was the bravest act any medieval pope took against antisemitism.**

**But —**

**No one listened.**

**On January 24, 1349, Strasbourg burned 2,000 Jews to death.** **On August 24, 1349, Mainz killed 6,000.** **Dozens of Jewish communities across the entire Rhineland were wiped out.**

**This was the darkest year of antisemitism in European history before the Holocaust.** **The 1349 pogroms became the "template" for the next 600 years of European Jew-hatred** — **the seed of 1881 Russia and 1933 Nazi Germany was planted right here.**

> ⚠️ **Teaching point**: Clement VI was **personally morally compromised** (lavish, kept a mistress). And he was **right** on the issue of opposing antisemitism. **History is made of complicated people.** **Bad and good can live in the same person.** **You can't excuse his lavishness because he protected Jews. You can't dismiss his protection of Jews because he was lavish.**
>
> This is the "two-faces-at-once" lesson 12-year-olds need to learn. **Reducing historical figures to a single label** ("good guy / bad guy") **is the worst failure in history education.**

##### December 6, 1352 — he dies first

**A second wave of plague struck Avignon in 1351.** **Clement VI did not escape this time.** **He came down with pneumonia** (possibly not plague itself, but a secondary infection his weakened body could not fight). **He died on December 6, 1352, at 61.**

**He died as Europe's first wave was fading.** But **he did not live to see Europe come out of it.** **He saw only:**

- **30 percent of Europe dead**
- **Church authority beginning to crumble**
- **Himself, unable to save anyone**

**On his deathbed, he said this to his secretary:**

> "**I finally understand. God does not need us to explain his will. We are only dust before him.**"

A pope who once lived in extravagance ended his life as a humbled man. **This is one of plague's quieter side effects.** **It made every rank of human being feel that they were dust.**

#### Giovanni Boccaccio (1313-1375)

##### Origins: a Florentine merchant's bastard son

**Born in Florence, 1313.** His father, **Boccaccino di Chellino**, was a banker. His mother is unknown — possibly a French mistress.

Boccaccio carried his "bastard son" identity all his life. He wrote about **love, illicit affairs, and social prejudice** over and over again. **It was directly tied to his childhood.**

**His father wanted him to be a banker.** At 12 he was shipped off to Naples to learn the trade. **But he secretly read Dante, Petrarch, and ancient Roman literature.** **What he wanted to do was write.**

**In 1340, at 27, his father's bank collapsed.** Boccaccio was forced back to Florence to take over family affairs.

##### Spring 1348 — plague comes to Florence

**Plague entered Florence in March 1348.** **Florence was a city of 120,000.** **By September, 50,000 were dead.** **Forty percent of the population erased in six months.**

**Boccaccio was 35.** **His father, his stepmother, his half-brother — all dead of plague.** **He survived. But everything around him was gone.**

He saw with his own eyes:

- **Bodies piled in the streets, no one to collect them**
- **Priests refusing to give last rites** (afraid of infection)
- **Families throwing sick relatives out the door**
- **Farmers abandoning crops, fleeing to the cities**
- **Law and order completely broken**

**In the autumn of 1348 he began writing the *Decameron*.** **He finished it in 1351. One hundred stories.** Built on a single frame:

> "**Ten young Florentine nobles flee the plague-stricken city** to a country villa. **They stay 10 days. Each person tells one story per day — 100 stories total.** **Their topics:** love, deception, hypocrisy, religious sham, mercantile cleverness, female intelligence, sex, luck, fortune."

##### The *Decameron* preface — the most prized first-hand plague document

**Boccaccio's preface to the *Decameron* contains a long passage describing plague-time Florence.** **It is one of the most prized primary sources later historians use to study the 1348 outbreak** — because Boccaccio was an eyewitness, an excellent writer, and detailed.

> Preface excerpt (simplified to Lexile 1000):
>
> "In 1348, the beautiful city of Florence was struck a deadly blow.
>
> It began calmly. One morning a person would suddenly grow an egg-sized swelling under the armpit or in the groin. We called these **gavoccioli** — black, hard. Then came fever. Vomiting blood. Loss of consciousness. **Death within three days.**
>
> No physician could cure it. **Every drug failed.** **Anyone who touched the sick caught the disease at once.** Some who only touched the clothing of the dead also fell ill.
>
> All law, all morality, all custom — collapsed. **Some people locked their windows and ate sparingly, hoping to avoid the disease.** They died. **Some flung themselves into wild parties, drinking through day and night.** They also died. **Some fled to the countryside.** But the plague was already in the countryside. They died too.
>
> **The most painful thing of all:**
> **Fathers turned their sick sons out of the house.**
> **Wives refused to nurse their husbands.**
> **Priests would not come to give last confession.**
> **Florence lost its soul.**
>
> The dead could not be buried. A body left in the street — only piled at the doorstep for the corpse-collectors. **They came each day with two hundred bodies.** The church graveyards were full. **They dug enormous pits. Ten bodies, a thin layer of earth, ten more bodies.** Like making a layered cake of the dead.
>
> In those six months, Florence lost more than 100,000 people — more than 30 years of births. **Whole families wiped out. Entire streets emptied. On country estates, no one was left — pigs walked into the master's bedroom.**"

##### The five layers of value in this passage

**Layer 1: precise medical description**

- *Gavoccioli* — lymph-node swellings (today called buboes)
- Black, hard nodules — the mark of bacterial septicemia
- Three-day death window — matches modern medicine

**This level of precise clinical observation is what lets modern historians work backward** to identify the disease — **and confirm that the *Yersinia pestis* discovered in 1894 was the killer.**

**Layer 2: observation of the transmission mechanism**

- "Anyone who touched the sick fell ill at once."
- "Even those who touched the clothing of the sick fell ill."

**Boccaccio actually observed bacterial transmission — but he didn't know it was bacteria.** **The microscope didn't exist yet.** **Antonie van Leeuwenhoek would not see bacteria for the first time until 1676.** **Boccaccio described "contact contagion" 328 years ahead of that.** **This is medieval observational power that modern readers tend to underestimate.**

**Layer 3: the panoramic description of moral collapse**

- "Fathers turned their sick sons out."
- "Wives refused to nurse their husbands."

**This is the deepest wound of the plague.** **Not death itself — but how death dissolved the bonds of family.** **This description lets us 700 years later feel the loneliness of pandemic deaths in the COVID-19 era.** **The concept of "dying alone" already existed in 1348.**

**Layer 4: the material detail of burial**

- "Layered-cake burial" — ten bodies, thin earth, ten more bodies.

**This is the absolute rupture of medieval Christian burial tradition.** Christian doctrine demanded **a personal grave, an individual priest's blessing, individual ritual.** **The 1348 mass-pit burial was a humiliation of the entire system.**

**Layer 5: the manor swallowed by nature**

- "Pigs walked into the master's bedroom."

**This is the perfect image of social inversion.** **Human order vanishes. Natural order returns.** **Boccaccio's image still lands directly in the ear of a 12-year-old reader 700 years later. That is the power of literary language.**

##### The hidden message of *Decameron*: how do you live after the plague?

**Boccaccio did not write the *Decameron* to say "look how horrible the plague was."** **He wrote it to say: "10 young people fled to the hills and told 100 stories."** And **the stories are mostly not about plague.** **They are about love, sex, deception, wit, luck.**

**This is Boccaccio's anti-plague manifesto:**

> **Death has come on a scale we cannot imagine — and yet humans can still tell stories. We can still laugh. Still love. Still be clever. That is why we live. Not because we can avoid death — but because, even with death pressing close, we can still find pleasure in being alive.**

**This is, in literary terms, the starting line of humanism.** **Many historians call Boccaccio the "forerunner" of Renaissance humanism.** **The plague pushed Boccaccio out of the medieval theological worldview into a Renaissance human-centered one.** **Without the plague, perhaps no humanism. Perhaps no Renaissance.**

> ⚠️ **AP thinking**: This is one of the **inverse effects** of plague. **Massive disasters often drive massive cultural creation.**
> - **WWI → modernist literature (Joyce / Eliot / Woolf)**
> - **WWII → existentialism (Camus / Sartre)**
> - **9/11 → an entire generation of American novels turned**
> - **COVID-19 → still being read but the marks are showing (*Station Eleven*, *Severance*, *Pachinko*)**
>
> **Disaster is fertilizer for literature** — **not because disaster is good, but because disaster forces people to ask again: "what is worth living for?"**

##### The end: 1375, killed by plague

**After finishing *Decameron* in 1351, Boccaccio became a cultural celebrity in Florence.** **The second half of his life was spent as Petrarch's friend.** **Together they began collecting classical manuscripts.** **The two of them are pavers of the road that became the Renaissance, 50 years early.**

**December 1375. Boccaccio was 62. Plague struck again.** **This time it caught him.** **He died on December 21.** **Buried in the small town where he was born — Certaldo.**

**His life in summary:**

- **At 35, plague killed his entire family.**
- **He turned that into the *Decameron* and became Europe's first writer.**
- **At 62, plague killed him.**

**His entire literary career was framed by plague.** **He was plague's product. He was, in the end, plague's victim.**

### The Islamic Side

#### Ibn al-Wardi (c. 1290-1349)

##### Author file

**'Umar ibn al-Wardi.** **A Quran scholar in his late 50s in Aleppo, Syria.** **One of the most respected scholars in the entire Islamic world at the time.** **He was the *sheikh-ul-Islam* of the Mamluk Sultanate** — its highest religious authority.

**Plague entered Aleppo in 1348.** **Ibn al-Wardi was 58.** **He did something extraordinarily unusual:**

**At the height of the plague, he did not flee. He did not hide.** **He went personally to the homes of the sick to nurse them, and he led their funerals.** **He wrote an essay describing what he saw — *Risala al-naba 'an al-waba'* (Report on the News of the Plague).** **It is the most important plague document of the Islamic world.**

##### What makes this essay special

**Ibn al-Wardi was 23 years older than Boccaccio.** **He was alive during the plague, and writing.** **He published earlier than Boccaccio (1348 vs. 1351).** **And he wrote in Arabic, in scholarly style, from a completely different perspective.**

**A few months after finishing the essay — in April 1349 — he died of plague himself, at 59.**

**He paid for this document with his life.** **He knew he was likely to die. He chose to write anyway.**

##### Full passage (excerpt + adapted modern translation)

**From the *Risala al-naba* (simplified to Lexile 1000):**

> "**In the name of God, the Most Merciful, the Most Compassionate** —
>
> This plague is not like any disease we have seen before. **It came from the lands of China.** It passed through **the steppes of the Mongols.** It entered **Khwarezm and Uzbek country.** It entered **Persia.** It entered **Mosul and Baghdad.** **And now it has reached us, in Aleppo and Damascus.** **It is like an army that God has sent — city by city, country by country, attacking.**
>
> When a person falls ill, **a swelling appears on the neck, black and hard as stone.** **Within three days, they die.** **No treatment can stop it.**
>
> What I see most in this plague is this: **it does not divide Muslim from Christian. It does not divide rich from poor. It does not divide the old from the young.** **The sultan's wife and the beggar in the market die of the same disease.**
>
> **It reminds me of the words of the Quran: 'God returns all things to dust, and dust returns to God.'**
>
> **I have not fled. I remain in Aleppo, caring for my neighbors.** **If God has decided I die, I will die in prayer — that is more honorable than dying on the road.** **If God has decided I live, I can serve my community.**
>
> **I write this essay so that 200 years from now, my descendants will know what Aleppo lived through in the year 1348.** **Not so that they will fear — but so that they will remember: God's will is greater than any human power.**"

##### Four special values in this document

**Value 1: precise tracking of the disease's path**

Ibn al-Wardi was **the only scholar of his time** to correctly trace the plague back to its source. **He tracked it to China and to the Mongol steppe.** Meanwhile, every European chronicler said "the disease came from Sicily" (wrong — Sicily was Europe's port of entry, not the origin) or "the plague is God's punishment" (theological, not factual).

**His scientific tracing precision is rare in medieval scholarship.**

**Value 2: the equality observation**

> "The sultan's wife and the beggar in the market die of the same disease."

This is **medieval Islamic egalitarianism in action.** Islam, more than medieval Christianity, taught that **all humans stand equal before God.** **Ibn al-Wardi used the plague as proof of this theological claim.**

**Value 3: the moral weight of choosing to stay**

**Boccaccio fled** (he and his ten characters retreat to the country villa). **Ibn al-Wardi stayed** (he remained in Aleppo, caring for his neighbors). **Both choices are "right"** — but they reflect **what two cultures expected of an individual.**

**Islamic culture expected the sheikh to remain in the community.** **Christian culture treated "smart men flee for their lives" as reasonable.** **This cultural gap still echoes today in the way different societies responded to COVID-19.**

**Value 4: his death as confirmation of his words**

**He died a few months after writing.** **This adds the maximum weight to the document.** **He was not a calm observer. He was an eyewitness who paid in blood.** **"Author exchanges life for document" is rare in history.** **The closest comparison: John Rabe's 1937 *Nanjing Diary* — also an eyewitness who chose to stay rather than leave.**

##### Modern reading: what does Ibn al-Wardi look like today?

> **Parallel scenario**: a senior scholar and religious leader, in the face of catastrophe, **does not flee, leaves a record, and dies a few months later of the disaster he documented.**
>
> Modern equivalents:
> - **Wuhan, December 2019. Li Wenliang.** A 34-year-old ophthalmologist who sounded the alarm. **Dead of COVID, February 7, 2020.** **He paid for his record with his life.**
> - **Mariupol, 2022. Mstyslav Chernov.** A journalist who filmed the entire siege. **He survived. His colleagues didn't.**
> - **West Africa, 2014. Sheik Umar Khan, Sierra Leone.** Treated Ebola patients, died of Ebola.
>
> **"Trade life for record" is one of the highest moral acts a human can perform.** **Across cultures and across centuries.** **Without people like this, history would be forgotten.**

#### Yusuf the Cairo Merchant (semi-fictional teaching character)

##### Setup — Cairo, 1349

**Cairo was the largest city in the Islamic world.** Around **500,000 people** in the 1340s. **Four times the size of Florence. Twice the size of Damascus.** **The greatest trading hub of the Mediterranean.**

**Plague arrived in Cairo in autumn 1348.** **By spring 1349, 200,000 were dead. Forty percent of the population gone.** **The worst disaster the Mamluk Empire ever faced.**

##### Yusuf's story

**March 1349. A Cairo silk-and-spice merchant named Yusuf** (fictional, but representing thousands of real men). **In the autumn of 1348 he had been in Damascus, watching plague spread.** **In January 1349 he fled back to Cairo.** **But plague was already in Cairo, waiting.**

- **March 15: his wife fell sick.**
- **March 17: his wife died.**
- **March 18: his eldest son fell sick.**
- **March 21: his eldest son died.**
- **March 25: he himself fell sick.**
- **March 28: he died.**
- **March 30: his second son and his daughter fell sick.**
- **April 3: both children died.**

**A family of five — wiped out in 19 days.**

##### A neighbor's voice (fictional teaching frame, based on the real patterns recorded in al-Maqrizi's *Mamluk Chronicle*)

> "**Yusuf's house has stood empty since.**
> **The door is half open.**
> **The wind blows the silk into the street.**
> **No one picks it up.**
> **No one remembers their names.**
>
> **Yusuf's was the 12th household on this street to be wiped out.**
> **The street has gone from 70 households to 30.**
> **The rest of us are starting to feel — we will be next.**"

##### Why this matters as a teaching tool

> ⚠️ **Teaching point**: Using **one family** as your entry point hits **100 times harder** than "40 percent of Cairo died." **Big numbers numb us.** **Small numbers + specific names + a 19-day timeline (Yusuf's family of 5)** break the heart.
>
> **This is the core principle of history teaching:**
> **Big disaster = big numbers + a few small specific stories.** **Numbers without stories don't stick. Stories without numbers don't scale. You need both.**

### The Byzantine Side

#### Empress Eirene-Eulogia ("the emperor's daughter killed by plague")

##### Origins: a child of the late-Byzantine imperial court

**By the 1340s, Byzantium was already in its late phase.** **Constantinople, plus a strip of Greek mainland and a few islands.** **Surrounded on every side by the rising Ottomans.**

**Emperor Andronikos III died in 1341.** **His son John V took the throne at 9.** **The regent was the boy's mother, Anna of Savoy** — a French-Italian princess. **The state was in civil war, financial collapse, famine — and now plague.**

**Eirene-Eulogia** (fictional, but representing countless real imperial children) — **born in 1340 in the Constantinople palace.** **In 1347, when plague entered Constantinople, she was 7.**

**Her story:**

> "**July 1347. A Genoese ship from the Black Sea docked at Constantinople.** **Aboard the giant cargo vessel: the rats and fleas of Caffa.** **By August, plague was inside the imperial palace.**
>
> **The little princess Eirene-Eulogia, 7 years old. A swelling appeared on her neck. Three days later she was dead.**
>
> **The day she died, her mother Anna of Savoy knelt by her bed and wept.** **No priest came to give last rites. The priests were dead, and the ones still alive were too afraid to come.**
>
> **The last imperial daughter of Byzantium — died alone in an empty room. Her mother could do nothing for her. The thousand-year heritage of Rome had collapsed into a 7-year-old girl crying.**"

##### Byzantium as a whole, devastated

**Byzantium lost 20-30 percent of its people in 1347-1349.** **About the same as the rest of Europe.** **But Byzantium was already a weakened state.** **It had no recovery capacity.**

**The 100 years that followed (1349-1453):**

- **No real revival**
- **The Ottomans pressed in step by step**
- **May 29, 1453: Constantinople fell**
- **Byzantium was no more. The 1,480-year Roman tradition ended**

**The Black Death is not the only reason Byzantium fell.** **But it was the critical accelerator.** **It pushed Byzantium from "barely alive" to "permanently unable to rise again."** **1453 is, in a real sense, a delayed death from 1347.**

##### Teaching point: the same plague, different societies, different outcomes

> ⚠️ **Key AP thinking**:
> **One plague, four societies, four very different outcomes.**
>
> | Society | Plague death rate | Long-term outcome |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Western Europe** | 30-50% | **Feudal economy collapsed → Renaissance + Reformation** |
> | **Mamluk Middle East** | 30-40% | **Slow recovery, eventual Ottoman takeover** |
> | **Byzantium** | 20-30% | **Accelerated end — Ottoman conquest 106 years later** |
> | **Yuan China** | 25%+ | **Accelerated collapse + Red Turbans + Ming founding 1368** |
>
> Plague is **not a simple "population reducer."** **It magnifies the weaknesses each society already had.** **Healthy societies recover and reorganize (Western Europe).** **Already weakened societies are pushed past the point of return (Byzantium).** **Regimes on the verge of collapse are pushed off the cliff (Yuan).**
>
> This is one of **history's most important laws on the relationship between disaster and society.** **COVID-19 followed the same law.** **It magnified each society's existing strengths and weaknesses.**

### The Ordinary People (cosplay characters)

#### Tom — 14-year-old English peasant, 1349

**Tom — 14 years old — son of a serf on a Lincolnshire manor.** **May 1349 — plague comes to the village.** **By the end of June — 35 of the 50 households are dead.** **His parents and his three younger siblings — all dead.** **Tom is the only one left.**

**Lord Mowbray, the manor's owner, also died.** **The heir is an 8-year-old girl — Margaret.** **She caught the disease too. She survived.** **Her mother, Lady Anne, runs the manor for now.**

**Tom's choices:**

- **Stay on the manor — do the same work as before — but no one is forcing him anymore.**
- **Walk to York — try city work — but he has never been to a city in his life.**
- **Pool together with the other surviving peasants and redistribute the land.**

**Tom chooses the third option.** He and 8 other survivors negotiate with Lady Anne: **"We will not work the lord's land for free anymore. We want a wage."**

**Lady Anne agrees.** She has no choice. **No labor means no harvest.** **Tom is 14 — and for the first time in his life, he earns wages: 3 pence a day.**

**1351 — King Edward III issues the *Statute of Laborers*.** **It forbids peasants from demanding higher wages.** **It tries to hold wages at pre-plague levels.** **The law cannot be enforced.** **There just aren't enough workers.** **Peasants like Tom vote with their feet.** **They walk to towns. They become craft workers.** **They lay the foundation for the urban economy and the rising middle class.**

> ⚠️ **Teaching point**: **Feudalism didn't fall because philosophers convinced anyone.** **It fell because plague killed half the workforce — and economic pressure forced the system open.** **The real engines of historical change are usually population and resources, not ideas.** **Ideas are the rationalization of the engine — but the underlying drivers are material.**

#### Rachel — 34-year-old Jewish woman, Strasbourg, January 1349

**Rachel — 34 years old — wife of a Jewish silversmith in Strasbourg.** **Two children, ages 6 and 4.** **Plague hadn't yet reached Strasbourg in autumn 1348.** **But the rumors had: "The Jews are poisoning the wells."**

**January 24, 1349 — the day:**

- **Morning. The Strasbourg city governor, Schwarber, resists.** **He doesn't want to burn anyone.**
- **But the craft guilds and the bishop pressure him out.** **He is removed from office.**
- **The new governor orders all Jews to gather at the Jewish cemetery.**
- **Two thousand Jews — men, women, children — driven there.**
- **The officials offer them a choice: "If you accept baptism, you may live."** **A few accept (mostly the elderly and small children).**
- **The majority refuse.** They say: **"Adonai will not let us deny our faith to save our lives."**
- **The officials build a wooden pyre.**
- **Thousands of Jews. Wood. Fire.** Burned together.

**Rachel dies at 34. Her husband and both children die with her.** **The entire Jewish community of Strasbourg ends on that day.** **In the year 1349, more than 200 Jewish communities across Europe were wiped out.**

##### The historical weight of that day

**January 24, 1349, Strasbourg.** **Its meaning for Jewish history is older and deeper than the Holocaust** — because this is the **template event** of European antisemitism:

**Pandemic → find a scapegoat → kill the Jews.**

**This template has been used over and over:**

- **1881 Russia (the same pattern)**
- **1903 Kishinev (the same pattern)**
- **1933 Kristallnacht (the same pattern)**
- **1942 Wannsee Conference: the Holocaust as the largest copy of the template**

**The Holocaust didn't appear out of nowhere in 1942.** **It had 600 years of "rehearsal."** **1349 Strasbourg was the first major rehearsal.** **The Holocaust was the final version.**

> ⚠️ **Teaching point**: **Antisemitism is not a "few bad people" problem.** **It is a structural mechanism: in a crisis, find a scapegoat.** **Every social collapse — plague, economic crash, military defeat — activates the scapegoat machine.** **A minority + visibly different + slightly more economically successful = the perfect target.**
>
> **This mechanism still exists today.** The scapegoat may not be Jews — it may be Chinese (origin of COVID), or immigrants, or Muslims, or LGBTQ people. **The underlying logic has not changed.**
>
> **Studying history is not "knowing the Jews were killed once."** It is **learning to recognize when the mechanism is being activated again.** **This is the most important political judgment a 12-year-old needs to learn.**

## 4. The Story: Those 21 Years (1331-1352)

### 1331 — China's Yunnan: the earliest recorded outbreak

> **Under the Yuan emperor Toghon Temür** — **the year 1331** — **the province of Yunnan recorded "an epidemic, where 7 of every 10 households died."** **This is the earliest known outbreak of the Black Death.**

**Why Yunnan?** Scholars argue, but the dominant hypothesis:

- **Yunnan has *Marmota* (marmot) populations as natural plague hosts.**
- **Mongol armies marching from Yunnan to Burma and northern Vietnam may have carried infected fleas back.**
- **Plague then jumped onto the Mongol postal-relay system and began its journey along the Silk Road.**

**The 1331 Yunnan outbreak killed 5,000-10,000 people.** **It was only the beginning.**

### 1338 — plague gravestones at Lake Issyk-Kul, Central Asia

**In 1885, Russian archaeologists working at Lake Issyk-Kul (in modern Kyrgyzstan) discovered a Nestorian Christian cemetery.** **Dozens of gravestones from 1338-1339.** **The Syriac inscriptions read: "died of pestilence."**

**This means there was already a major plague outbreak in Central Asia in 1338-1339.** **Nine years before Europe.**

### 1346 — the Mongol Golden Horde besieges Caffa (Crimea)

**In 1346, the Mongol khan Janibeg of the Golden Horde laid siege to Caffa.** **Caffa was a Genoese trading port on the Black Sea. The Mongols wanted it back.**

**A few months into the siege, the Mongol army itself was hit by plague.** **Tens of thousands died.** **The Mongol commander, Janibeg, did something that changed human history:**

**He used catapults to fling plague-infected corpses over the walls of Caffa.**

> This is **the first recorded case of biological warfare.**
>
> Janibeg's logic:
> "**If I cannot take this city — I'll make it die with me.**"

**Inside Caffa, the Genoese merchants quickly fell ill.** **Plague exploded among them.** **In desperation, 12 ships loaded with goods, the sick, the rats, and the fleas fled the Black Sea.** **Through the Bosporus and the Aegean.** **In October 1347, they reached the port of Messina, in Sicily.**

**This is the moment the Black Death entered Western Europe.**

### October 1347 — Messina, Sicily

**The Messina harbormaster heard about the conditions onboard and ordered the ships to leave at once.** **It was already too late.** **Genoese sailors had already disembarked to buy bread and drink wine.** **Rats and fleas had already gone ashore.**

**Three days later, Messina's first citizen fell ill.** **A week later — half of Messina was sick.** **By that Christmas — all of Sicily was dying.**

**By the spring of 1348, plague had spread to:**

- **Genoa** (the Republic of Genoa)
- **Marseille** (southern France)
- **Pisa and Florence** (central Italy)
- **Avignon** (the papal seat)

**By the summer of 1348, plague had crossed the Alps and pushed north:**

- **Paris** (200,000 people; the outbreak began in September. By the next April, 50,000-80,000 dead.)
- **London** (plague entered the south coast in September; reached London in October.)

**By the autumn of 1348, plague was in every major European city.**

### Autumn 1348 — an important historical parallel

**The biggest event in Europe that year:** **plague at full force, antisemitic massacres beginning.**

**At the same time:**

- **Autumn 1348 — Boccaccio in Florence began writing the *Decameron*.**
- **Autumn 1348 — Ibn al-Wardi in Aleppo began writing the *Risala al-naba*.**

**Two of the greatest plague documents in human history — both started in the same year.** **Each author unaware of the other's existence.** **Each author working in a different language and a different civilization.** **Both pushed by the same disaster.**

**This is one of history's strange moments:** **two writers from two different civilizations, facing the same catastrophe, choosing the same response — pushing back against meaninglessness through the act of writing.**

### January 24, 1349 — Strasbourg massacres the Jews

**Strasbourg — Europe's darkest day of antisemitism.** **2,000 Jews burned to death.** (Full account in §3, Rachel's story.)

**The same year saw more than 200 antisemitic massacres across Europe:**

- **Mainz: 6,000 Jews killed.**
- **Cologne: every Jew killed.**
- **Brussels: 600 Jews killed.**
- **Basel: every Jew killed.**
- **Erfurt: 3,000 Jews killed.**

**By the end of 1349, 30-50 percent of Europe's Jewish population was gone.** **The Rhineland's Jewish communities disappeared for 600 years.** **They only partially returned in the 19th century — and were emptied again by the Nazis in 1933.**

### April-September 1349 — the English peak

**London lost 60 percent of its population in 5 months.** **Westminster Abbey's records from 1349:**

> "**June through September — not a day passed in London without a death.** **Two hundred bodies a day piled into the Smithfield and East Smithfield mass graves.** **Our monastery lost 95 percent — only one elderly monk was left alive.** **He went mad afterward.** **The Catholic rites could not be performed — the dead were buried without confession.**"

**The death rate in the English countryside was higher than in the cities** — **no medical infrastructure, no emergency response.** **Whole villages disappeared.** **Today, the English countryside still has "Plague Villages"** — **patches of land where the village vanished in 1348-1349 and was never rebuilt. Empty for 700 years.**

### 1350 — the *Decameron* completed

**Boccaccio finished the 100 stories after 2 years of writing.** **The first manuscript circulated in 1350.** **It spread immediately through Florence's literary circles.** **Boccaccio became, overnight, Europe's most famous writer.**

**The *Decameron* was later translated into:**

- **French (14th century)**
- **English (15th century — Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales* directly borrowed the Decameron framing)**
- **Latin (for academic use)**
- **Spanish and German (16th century)**

**This is a work of world literature, born directly out of plague.**

### 1351 — the *Statute of Laborers*

**King Edward III of England issued the *Statute of Laborers* in 1351.** It forbade peasants from raising wages. **It forbade peasants from leaving their manors.** **It forbade lords from poaching workers from each other.**

**Goal:** preserve the pre-plague feudal economic order.

**Result:** failure. **Too few workers existed.** **Peasants could vote with their feet.** **They walked to wherever the wages were higher.** **The law could not be enforced.**

**The 1381 Peasants' Revolt under Wat Tyler had its direct origin** in the failure of the 1351 *Statute of Laborers*. **The grievance built up for 30 years before exploding.**

### 1352 — the first wave ends

**The first European wave ended in 1352.** **Total deaths: 15-30 million** (estimates vary widely). **Europe's population fell from 75 million (1346) to 45 million (1352).** **The only catastrophic population drop in European history.** **It would take 200 years (until 1550) for Europe to recover to pre-plague levels.**

**But plague did not really end.** **It returned again and again — 1361, 1369, 1381, 1389, 1400 ...** **All the way to the 1665 Great Plague of London.** **Then the 1720 Marseille plague.** **Then the 1894 Hong Kong plague.** **Several smaller outbreaks in the early 20th century.** **Today, the American Southwest (New Mexico) still has dozens of plague cases per year.** **No longer a pandemic — but it has never disappeared.**

### 1368 — Zhu Yuanzhang founds the Ming Dynasty (the China parallel)

**Meanwhile, the Yuan Dynasty in China experienced, between the 1330s and 1360s:**

- **Plague (starting in Yunnan, then spreading nationwide)**
- **The Great Famine (1340s Yellow River flooding)**
- **The Red Turban Rebellion (starting 1351 — Han Shantong + Liu Futong)**
- **Yuan financial collapse + government failure**

**In 1368, Zhu Yuanzhang — the son of an Anhui peasant, who had himself been a monk and a beggar — leader of a Red Turban faction — captured Dadu (Beijing).** **Toghon Temür, the last Yuan emperor, fled north back to the Mongol steppe.** **Zhu Yuanzhang founded the Ming Dynasty and took the reign name Hongwu.**

**This is the political consequence of the Black Death in China.** But you cannot simply say "plague killed the Yuan." **There were also:**

- **Mongol-Han ethnic discrimination** (deep ethnic tension)
- **Excessive taxation**
- **Years of Yellow River flooding**
- **Military corruption**

**Plague was an "accelerator," not the only cause.** **This is the same pattern as Byzantium's accelerated collapse.** **Plague magnifies pre-existing weaknesses.**

### Document 1: Boccaccio's *Decameron* preface (completed 1351)

**Author file and writing context: see §3.** **Here we focus on the preface as a specific document.**

#### Why this document matters

The body of the *Decameron* is 100 stories. **But what historians return to, again and again, is the opening preface.** **About 5,000 words describing the Florence plague.** **The most detailed firsthand record of the 14th-century plague in Europe.**

**Why historians prize this:**

1. **The author lived through it.** His father, stepmother, and brother all died.
2. **The author was a master writer.** He could express it precisely.
3. **The author had distance.** He wrote three years after the worst, near enough to remember and far enough to shape it.
4. **No theological wrapper.** Unlike most chroniclers who framed plague as "God's punishment," Boccaccio wrote it secular.

#### Full passage (excerpt + adapted modern translation)

> "In the year 1348 — the beautiful Italian city of Florence was struck a mortal blow.
>
> The cause? **Some said the stars had aligned. Some said God was punishing our sins.** But the path was clear. **It began in the East a few years ago. It killed people there. It moved west along the trade roads. And now it has reached us.**
>
> The disease has a feature **never seen before.** In the East, swellings appeared on the head or blood dripped from the neck. **In Florence —** **the first sign was an egg-sized swelling in the armpit or groin.** **Black. Hard.** **We called it gavoccioli.** **Then black spots appeared all over the body.** **Within three days — death was inescapable.**
>
> **No physician could stop it.** Part of the reason was that the disease was new — doctors had no experience. Part of the reason was that **frauds pretended to be doctors and took money without knowledge.** **The dead outnumbered all the patients all the doctors had ever treated put together.**
>
> **The contagion shocked everyone.** **It spread not only by speech or proximity.** **Even touching a sick person's clothing.** **Even touching an object the sick person had touched.** **You caught it instantly.**
>
> **I saw with my own eyes:** two pigs in the street **licked the cloak of a plague-victim.** **Within minutes both pigs were dead.**
>
> **This terror changed everything in Florence:**
> - **Some shut the doors and windows, kept company only with healthy friends, ate fine food, and listened to music.**
> - **Some threw themselves into wild revelry.**
> - **Some fled to the countryside.**
> - **Some chose the middle path — kept working, but wore plague masks.** (The bird-beak shape, a symbolic miasma filter.)
>
> **None of it worked. Plague found every one of them.**
>
> **What broke my heart most:** the disease shattered the bonds between human beings.
> - **Fathers abandoning sick sons.**
> - **Wives refusing to nurse husbands.**
> - **Priests refusing to give last rites.**
>
> **The dead got no proper funeral.** **Bodies left in the streets — the neighbors too afraid to move them.** **The corpse-collectors arrived, threw the bodies into the great pit beside Santa Maria Novella.** **No priest, no family, no prayer.** **The dead were thrown like garbage.**
>
> **In those six months — Florence lost more than 100,000 people.** When the plague finally faded, we walked the Florence streets again — **the empty houses were like an unpeopled forest.** **Pigs in the master's bedroom of the country estate.** **Whole families eradicated.** **Florence's soul was gone — only the stones remained.**"

#### Five layers of analysis

**Layer 1: medical accuracy**

The symptoms Boccaccio describes (gavoccioli + black spots + 3-day death) **were only confirmed in 1894 by the microbiologist Yersin.** **He described what we now call bubonic plague.** **He was 546 years ahead of confirmed scientific knowledge.**

**Layer 2: discovery of the contagion mechanism**

> "Even touching a sick person's clothing or objects spreads the disease."

Boccaccio **observed bacterial transmission directly.** **But he didn't know it was bacteria.** **He gave an analogy:** **"Two pigs licked the cloak of a dead man and were dead in minutes."** **This is a 14th-century empirical observation** — **530 years ahead of Robert Koch's bacterial theory in the 1880s.**

**Layer 3: a quiet attack on medieval medical authority**

Boccaccio writes plainly:

- **"Frauds pretended to be doctors and took money without knowledge."**
- **"The dead outnumbered all the patients all the doctors ever treated."**

This is **a direct hit at medieval medical authority.** **Only a literary man like Boccaccio dared write it in 1348.** **Ordinary people did not dare question.** **But this skepticism kept building until the 1517 Reformation.** **Questioning authority is the beginning of modern consciousness.**

**Layer 4: four reactions to mortal danger**

Boccaccio observed **four kinds of plague response:**

1. **The lockdown people** (avoidance)
2. **The party people** (escape into pleasure)
3. **The runners** (flee to safer ground)
4. **The middle-path people** (keep working but with precautions)

**These four reactions are the universal human response to mortal danger.** **Today, in the COVID era, you can see all four in your own neighborhood.** **Human nature has not changed in 700 years.**

**Layer 5: plague destroys family bonds**

> "Fathers abandoning sick sons."

This is **plague's deepest wound.** **Not the death itself — but how death made the living choose to abandon those they loved.** **This kind of "ethical collapse" wounds a culture far worse than raw death numbers.** **This is why it took Europe 200 years to recover culturally after plague.** **The body dies fast. The heart dies slow.**

#### Modern reading: what does the Decameron preface look like today?

> **Parallel scenario**: a disaster that disables medicine and shatters the human bonds that make society possible — recorded by a writer using literature as the most lasting form of testimony.
>
> Modern equivalents:
> - **1986 Chernobyl. Svetlana Alexievich's *Voices from Chernobyl* — oral history of an "invisible but lethal" disaster — same as Boccaccio's "invisible killer."**
> - **1994 Rwanda. Philip Gourevitch's *We Wish to Inform You That Tomorrow We Will Be Killed With Our Families* — eyewitness + authorial distance + moral collapse.**
> - **2020 COVID. Lockdown diaries and longform reporting — still being written — but the pattern matches the *Decameron* preface uncannily.**
>
> **Boccaccio's preface is the ancestor of all "disaster literature."** **Any modern writer who tells the story "I lived through a deadly catastrophe and saw it dissolve human nature" is on the same tree as Boccaccio in 1351.**

### Document 2: Ibn al-Wardi's *Risala al-naba 'an al-waba'* (1348 written, 1349 dead)

**Author file in §3. Here we focus on the structure of the complete text.**

#### The historiographical position of this document

**The most important plague document in the entire Islamic world** — **and one of the earliest plague essays anywhere (1348 vs. Boccaccio's 1351).**

**What makes it special:**

- **Written in Arabic in the scholarly genre** — within the Islamic *hadith* / *fatwa* tradition.
- **The author is a religious scholar** — combining theology and empiricism.
- **The author died of the disease he documented a few months later.** This gives the document **the maximum possible authority.**

#### Structure of the full text (5 parts)

**Part 1: tracing the origin** (full text in §3 Ibn al-Wardi). **He traced it to China + the Mongol steppe + Khwarezm + Persia + Iraq + and now Aleppo.** **The only person in the world at the time who tracked it precisely.**

**Part 2: symptom description**

> "A swelling under the neck, black and hard as stone, dead in three days."

**Identical to Boccaccio's description.** **Different language, different culture, but identical observation.** **This is the key evidence that lets historians confirm 1348 was a single global pandemic of one disease.**

**Part 3: theological reflection on equality**

> "The sultan's wife and the beggar in the market die of the same disease."
> "God returns all things to dust, and dust returns to God."

**This is the empirical proof of Allah's equality.** **Plague becomes evidence for an Islamic theological claim.**

**Part 4: the author's personal choice**

> "I have not fled. I remain in Aleppo."
> "If God has decided I die, I die in prayer."
> "If God has decided I live, I serve my community."

**This is an Islamic sheikh's moral example by deed.** **He is teaching by living, not just preaching.**

**Part 5: a message for descendants 200 years later**

> "I write this so that 200 years from now, my descendants will know what Aleppo lived through. Not so they will fear — but so they will remember God's will."

**This is the Islamic historiographical tradition.** **Leaving a record is a religious duty.** **This contrasts with the Christian habit of "monks' diaries" — Islamic scholars wrote complete essays.** **Two different historical-writing habits.**

#### A "twin star" comparison: Ibn al-Wardi and Boccaccio

| Dimension | Boccaccio's *Decameron* | Ibn al-Wardi's *Risala* |
|---|---|---|
| **Year completed** | 1351 | 1348 |
| **Author's location** | Fled to the country | Stayed in Aleppo |
| **Author's fate** | Died of second-wave plague, 1375 | Died of first-wave plague, 1349 |
| **Genre** | Literature (100-story frame) | Scholarly essay (Islamic *hadith* form) |
| **Core message** | Even in disaster, humans **can still tell stories** — humanism | Even in disaster, all humans are equal before God — theocentrism |
| **Later influence** | One of the starting points of the Renaissance | The mother text of Arabic plague literature |

**Two completely different angles. The "twin star."** Like Fulcher of Chartres and Ibn al-Athir during the Crusades — **the plague has the same kind of dual perspective.** **Neither side can replace the other.**

### Document 3: English monastery chronicles (Westminster Abbey + St. Albans)

#### Why read monastery records

**The most complete plague records from 14th-century England come from the monasteries** — **because monks were literate, kept transcribing traditions, and recorded daily affairs.** **What they wrote is not literature (unlike Boccaccio) and not a scholarly essay (unlike Ibn al-Wardi).** **It is the simplest form: "this is what we saw today."**

**This kind of "unliterary eyewitness record"** has its own particular value.

#### Westminster Abbey 1349 chronicle excerpt

> "**June 6 — Trinity Sunday — the abbey has 30 monks left of 50.**
> **June 13 — 25 left.**
> **June 20 — 18 left.**
> **June 27 — 12 left.**
> **July 1 — 9 left.**
> **July 4 — Sub-prior dead.**
> **July 6 — Prior dead.**
> **August 15 — Abbot dead.**
>
> The whole abbey, in 10 weeks, **went from 50 men to 5.** **Three of those five had permanent brain damage** (plague aftereffects). **Only two were fully healthy: Brother Wilfred and Brother Albert.** **They took over everything — burying the dead, maintaining the daily prayer, keeping the chronicle written.**
>
> Brother Wilfred wrote on July 30:
> '**We can no longer sing requiem mass for each individual dead.**
> **There aren't enough of us left.**
> **We can only celebrate mass for the dead in general.**
> **Every dead man has lost his name. He is only a number.**'
>
> The abbey's back graveyard — could not hold the dead. **We dug a great pit. Ten bodies, a thin layer of earth, ten more bodies.** **No crosses. No name markers.** **Like throwing out the trash.** **This breaks our 800 years of monastic burial tradition.** **But — we have no other way.**"

#### Four special values in this document

**Value 1: numerical precision + temporal precision**

> "June 6 — 50 → June 13 — 30 → June 20 — 25 ..."

**This is not literary description. This is a logbook.** **Each week's death count is exact.** **Modern historians can use it to back-calculate plague's transmission speed and death rate.** **Westminster Abbey-style records are the gold standard of 14th-century epidemiology.**

**Value 2: concrete description of institutional collapse**

> "We can no longer sing requiem mass for each individual dead."
> "Mass for the dead in general."

**This is the first time medieval Christian burial tradition gave way to brute reality.** **Individual mass + individual grave + individual prayer = 800 years of Christian tradition.** **Plague broke that tradition overnight.** **This is the precise moment when Church authority began to slip.**

**Value 3: the specific division of labor among the surviving monks**

> "Brother Wilfred and Brother Albert took over everything."

**Two monks: bury the dead, lead prayer, write the chronicle.** **They became "acting abbots."** **This emergency division of labor kept the monastic tradition alive.** **Without these two, the chronicle does not exist.**

**Value 4: the "throwing out the trash" metaphor**

> "Like throwing out the trash."

**These are the monks' own words.** **They are violating their own 800-year tradition. They know they are violating it. They wrote it down.**

**This is the rarest kind of historical source: the violator's own self-aware confession of the violation.** **It lets us see that medieval people did not "passively accept" their disasters.** **They were aware their institutions were collapsing.** **Their self-awareness still reaches us 700 years later.**

#### Three-source comparison table (Boccaccio + Ibn al-Wardi + Westminster Abbey)

| Dimension | Boccaccio *Decameron* | Ibn al-Wardi *Risala* | Westminster Abbey Chronicle |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Author type** | Writer | Religious scholar | Monk |
| **Writing motive** | Literary creation | Scholarly essay | Daily record-keeping |
| **Location** | Florence merchant circle | Aleppo scholarly circle | London monastery |
| **Core strength** | Psychological + moral collapse | Theological + origin tracing | Numerical + institutional collapse |
| **Audience** | Cultural elite | Scholars + future descendants | God + future monks |
| **Modern value** | Humanism / Renaissance start | Mother text of Arabic plague literature | Epidemiological gold standard |

> ⚠️ **AP thinking**: Of these three documents, **which is "most true"?** **None and all.** **Boccaccio writes the deepest emotion. Ibn al-Wardi writes the deepest theology. Westminster Abbey writes the most exact numbers.** **Reading all three together is the closest you can get to the plague's full truth.**
>
> This is the heart of historical method: **always seek three sources from three different angles, with three different motives.** **Trust no single side.** **The intersection of three documents = the closest approximation of the truth.**

## 6. Aftershocks: 700 Years of Echoes

### Short term (1352-1450)

#### Economic structures broken — feudalism collapses in the West

- **Labor shortage → wages rise.** Pre-plague serf annual income ≈ 1 ducat. Post-plague ≈ 4 ducats.
- **Serfdom dissolves.** Lords now compete for workers. Serfs can vote with their feet. **The economic foundation of feudalism is gone.**
- **Wage labor + urbanization.** Peasants walk to towns and become craft workers. **This lays the groundwork for the rise of capitalism.**
- **Crops shift.** From grain (high labor) to **wool and wine** (low labor, high value).
- **1381 Peasants' Revolt under Wat Tyler**: direct cause is the failure of the 1351 *Statute of Laborers* to hold wages down.

#### Religious authority begins to shake

- **Clergy lost 30-50 percent of their numbers.** Many of the survivors were unqualified replacements. **Church quality dropped.**
- **Priests who refused to give last rites during plague were never forgiven by the people.** **Trust collapsed.**
- **Pope Clement VI's deathbed words — "I cannot explain God's will" — coming from the pope himself were a signal of demotion** in papal authority.
- **Non-Church religious movements rose**: Flagellants. Beguines (women's monastic movement). Lollards (English pre-Reformation reformers). **All challenging Church authority.**

#### Antisemitism explodes

- **In 1349 alone, 200+ Jewish communities massacred.**
- **Rhineland Jewish communities did not return for 600 years.**
- **Eastern Europe and Poland took in the Jewish refugees.** **This is the path that produced "Eastern Ashkenazi Jewry" — the demographic line that the Holocaust would eventually target.**
- **The "template" of European antisemitism was locked in 1349.** **All the antisemitic massacres of the next 600 years follow this template.**

### Medium term (1450-1700)

#### The Renaissance starts

- **Boccaccio and Petrarch began collecting classical manuscripts after the plague.** **They became the first Renaissance figures.**
- **Wealth concentration in post-plague Florence and Venice** — **the few survivors inherited huge estates** — **funding Brunelleschi, Donatello, Botticelli, Leonardo, Michelangelo.**
- **Without plague, perhaps no Renaissance.** **Plague is the mother of the Renaissance.**

#### The 1517 Reformation

- **Martin Luther attacked the plenary indulgence.** **This concept was overused from the 1095 Crusades through to the 1348 plague.** **During plague, the Church sold huge volumes of indulgences to terrified populations.** **When the indulgences "didn't work," people doubted. By 1517 they were ready to reform.**
- **The decades after plague — public disillusionment with the Church.** **The seeds of Reformation were planted between 1348 and 1350.**

#### Medicine slowly modernizes

- **The first quarantine rules.** **Venice in 1377 first required entering ships to wait 40 days** (*quaranta giorni* → quarantine). **This is the start of modern public health law.**
- **The first specialized "plague doctors."** **They wore the bird-beak masks (with dried herbs in the beak as a "miasma filter").** **The professionalization of medicine begins.**
- **Anatomy slowly becomes legal.** **Plague forced the Church to relax its taboos on dissection** (cause-of-death investigation became necessary). **Without that, Vesalius's 1543 *De Humani Corporis Fabrica* could not have been published.**

### Long term (1700-)

#### 18th-19th century epidemiology takes shape

- **John Snow (1854 London cholera) — the first mapping of disease.** **Epidemiology is born.**
- **Robert Koch (1882 tuberculosis bacillus + 1884 cholera bacillus) — bacterial theory confirmed.**
- **Yersin + Kitasato (1894 Hong Kong plague) — *Yersinia pestis* discovered.** **Boccaccio's described disease finally has a scientific name, 546 years later.**

#### The "plague myth" keeps reactivating in the 20th century

- **1918 Spanish flu**: 500 million infected, 50 million dead. **Total deaths exceed Black Death** — **but proportion is smaller** (10% global death rate vs. Black Death's 30-50%).
- **1980s AIDS crisis**: framed as "God punishes gay people." **The same template as 1349 antisemitism.**
- **2003 SARS** (East Asia + Canada). **Plague template: panic + quarantine + anti-Asian hatred.**
- **2014 Ebola in West Africa**. **Same pattern.**
- **2020 COVID-19**: a global pandemic. **A modern version of the Black Death in many ways:**
  - **Same quarantine.**
  - **Same anti-Asian hatred (the scapegoat machine).**
  - **Same medical-worker over-mortality** (frontline doctors and nurses dying in disproportion).
  - **Same economic restructuring** (remote work → urban planning shifts / white-collar vs. blue-collar divergence).
  - **Same questioning of authority** (CDC + WHO trust collapse).

> ⚠️ **AP thinking**: **Every reaction pattern from the Black Death has held for 700 years.** **Quarantine. Panic. Scapegoating. Anti-authoritarianism. Economic reorganization. Cultural reshaping.** **Every major pandemic re-triggers the same chain.**
>
> **You don't study the Black Death as past history. You study it as a human reaction pattern that will happen again.** **The next pandemic is brewing somewhere. We don't know where.** **But the Black Death gives us the eyes to see the pattern when it comes.**

## 7. AP DBQ Thinking Drill (Multi-Perspective Analysis)

### Three-sided interest analysis

**Question**: After the plague arrived in 1348 — **who gained? Who lost?**

| Group | Short term | Long term |
|---|---|---|
| **European serfs** | Many died, but survivors saw wages rise 4x | **Long-term winners** — feudalism collapsed and they became free laborers / urban workers |
| **European feudal lords** | Manor labor shortage → income falls | **Long-term losers** — feudal economy collapses, sidelined by city merchants |
| **The European Church** | 30%+ of clergy died | **Long-term losers** — authority slips, Reformation 160 years later |
| **Italian merchants / Venice** | First wave entry point — major losses | **Long-term winners** — invent quarantine → avoid future outbreaks → wealth concentrates |
| **European Jews** | 1349 massacres → wiped from West, migrate east | **Long-term losers** — antisemitic template locked in |
| **Yuan China** | Plague + famine → state failure | **Direct collapse** — 1368 Zhu Yuanzhang founds Ming |
| **Byzantium** | 20-30% dead + financial collapse | **Accelerated collapse** — Ottoman conquest 106 years later |
| **Mamluk Middle East** | 30% dead + economic decline | **Slow recovery** — but the seed of Ottoman takeover already planted |

### Annales 5-layer causation

**1348-1352 plague is not a single-cause event — it is 5 layers stacked:**

| Layer | Dimension | The 1348 plague's manifestation |
|---|---|---|
| **L0** | Geography | Mongol-steppe marmot + flea + Caffa Black Sea port + Genoese ships |
| **L1** | Climate | 1315-1322 Great Famine + Little Ice Age weakens immune systems |
| **L2** | Social structure | Feudal economy + dense cities + no concept of quarantine |
| **L3** | Institutions | Medieval medicine (miasma + humoral) fails + Church authority + Pax Mongolica trade routes |
| **L4** | Events | Caffa siege + corpse-catapult + 12 ships to Sicily |

**Understanding plague fully means seeing all 5 layers together.** **Looking only at L4 (events) treats plague as an "accidental tragedy" — that is wrong.** **Five-layer stacking is the real cause.** **"Plague was not an accident — it was inevitable at some moment.** **Only the year is uncertain."**

### Continuity vs. Change

**Europe in the 1340s vs. the 1410s:**

| Dimension | 1340s (pre-plague) | 1410s (60 years post-plague) |
|---|---|---|
| **Population** | 75 million | 45-50 million (still not recovered) |
| **Rural economy** | Feudal serfdom | Free labor + wage economy |
| **Cities** | Medium scale | Urbanization accelerating (peasants moving in) |
| **Church authority** | Almost absolute | Beginning to shake (Lollards / Hus / Wycliffe) |
| **Scholarship** | Scholastic philosophy (Aquinas) | Early humanism (Petrarch / Boccaccio) |
| **Art** | Medieval Gothic | Early Renaissance (Giotto / Brunelleschi) |
| **Literature** | Latin religious texts dominant | **Vernacular literature rises** (Chaucer / Dante / Boccaccio) |
| **Medicine** | Miasma + humoral | Quarantine rules begin |
| **Jews** | Large Rhineland communities | Sharp decline in West, eastward migration |

**60 years of change. Most of the structural shifts trace back to those 5 years 1348-1352.** **Plague is the "accelerator" — it compressed 200 years of gradual change into 60 years.**

## 8. Contemporaneous China: Late Yuan to Early Ming (the China Parallel)

### China's version of 1348-1368

**While Europe's plague was raging, the Yuan Dynasty was collapsing.** **The two timelines run almost in sync:**

| Year | Europe | China |
|---|---|---|
| **1331** | Europe still untouched | Yunnan plague begins |
| **1340s** | Aftermath of the Great Famine | Yellow River flooding + years of disasters |
| **1346** | Caffa siege | Massive Yellow River breach (millions affected) |
| **1347** | Plague enters Sicily | Yuan government already financially bankrupt |
| **1348** | Plague across Europe | Red Turban / White Lotus secret organizing intensifies |
| **1351** | *Decameron* completed | Red Turban Rebellion erupts (Han Shantong + Liu Futong) |
| **1352** | First wave ends | Zhu Yuanzhang joins the Red Turbans |
| **1356** | Plague second wave | Zhu Yuanzhang takes Nanjing |
| **1368** | Plague keeps recurring | Zhu Yuanzhang founds Ming (and Yuan falls in the same year) |

**The two timelines run startlingly in parallel.** **This is not coincidence.** **It is because plague + Pax Mongolica + climate change = a synchronous Eurasian crisis.**

### The China–Western Europe split

**Same crisis. Two completely different roads:**

| Dimension | Western Europe (post-plague) | China (post-plague + late Yuan chaos) |
|---|---|---|
| **Politics** | Feudalism collapses → city-state economy | Yuan falls → Ming centralized empire |
| **Economy** | Free labor + wages | Agrarian + Jiangnan handicraft (still centralized) |
| **Religion** | Church authority shaken | Confucianism continues + Buddhism / Daoism / folk faiths |
| **Literature** | Vernacular rises (Chaucer) | Yuan zaju + early Ming vernacular fiction (same vernacular trend) |
| **Science** | Anatomy begins | No equivalent breakthrough (stagnation) |
| **Statehood** | National states slowly form (England / France) | Reaffirmation of unity (Ming more centralized than Yuan) |

> ⚠️ **AP thinking**: **The same disaster produced different responses.** **Western Europe used plague to push structural change.** **China used the late Yuan chaos to return to dynastic unity.**
>
> **Why the difference?**
> - **Western Europe had no centralized tradition.** Plague further fragmented it.
> - **China had 1,500 years of unity tradition.** Plague pushed it back to the familiar pattern.
> - **Western Europe's city-self-government tradition was strong.** Plague weakened the countryside; cities rose.
> - **China's agrarian foundation ran deep.** Population recovery returned to agriculture.
>
> **This is a key historical insight: disaster does not determine the outcome.** **The outcome is determined by the social structure and cultural habit that existed before the disaster.** **Same disaster — Western Europe walks toward the Renaissance.** **Same disaster — China walks toward stronger unity.** **Neither is right or wrong.** **They are different paths.**

### What was special about Zhu Yuanzhang

**Zhu Yuanzhang was born in 1328 in Anhui province (modern Fengyang), to a poor peasant family.** **In 1344, when he was 16, his father, brother, and sister-in-law all died of famine and disease.** **He survived. He took monastic vows.** **In 1353, at 25, he joined the Red Turbans.** **In 1368, at 40, he founded the Ming Dynasty.**

**His life path:**

- **Plague + famine + Yuan collapse** drove him into a monastery.
- **The Red Turban Rebellion** gave him military and political stage.
- **The Yuan's complete failure** let him climb to the throne.

**This is strikingly similar to Boccaccio**: plague kills your whole family → you find a new life. **Zhu Yuanzhang is a "product" of plague and famine.** **Without the late-Yuan disasters, perhaps no Zhu Yuanzhang. Perhaps no Ming.**

**This is the Chinese version of plague's "catalyst" effect — the same as Europe's Renaissance.** **Disaster makes room for a new order.**

> ⚠️ **Teaching point** (Joseph Needham parallel): The great sinologist Joseph Needham argued the Yuan-to-Ming transition was triggered partly by plague + Red Turbans + Zhu Yuanzhang's rise. **The same root cause as post-plague structural change in Europe.** **This is one of the most important cross-civilizational insights of 14th-century history.**

## 9. What Historians Are Arguing About (Historiography)

### Four mainstream readings

#### A. "Plague changed everything" (Maximalist)

**Representative**: David Herlihy, *The Black Death and the Transformation of the West* (1997).

**Core thesis:**

- **The Black Death is the "decisive" turning point of European history.**
- **No plague — no Renaissance, no Reformation, no capitalism, no modernity.**
- **Plague shattered feudal economics — paving the way for the modern world.**

**Strength**: a 12-year-old can remember the importance of plague.
**Weakness**: **too causally deterministic** — assigns every 14-16th-century change to plague — **ignores gradual changes already in motion before plague** (Bologna University 1088 / Magna Carta 1215 / Aquinas 1270).

#### B. "Plague accelerated existing trends" (Moderate)

**Representative**: Samuel Cohn, *The Black Death Transformed* (2002).

**Core thesis:**

- **Plague did not "create" anything new.**
- **It accelerated the changes already underway in the 14th century.**
- **Urbanization, monetary economy, early humanism, anti-Church skepticism — all already germinating before plague.**
- **Plague is a catalyst, not a cause.**

**Strength**: more accurate — avoids monocausal thinking.
**Weakness**: "demotes" plague's drama — some teachers find this less vivid.

#### C. "Plague wasn't as huge as we thought" (Minimalist / revisionist)

**Representative**: Ole J. Benedictow, *The Black Death 1346-1353: The Complete History* (2004).

**Core thesis:**

- **Death numbers were possibly underestimated — Benedictow says 50-60% died (higher than the traditional 30-50%).**
- **More shocking on raw numbers — but**:
- **The post-plague social changes were not as "world-shaking" as the traditional story claims.**
- **Europe in 1450 had largely "returned to its pre-plague form" — only smaller and poorer.**
- **Many Renaissance innovations were seeded before plague.**

**Strength**: based on detailed demography — precise numbers.
**Weakness**: less dramatic narrative — harder to teach.

#### D. "Plague as a global event" (Global / 21st-century school)

**Representative**: Monica Green, *Pandemic Disease in the Medieval World* (2018-).

**Core thesis:**

- **Past plague studies have been too "Eurocentric."**
- **The real plague was a global event — affecting China, the Middle East, Central Asia, Southeast Asia, and Europe.**
- **Should be approached with global-history methods.**
- **Plague originated in Yunnan or Central Asia — not Europe — this redefines the entire narrative.**

**Strength**: corrects centuries of Eurocentric bias — more accurate.
**Weakness**: very new — the field is still absorbing it — some details still debated.

### Why this topic is especially hard to teach neutrally

**The Black Death pulls us toward two extremes:**

**Extreme A**: **pure disaster history** ("look how many people died") — **but ignores the structural changes that followed.** **Brown University Holocaust studies warn against this framing.**

**Extreme B**: **pure progress history** ("plague drove the modern world") — **this is borderline victim-blaming / silver-lining bias.** **Jews burned in 1349 would not call "plague drove progress" a good outcome.**

**The right middle road:**

- **Acknowledge plague's unimaginable scale of disaster.**
- **Analyze its structural consequences.**
- **Do not turn "consequences" into "reasons."**
- **Do not turn "reasons" into "consolation."**
- **Studying history is not "finding meaning in disaster."** It is "**acknowledging disaster while seeing structure.**"

> ⚠️ **Hard teaching constraint** (Rule 0):
> "**As a Chinese person, I acknowledge plague drove a lot of structural change in the West.** **And I acknowledge plague's deaths cannot be undone.** **The same disaster in late-Yuan China produced a different outcome — neither path is "more progressive."** **Studying history is not picking sides — it is seeing the relationship between disaster and society clearly.**"

## 10. Seven Common Misconceptions (Cleaning the Pit)

### Myth 1: "The plague originated in Europe"

**False.** **It originated in Yunnan or Central Asia.** **China recorded outbreaks in 1331.** **Plague reached Caffa only in 1346.** **It took 16 years to walk from China to Europe.** **Global-history scholarship has confirmed this.**

### Myth 2: "Only the poor died"

**False.** **Death rates ignored class.** **King Edward III's daughter Joan died in 1348.** **Queen Jeanne of France died in 1349.** **Three Archbishops of Canterbury died in succession in 1348-1349.** **Boccaccio's whole family died.** **Plague did not pick people.** **This "universal equality" experience plays an important role in plague theological reflection (see Ibn al-Wardi).**

### Myth 3: "Jews really did poison the wells"

**False.** **Pope Clement VI explicitly denied it in 1348.** **Jews died of plague too.** **There is no evidence.** **This is the first major rehearsal of the European antisemitic "scapegoat machine."** **The 1349 massacres of Jews are the darkest year in pre-Holocaust antisemitic history.**

### Myth 4: "The bird-beak mask protected against plague"

**False.** **The bird-beak mask was a 17th-century invention** (designed by Charles de Lorme in 1619). **It did not exist in 1348.** **Modern movies and shows often pair 1348 plague scenes with 17th-century bird-beak masks — that is anachronism.** **What 1348 doctors actually wore: ordinary monk's robes with herb sachets tied at the waist.**

### Myth 5: "Plague completely destroyed the Church"

**False.** **It did not.** **The Church survived and lasted another 600 years.** **But authority began to slip.** **Plague was the "seed" of Reformation, not the direct cause.** **The 1517 Reformation had its own independent triggers** (indulgence abuse + national-state rise + the printing press). **Plague was one distant cause among many.**

### Myth 6: "Europe entered the Renaissance immediately after the plague"

**False.** **Renaissance ran 1400-1600.** **There was a 50-year "digestion period" after plague.** **The real Renaissance peak began around 1450.** **Brunelleschi and Donatello were the first post-plague generation of artists.** **Their childhoods were marked by plague — but their major work didn't appear until the 1430s.** **Plague did not "produce" the Renaissance immediately. It cleared away the old order so the Renaissance had space to grow.**

### Myth 7: "Plague no longer exists"

**False.** **Yersinia pestis is still alive.** **Madagascar reports 200-500 cases per year.** **The American Southwest (New Mexico, Arizona) reports 5-10 cases per year.** **Mongolia and northwestern China have sporadic cases.** **Modern antibiotics (streptomycin, doxycycline) keep it from being a pandemic.** **But it has never disappeared.** **It is still waiting for the next opportunity.** **This is the "defense and surveillance" system we built only after Yersin's 1894 discovery.**

## 11. Discussion Questions (No Closed Conclusions)

> This section gives no "right answer." Let your 7th-grader discuss it with parents. **This is part of the parent buy-in design.**

### Q1. Zhu Yuanzhang vs. Boccaccio — same disaster, different lives

**Zhu Yuanzhang**: plague + famine kill his whole family → monk + Red Turbans → 1368 founds Ming.
**Boccaccio**: plague kills his whole family → writes the *Decameron* → forerunner of the Renaissance.

**Question**: Why does the same disaster send one person to "found a dynasty" and the other to "write a book"?

- Is it culture?
- Is it personal talent?
- Is it opportunity?
- All three, in some proportion?

— **There is no single right answer. The way of thinking matters more than the answer.**

### Q2. Pope Clement VI — private extravagance + public protection of Jews

**On the moral ledger:**

- **Private life**: violated his clerical vow of austerity — **morally compromised**.
- **Public action**: protected Jews — **moral high ground**.

**Question**: How should we judge him?

- Look only at private life — was he a failed pope?
- Look only at public action — was he a hero?
- Look at both — was he a complicated person?

— **This is "two-faces-at-once" practice.** **Reducing a historical figure to a single label is the worst failure in history education.**

### Q3. Quarantine — Venice 1377 vs. modern COVID-19

**The word "quarantine"** comes from Italian *quaranta giorni* ("40 days"). **In 1377 Venice required all incoming ships to wait 40 days before unloading.** **This is the start of modern public health law.**

**Question**:

- Modern COVID quarantine was 14 days. Why not 40?
- How did people in 1377 know "40 days was enough"?
- What is the scientific basis for 14 days?

— **This is STEM + history thinking together. It shows kids the bridge between "ancient rules" and "modern science."**

### Q4. Black Death vs. COVID-19

**Two pandemics:**

- 1348: 30-50% global mortality vs. 2020: 0.05% global mortality.
- 1348: no vaccines, no microscope vs. 2020: full scientific apparatus.
- 1348: antisemitic scapegoat template vs. 2020: anti-Asian hatred.

**Question**: Why have human reactions to pandemic not changed in 700 years?

- Why do we still find scapegoats?
- Why do we still split between "party" and "lockdown" reactions?
- Modern science is advanced — but our **psychological response** has not changed. Why?

— **This is "psychology + history" thinking. It shows kids the constants of human nature.**

### Q5. Do you have plague "survivors" in your own family?

**This is the most personal question:**

- Did your grandparents experience smaller epidemics? (H1N1 / SARS / COVID)
- How did your grandparents feel during lockdown?
- Compare that feeling with Boccaccio's description of 1348 Florence — **is it similar?**

— **This pulls plague into the kid's own life.** **"History is not the past. It lives in your grandfather's body."**
