# The Meiji Restoration 1868 — Narrative Kernel (English Parallel)

> This file is the factual ground for the storyboard. It follows the 12-section + 4-appendix structure in AUTHORING_PIPELINE.md §4. EN side is written for Lexile 800-1000, ESL-friendly: short sentences, glossed terms.
> Rule 0 neutrality: top-down modernization built a state that kept its independence, and it also crushed countless ordinary people who lived inside the old order. Both are true at once. Japan is not "the good student who learned the lesson," and China is not "the slow student who failed." Both were making choices inside their own real limits.
> Cultural-fit rule: use Japan's own real terms (Emperor / Tennō, bakufu = shogunate, han = domain, daimyō = domain lord, samurai = warrior, shōgun). Do NOT translate them into Chinese concepts. The Emperor of Japan is not the same as a Chinese emperor.

## 0. Textbook Anchor

- **AP World History Modern · Unit 6 (Consequences of Industrialization, 1750-1900)** — Topics 6.2 and 6.4. The Meiji Restoration is one of AP World's main examples of a non-Western country responding to Western industrial power. Key point: Japan was one of the very few non-Western countries that was not colonized, and instead became an industrial power itself.
- **HS World History / CA HSS Grade 10** — Industrialization and empire; East Asia's different responses to the West.
- **Why this matters for Chinese-heritage students:** In China, students learn the Self-Strengthening Movement (the Qing reform effort). They already know terms like "Chinese learning as the base, Western learning for use" and "rich country, strong army." This lesson tells the same East Asian race from the Japanese side. Same Western shock, same slogan, two very different paths. The Japan-vs-China comparison (§9) is where these students have a real advantage.

## 1. The Hook (multiple viewpoints side by side)

In 1853, four American warships, black with coal smoke, pushed into the bay near Japan's capital. They forced Japan to open its doors. Fifteen years later, a group of low-ranking samurai in their twenties and thirties overthrew the shogunate that had ruled Japan for over 200 years. They put a teenage Emperor at the center. Then they did something the world rarely sees: **over about ten years, they tore down the very system that had made them samurai.**

The same event looks completely different from different positions:

- **A low-ranking samurai** climbs into the new government. He picks up a pen and signs laws that erase the "han" (the domains) and erase "samurai" as a class — using the system to kill the world that made him.
- **Saigō Takamori, the greatest hero of the Restoration,** helps build this new state. Then, in 1877, he leads the old samurai — the ones the new state left behind — in a rebellion against it. He loses, and he dies. The man who built the nation dies at the hands of the nation he built.
- **A farm family's daughter** in a country village makes no decisions at all. But she has to pay a new kind of tax she has never paid before. She watches her brother get drafted from a list. She watches the old certainties disappear one by one.

The people inside the room are asking, "How do we make the country strong?" The people outside are carrying the weight of "strong" landing on their lives. These are two sides of one history.

## 2. The Stage (geography / climate / society / institutions / events)

- **Geography:** Japan is a chain of islands in the Pacific. The ocean kept it apart from the mainland. Before 1853, the shogunate kept a policy of sakoku (closed country) for over 200 years. Only one small port, Nagasaki, stayed open for limited trade with the Dutch and Chinese. All other Western ships were turned away.
- **Where the change came from:** The energy for the Restoration came from two domains in the far southwest — **Satsuma** (today Kagoshima) and **Chōshū** (today Yamaguchi). These domains were farthest from the capital, least loyal to the shogunate, and earliest to meet Westerners (especially the British) and buy Western guns. The Restoration was driven from the edges into the center.
- **Society:** Society was a strict hereditary class system. There were samurai, farmers, artisans, and merchants, plus a special place for the Emperor's court. Samurai were about 5-6% of the people. They were the ruling class: they could carry swords, had family names, and received a yearly rice "stipend" from their domain. But many low-ranking samurai were actually poor.
- **Institutions:** Japan was not one central country. It was a layered system. The highest figure in name was the **Emperor (Tennō)** in Kyoto. (Important: the Tennō is a uniquely Japanese figure, honored as a sacred bloodline. For over 200 years the Emperor had almost no real power. This is not the same as a Chinese emperor who actually ruled.) Real power belonged to the **shogunate (bakufu)** and its head, the **shōgun** (passed down in the Tokugawa family). Below the shōgun were over 200 semi-independent **domains (han)**, each ruled by a **daimyō** with its own army, money, and laws.
- **The trigger event:** On July 8, 1853, U.S. Commodore Matthew Perry led four warships (the "Black Ships") into the bay near the capital. He delivered a letter from the U.S. President demanding that Japan open up. Perry returned in February 1854 with eight ships. The shogunate signed the Convention of Kanagawa (March 31, 1854), ending over 200 years of closed borders. Over the next years, Japan signed a string of **unequal treaties** (such as the 1858 Harris Treaty). These gave foreigners extraterritoriality (foreigners who broke the law were not judged by Japanese courts) and fixed low tariffs (Japan could not set its own trade taxes). The feeling of humiliation lit everything that followed.

## 3. The Main Characters

> Three viewpoints (actor / lonely-mediator / receiving-end), including at least one powerless side.

### "A Low-Ranking Samurai" — composite character (actor): the reformer who legislates away his own class

This is a **storytelling composite** (not one named person). He stands for the real young samurai from Satsuma or Chōshū. These were men in their twenties and thirties who risked their lives in the Restoration, then joined the new government, and then used their pens to erase the "han," erase the rice stipend, and erase the samurai's right to carry a sword. His name and home scenes are storytelling fill-in. What he does (low-ranking samurai leading the Restoration, then leading the abolition of the samurai class) is solid history.

Say he comes from a poor Chōshū samurai family, so poor the family ate plain brown rice and pickled radish all year. As a boy he was set on fire by a slogan: sonnō jōi — "revere the Emperor, expel the barbarians." It meant "honor the Tennō and drive the Western barbarians out." He and his friends read war books by lamp at night, sharpened their swords, and swore to drive off the Black Ships. But he soon hit a brutal truth: old swords and spears could not beat Western cannons. In 1863-64, Satsuma and Chōshū were each bombarded by Western fleets. Towns burned. Gun batteries were flattened. After taking that beating, he understood something frightening: **to drive out the barbarians, you first have to become one** — learn their guns, railroads, factories, and laws.

After the Restoration won, he joined the new government and helped draft the laws himself. The sharpest moment was the 1876 **Sword Ban (Haitōrei, March 28, 1876)**: from now on, except for soldiers and police, no one could carry a sword in public. For a samurai, the sword was his life and the symbol of who he was. He was a samurai too. When he signed it away, he knew he was closing the door he had come through. **He cut off the topknot (chonmage) on his own head** — the mark of a samurai for 200 years. The day he cut it, he looked in the mirror and saw, for the first time, a "man of the new age" — and, for the first time, a man who had betrayed where he came from.

### Saigō Takamori (lonely-mediator) — 1828-1877 · the greatest hero of the Restoration, who died fighting the state he built

A real historical person. Born January 23, 1828, in a low-ranking Satsuma samurai family. He was tall and broad, with a loud voice and large bright eyes. People who knew him said he had a presence that made others want to follow him to the death. He was not the cleverest planner, but he could pull people together better than anyone in Satsuma.

Satsuma and Chōshū had long been enemies. In January 1866, a wandering samurai from Tosa named **Sakamoto Ryōma** brought them together in Kyoto. Saigō spoke for Satsuma; Kido Takayoshi spoke for Chōshū. The two old enemies shook hands. This became the **Satchō Alliance** — the core military force that would overthrow the shogunate. In the **Boshin War** (1868-69), Saigō was the real military commander of the anti-shogunate army. On May 15, 1868, at the Battle of Ueno, he crushed the shogunate's last forces near the old capital. Without Saigō, there may have been no Restoration.

After the Restoration won, Saigō stood at the center of the new government. But he grew more and more torn. The state he had fought so hard to build then abolished the domains, ended the samurai's stipends, and took away their swords. The Satsuma samurai who had followed him into danger lost their status, their living, and their dignity overnight. Saigō was caught in the middle — between the central state he had helped build, and the old samurai he could not bring himself to leave behind. In 1873 he broke with the government over foreign policy and went home to Kagoshima. In 1877, the desperate old Satsuma samurai rose up with him as their figure. This was the **Satsuma Rebellion**. (Saigō may not have planned it; he looks more like a man pushed to the front who then could not step back.)

The rebel army was about 12,000-13,000 men. It marched north and was stopped at **Kumamoto Castle**. The castle was defended by the government's conscript soldiers, new troops drawn from farmers. The siege lasted over 50 days, giving the government time to gather a much larger army. This was the turning point: the old samurai sword lost to a conscript army that, the day before, had been farmers. It proved that the thing the Restoration had built, conscription, actually worked. And that was the cruelest part. On September 24, 1877, the last survivors retreated to a hill called Shiroyama in Kagoshima. Badly wounded in the final battle, Saigō took his own life. The greatest hero of the Restoration died at the hands of the Restoration. In 1889, the Meiji government pardoned him and rebuilt his image as a tragic national hero.

### "The Farm Daughter" — composite character (receiving-end, **DEFAULT viewpoint**): a girl in a country village on the receiving end of top-down modernization

This is a **storytelling composite** (a made-up name, a made-up family). She stands for the real situation of countless farm daughters of that time. Her specific actions are storytelling fill-in, but what she endures — the new land tax, the draft, the collapse of old certainties — is all solid history.

Say she is about fourteen, living in a mountain village in central Japan. Her family has grown rice for generations. The roof is thatch; the stove burns wood. For her, the Restoration is not about who took power in the capital. It is a string of changes that land on her own doorstep — changes she cannot understand and cannot escape.

The first is the **land tax**. The old way, you paid your dues in rice, based on the harvest. In a bad year you could pay less, or ask for mercy. After the Restoration (the 1873 land-tax reform), you paid in **cash**, based on the **value of the land**, no matter how the harvest went. When the price of rice fell, the family had to sell things or borrow from a moneylender to make that fixed payment. She does not understand "cash tax." She only knows that her mother sits up late at night doing sums, and her face gets tighter the longer she counts.

The second is the **draft**. In 1873 the government passed conscription: every young man must serve in the army at twenty-one. Villagers called it the "blood tax." One day, government men came into the village with a list and read out names. Her brother's name was on it. He was taken away to a place no one could explain, to serve an Emperor no one had ever seen. War used to be the samurai's business, nothing to do with farmers. Now the state reached straight into her home and pulled a person out.

The third is **the certainties that vanished**. The rules she had always known — who is a samurai, who farms, what family name you carry, who you bow to — stopped holding, one by one. Years later, her brother came home to visit. His hair was cut short (no topknot). He wore a Western-style uniform she had never seen. He used strange new words she did not understand. She stood in the doorway and looked for a while before she knew it was her own brother. She never read the Charter Oath. She does not know what "abolish the han" means. She only knows this: the world she was born into, where everything had its fixed place, is gone.

## 4. The Story (plot beats)

1. **1853-54, the Black Ships.** July 8, 1853: Perry's four Black Ships entered the bay. February 1854: he returned with eight. The shogunate signed the Convention of Kanagawa (March 31, 1854). Over 200 years of closed borders ended. Japan saw clearly: Western steam-and-cannon power was something the old Japan simply could not stop.
2. **1858, unequal treaties and humiliation.** The 1858 Harris Treaty and others brought extraterritoriality and fixed tariffs. The shogunate was attacked as weak and a traitor: "Why don't you drive the barbarians out?"
3. **The sonnō jōi movement.** "Revere the Emperor, expel the barbarians" became the banner of the anti-shogunate activists. The logic: the shogunate cannot protect Japan, so return power to the Emperor and push the barbarians back into the sea. Low-ranking samurai were the heart of this force.
4. **1863-64, the awakening from being bombarded.** Satsuma (clash with Britain, 1863) and Chōshū (hit by an allied Western fleet, 1864) each took a hard beating. The activists learned a painful lesson: old weapons cannot beat new industry. To "expel" the West, you must first "learn from" it.
5. **1866, the Satchō Alliance.** January 1866: Sakamoto Ryōma brought the old enemies Satsuma and Chōshū together (Saigō and Kido). They formed the military core that would topple the shogunate. Satsuma, which traded arms with Britain, supplied Chōshū with guns.
6. **1868, the Restoration of Imperial Rule and the Boshin War.** In late 1867, shōgun Tokugawa Yoshinobu "returned" power to the Emperor in name. On January 3, 1868, the new government declared the **Restoration of Imperial Rule (ōsei fukko)**, formally returning power to Emperor Mutsuhito (Emperor Meiji). The Tokugawa loyalists refused, and the **Boshin War** (1868-69) broke out. On May 15, 1868, Saigō broke the shogunate forces at Ueno. The shogunate fell.
7. **April 6, 1868, the Charter Oath.** The brand-new government issued the **Charter Oath**. It promised "deliberative assemblies" and "decisions by public discussion," to break with "the evil customs of the past," and to "seek knowledge throughout the world." This was the statement of the new direction: not going back to the old, but rebuilding.
8. **1871, abolishing the han (haihan chiken).** In 1871, with a single order, the government abolished over 200 domains. It took all the daimyō's lands into central hands and set up prefectures run by centrally appointed governors. A feudal map 200 years old was erased in a few months. This was the boldest step: pulling power fully back from the regions to the center.
9. **1871-73, the Iwakura Mission.** In 1871, a large mission led by Iwakura Tomomi — including core leaders like Ōkubo Toshimichi — traveled through Europe and America for nearly two years. They saw Western factories, parliaments, schools, and armies with their own eyes. They came home with one conclusion: to survive, Japan had to learn the West at the root of its institutions, not just buy a few cannons.
10. **1873, two heavy blows: conscription and land-tax reform.** In 1873 the conscription law passed: war was no longer the samurai's alone — every young farmer's son had to serve at twenty-one. The same year, land-tax reform replaced the old rice dues with a fixed cash tax based on land value. The first broke the samurai's monopoly. The second pushed all the risk onto the farmers.
11. **1876, the Sword Ban and stipend reform — the samurai class is abolished by law.** March 28, 1876: the Sword Ban (Haitōrei) forbade carrying swords in public, except for soldiers and police. August 5, 1876: a forced "stipend settlement" converted the samurai's hereditary stipends into government bonds, all at once. Carrying a sword, drawing a stipend, hereditary status — all three pillars of being a samurai were removed by law. The bitter irony: the people who led these laws were the former low-ranking samurai themselves.
12. **1877, the Satsuma Rebellion.** The stripped-down Satsuma samurai rose up with Saigō. About 12,000-13,000 men marched north and were held at Kumamoto Castle for over 50 days — by a conscript army of former farmers. The old samurai sword lost to the new army the Restoration itself had built. On September 24, 1877, Saigō, defeated, took his own life. This was the last great stand of old samurai Japan — and its funeral.

**Ironies:** The people who rose up shouting "expel the barbarians" saved the country in the end by becoming like the barbarians. The system that made men into samurai was torn down by the very low-ranking samurai it had made. The battle that proved conscription worked (Kumamoto) killed the greatest hero of the Restoration.

## 5. Primary Source Deep-Read

### A. Charter Oath, Article 5 (April 6, 1868)
> Text: "Knowledge shall be sought throughout the world so as to strengthen the foundations of imperial rule."
> 7th-grade read: "Wherever in the world there is useful knowledge, we will go learn it, and use it to make the country's foundation strong."
> Note: It does not say "we will become the West." It says learn from the world to strengthen the Emperor's foundation. Learning from the West is the means; keeping Japan's own core is the goal. This actually starts in a place close to China's "Chinese learning as base, Western learning for use." The difference is how far each went later (see §9).

### B. The word "blood tax" in the conscription notice (1873)
> Background: The 1873 conscription notice described army service as a citizen's duty to the nation. But the phrase "blood tax" was understood by farmers as "the government wants to take our blood."
> 7th-grade read: The government meant "serving is every citizen's duty." Farmers heard "the state will take a person — and blood — from my home."
> Note: The same word meant opposite things to the people giving the order and the people on the receiving end. In 1873, "blood-tax riots" broke out in several places — a mix of this misunderstanding and real fear. What does a "modernization" policy feel like when it lands on a farm daughter no one ever explained it to? This source puts you on that side.

### C. The Sword Ban (Haitōrei core clause, March 28, 1876)
> Text (official notice, in plain terms): Except for soldiers, police, and formal-dress ceremony, carrying swords is forbidden.
> 7th-grade read: From today, except for soldiers and police, no one may wear a sword in the street.
> Note: To us today, this is just a "no weapons" law. But to a samurai, the sword was not a weapon — it was proof of who he was. For 200 years, the sword and the topknot were the sentence "I am a samurai." A single notice erased a 200-year identity in law.

### D. Saigō Takamori's last stand at Shiroyama (1877)
> Record: In September 1877, Saigō's last men retreated to Shiroyama hill in Kagoshima, surrounded by government troops. On September 24, in the final assault, Saigō was badly wounded and took his own life.
> Note: If this passage tells you what was turning in his mind here — he left no record of those thoughts. What is solid is his situation, the way he died, and his choice to share the fate of the old samurai. The part you "hear" is the story drawing you close to his bind, not words he wrote down. A man who helped build a nation choosing to die with the people that nation cast off — that choice itself is the history.

## 6. Consequences (short / middle / long term)

- **Short term (1868-1890):** In a few decades, Japan went from a split feudal system to a centralized modern nation-state — one army, one tax system, one currency, required schooling, railroads and factories. In 1889 it issued the Constitution of the Empire of Japan; in 1890 it opened a parliament. It became **the only non-Western country that was not colonized and instead pushed its way into the ranks of industrial powers.**
- **Middle term (1894-1945):** The national strength built by "rich country, strong army" soon turned outward. In the First Sino-Japanese War (1894-95), Japan defeated Qing China (the Treaty of Shimonoseki took Taiwan, demanded a huge payment, and ended Korea's tributary tie to China). In the Russo-Japanese War (1904-05), Japan defeated Russia. The Restoration saved Japan from being colonized, and it also set Japan on the road to colonizing others — a line of expansion that led to the wars of the early 20th century.
- **Long term (to today):** The Meiji Restoration is the world's classic case of "how a late-developing country modernizes fast under Western pressure." It is studied and argued over again and again. It also leaves a sharp question: **when modernization is fast and top-down, done to "keep the country safe," who pays the price?** From the samurai abolished by law, to the farm daughter carrying the new tax and the draft — "the country got stronger" and "my life got crushed" were often two sides of one thing.

## 9. China in the Same Era (structural comparison, Rule 0 veto dimension) — the killer bridge

This is the sharpest part of this lesson for Chinese-heritage students, and the section where Rule 0 guards most strictly.

**Same era, same shock, same slogan, two paths.**

- Japan was opened by Perry's Black Ships (1853). Qing China was opened earlier, in the two Opium Wars (1840-42, 1856-60) by Britain (and Britain and France). The two great East Asian powers hit the same wall almost back to back: Western industrial-military power that the old system could not stop.
- Both raised almost the same goal. Japan said fukoku kyōhei — "rich country, strong army" — and shokusan kōgyō, "promote industry." Qing China's Self-Strengthening Movement (1861-1895) said "self-strengthening" and "seek wealth," "rich country, strong army." Even the starting logic was alike: learn the West's ships and cannons in order to keep yourself safe.
- Even the worry over how far to learn was alike. The Qing reformers' guiding idea was "**Chinese learning as the base, Western learning for use**" — Western technology (use) can be learned, but China's root institutions and values (base) must not be touched. And the Charter Oath's "seek knowledge throughout the world to strengthen the foundations of imperial rule" also starts from "learn skills to keep our own core."

**So why did the results split apart?** — that is the real question.

The difference is **not** "the Japanese were smart and the Chinese were foolish." It is **not** "the Japanese loved their country and the Chinese did not." The difference is in **how deep each reform cut**, and in how different each side's **structural limits** were:

- **Depth of reform:** Meiji Japan tore down nearly the whole old state and rebuilt it. Abolishing the han (1871) wiped out a 200-year feudal map at once; conscription (1873) ended the samurai's military monopoly; the samurai class was abolished by law (1876); required schooling was set up; a constitution and parliament came (1889-90). **The roots of the institutions were turned over.** The Self-Strengthening Movement mostly stopped at the level of "use": building shipyards and arsenals, buying warships, sending students abroad. But the civil-service exams, the bureaucracy, imperial power, and the gentry structure (the "base") were mostly left untouched. New factories were bolted onto an old core that had not really changed.
- **Different structural limits (this must be said, or it becomes "Japan good / China bad"):** Japan was a small, concentrated island country. Once the anti-shogunate side took power, the low-ranking samurai — a group ready to act — could quickly form a new center. Qing China was a vast empire of hundreds of millions, held together by the exam system and the gentry. Any reform that touched the "base" had to move far larger entrenched interests and balance center and region at a far harder level. The same cut that worked in Japan might have split the Chinese empire apart. Both sides chose inside their own real limits; neither stood on higher moral ground.

**The ending was settled by a war.** In the First Sino-Japanese War (1894-95), Meiji Japan defeated the Qing China that the Self-Strengthening Movement had built over more than thirty years. The Treaty of Shimonoseki took Taiwan, demanded a crushing payment, and ended Korea's tributary tie to China. This war is widely seen as the "final exam" of two paths to modernization — but be careful: who wins a single war is not a verdict on which civilization is better. It shows that "reform down to the institutional root" and "reform bolted onto an old base" worked differently in that particular race. It is **not** "Japanese civilization advanced, Chinese civilization backward."

**Rule 0 close:** We cannot say Japan "won, so it was right" and China "lost, so it was wrong." Japan's "down to the root" path cost the lives and worlds of countless samurai abolished by law and farmers crushed by the new tax and draft — and it soon turned that strength against its neighbors. China's "keep the base" path reflected the real worries of a huge civilization under huge limits. Each path answered its own situation, with its own wisdom, blind spots, and costs. **Which side you stand on changes the story you see. That is exactly the skill this lesson asks you to practice: see both sides clearly first, then judge.**

## 10. What Historians Argue About (historiography)

- **"Revolution" or "elite reshuffle"?** One reading stresses that the Restoration was a true revolution — it abolished a hereditary class system and rebuilt the whole state. Another stresses that it was top-down, led by a low-samurai elite, with ordinary people (farmers, women) having no voice — calling it "revolution from above."
- **The Emperor's role:** One reading tells the story as "power returns to the Emperor." Another points out that Emperor Meiji was only a boy at the time; the real power was a small group of Restoration leaders, and the Emperor was more a symbol raised up to provide legitimacy.
- **The cost of modernization:** Recent social history turns the camera from "successful nation-building" to "those on the receiving end" — peasant uprisings (blood-tax riots, land-tax riots), the abolished samurai, the situation of women under the new order. It argues that the "Japanese modernization miracle" story hides the price paid at the bottom.
- **The comparison with China:** Comparative historians long debate why Japan and China diverged — some stress Japan's "flexibility / small scale," some stress the Qing's "structural limits," and some object to the "honor student vs. failing student" framing and argue each country should be read in its own context.

## 11. Common Misconceptions (myth-busting)

1. ❌ "Emperor Mutsuhito (Meiji) led the Restoration himself." → ✅ He was a boy when he took the throne. The real planners were a small group of Restoration leaders from the low-ranking samurai (Saigō, Ōkubo, Kido, and others).
2. ❌ "Japan went fully Western and turned itself into the West." → ✅ The slogan was to learn Western skills to strengthen Japan's own core (the Emperor, the nation). It was a means, not the goal; many old structures (Emperor's legitimacy, elite rule) were kept or even strengthened.
3. ❌ "The Tennō is Japan's 'emperor,' the same as a Chinese emperor." → ✅ The Tennō is a uniquely Japanese figure, honored as a sacred bloodline, with almost no real power for over 200 years. The Chinese pattern of dynasties rising and falling, with an emperor who actually rules, does not apply.
4. ❌ "The samurai were destroyed by outsiders." → ✅ The government that abolished the samurai class by law (the sword ban, the stipend settlement) was made up of former low-ranking samurai themselves.
5. ❌ "Saigō Takamori was a conservative who opposed the Restoration." → ✅ He was one of its greatest heroes and the commander of the anti-shogunate army. His later rebellion was the tragedy of a man caught between the new state he built and the old samurai it cast off — not lifelong opposition to reform.
6. ❌ "Japan reformed well because the Japanese were smarter or more patriotic than the Chinese." → ✅ The difference lies in the depth of reform and each side's structural limits, not in which people were better (see §9, Rule 0).
7. ❌ "The Sino-Japanese War proved Japanese civilization is superior to Chinese civilization." → ✅ It proved that two paths to modernization worked differently in that one race. Who wins a single war is not a verdict on which civilization is better.

## 12. Open Questions (no conclusions)

1. The low-ranking samurai signed laws that erased "samurai" as a class — that is, erased the thing that made him who he was. Would you destroy the old world that made you, for a new country you believe in?
2. Saigō Takamori helped build the new nation, then died alongside the old samurai it cast off. When "be loyal to the nation you built" and "do not abandon the people who risked their lives with you" tear apart, which side matters more?
3. The farm daughter made no decisions, yet she carried the new tax, lost her brother, and watched her familiar world collapse. When a country gets strong fast "for everyone's good," who decides, and who carries the cost — is that fair? In this lesson you only heard her side. How would the other two viewpoints challenge your judgment right now?
4. Japan and Qing China shouted almost the same slogan, "rich country, strong army," and ended on different paths. If you were a Qing reformer facing a vast empire held together by the exam system and the gentry, would you dare — could you — reform "down to the institutional root" the way Japan did? If not, is that because you were not brave enough, or because your situation was simply different?
5. "The country got stronger" and "countless ordinary lives got crushed" are often two sides of one thing. Around you, is there anything where, "for the greater good," some people quietly carry the cost? Who has the right to make that decision?

## Appendix A: Key Timeline

- 1853.7.8: Perry's four Black Ships enter the bay; demand Japan open up.
- 1854.3.31: Convention of Kanagawa; 200+ years of closed borders end.
- 1858: Harris Treaty and other unequal treaties (extraterritoriality, fixed tariffs).
- 1863-64: Satsuma and Chōshū are bombarded; activists learn "learn from the West to resist the West."
- 1866.1: Sakamoto Ryōma brokers the Satchō Alliance (Saigō + Kido).
- late 1867: Shōgun Tokugawa Yoshinobu "returns" power to the Emperor.
- 1868.1.3: Restoration of Imperial Rule; power to Emperor Mutsuhito (Meiji).
- 1868.4.6: The Charter Oath is issued.
- 1868-69: The Boshin War; 1868.5.15 Battle of Ueno.
- 1871: Abolition of the han (haihan chiken); 200+ domains become prefectures.
- 1871-73: The Iwakura Mission travels through Europe and America.
- 1873: Conscription law + land-tax reform.
- 1876.3.28: The Sword Ban (Haitōrei). 1876.8.5: forced stipend settlement.
- 1877.2.15: Satsuma Rebellion begins; Kumamoto siege ~50+ days.
- 1877.9.24: Saigō Takamori, defeated, takes his own life (Shiroyama).
- 1889-90: Constitution of the Empire of Japan; parliament opens.
- 1894-95: First Sino-Japanese War; Treaty of Shimonoseki.

## Appendix B: Core Vocabulary

- **Emperor / Tennō** — Japan's unique highest symbol, honored as a sacred bloodline (NOT the same as a Chinese emperor)
- **shogunate (bakufu) / shōgun** — the military government that actually ruled / its hereditary head (the Tokugawa family)
- **han / daimyō** — a semi-independent domain / its lord
- **samurai** — the hereditary sword-bearing ruling class (~5-6% of the people)
- **Black Ships** — Perry's U.S. warships, 1853
- **unequal treaties / extraterritoriality / treaty tariffs** — treaties favoring the West; foreigners not judged by Japanese courts; Japan unable to set its own trade taxes
- **sonnō jōi** — "revere the Emperor, expel the barbarians"
- **Satchō Alliance** — the Satsuma-Chōshū anti-shogunate alliance (1866)
- **Restoration of Imperial Rule (ōsei fukko)** — 1868, power returned to the Emperor
- **Boshin War** — 1868-69, the anti-shogunate civil war
- **Charter Oath** — 1868, the new government's program
- **haihan chiken** — 1871, abolishing the han, setting up prefectures
- **Iwakura Mission** — 1871-73, the study mission to Europe and America
- **conscription** — 1873, universal military service
- **land-tax reform** — 1873, cash tax based on land value
- **Haitōrei (Sword Ban)** — 1876, carrying swords forbidden
- **Satsuma Rebellion** — 1877, the samurai's last stand
- **fukoku kyōhei / shokusan kōgyō** — "rich country, strong army" / "promote industry" (Restoration slogans)
- **chonmage (topknot)** — the samurai's topknot (the old mark of status)
