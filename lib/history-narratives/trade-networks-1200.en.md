# Afro-Eurasian Trade Networks · 1200-1450

> **Lock status**: narrative kernel v0 — pending review
>
> **AP anchor**: AP World History Modern, Unit 2.1-2.7 (Networks of Exchange) — 8-10% of the exam
> **Through-line**: the most powerful thing in this era was not an empire — it was connection itself
> **Difficulty**: 3/5 (many places and terms; the payoff is seeing one giant web)
> **Expected duration**: 35-45 min

---

## 0. Open Your Textbook First (Textbook Anchor)

> This belongs to **AP World History Modern, Unit 2 (1200-1450) — "Networks of Exchange,"** which is **8-10% of the whole AP exam** and the **structural backbone** of this era.
>
> Your textbook will tell you about three great networks:
> - **The Silk Roads** — overland relay routes from China through Central Asia to the Mediterranean.
> - **The Indian Ocean trade** — the sea network linking East Africa, Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, and China by **monsoon** winds.
> - **The Trans-Saharan trade** — camel caravans swapping West African **gold** for Saharan **salt**.
>
> **What your textbook may NOT make clear** (this is why we need this lesson):
> - **It's a network, not one road.** No single person walked the whole Silk Road. Goods were handed off, relay-style, in one middleman city after another.
> - **The biggest sea network was the Indian Ocean, not the Silk Roads** — and it is the piece most online courses leave nearly blank.
> - **It's not just goods that move.** Along the same routes traveled technology (the **compass**, the **lateen sail**, the **camel saddle**), religion (Islam spread along all three networks), crops, knowledge, people (diaspora communities AND enslaved people), and germs (the Black Death).
> - **Connection cut both ways.** The same web that made the world rich carried the Black Death of 1347-1351, which may have killed about a third of Europe.
> - **China was one of the biggest engines of this web** — long before the famous voyages of Zheng He. This lesson holds both the wonder and the cost. It does not pick a final verdict for you.

## 1. One-Sentence Anchor (Three Networks at Once)

Between 1200 and 1450, the Old World (Afro-Eurasia — Africa plus Eurasia) was stitched, for the first time, into almost one connected whole by **three giant trade networks**. They were not one "road." They were countless short relay legs — no one walked end to end. Goods were passed like a baton, changing hands in one middleman city after another. The three networks were:

- **The Silk Roads** — from northwest China, through the **oasis cities** of Central Asia (like **Samarkand**), camel caravans relayed goods leg by leg to the Mediterranean. The Mongol-built peace (**Pax Mongolica**, "the Mongol Peace") made this route safer than ever in the 1200s.
- **The Indian Ocean trade** — powered by the **monsoon** (a wind that blows one way for half the year and the opposite way for the other half). This natural "wind timetable" linked East Africa, Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, and China into one sea web. It was the **largest** sea network of the era — sailed by triangular-sailed **dhows** and big Chinese ships.
- **The Trans-Saharan trade** — camel caravans crossed the Sahara, the world's largest desert, to swap West African **gold** for **salt** from the north (salt was so scarce in the desert it could trade for its weight in gold). Cities like **Timbuktu** grew rich in both gold and learning.

In the same era, at the eastern end of the earth, China's **Song and Yuan** dynasties were one of the **biggest engines** of this whole web. The ports of **Quanzhou** and **Guangzhou** were crammed with foreign ships; Chinese porcelain and silk sailed the maritime Silk Road all the way to East Africa; and the compass spread out into the world from Chinese ships. China was not an isolated block in the East — it was one of the beating hearts of this world-spanning web.

But the easiest thing to forget in this web is **the people who actually held it up with their feet and hands** — the porters leading camels, the sailors climbing masts, the workers hauling slabs of salt. History left almost no names for them. Yet without them, not one inch of Samarkand's silk, Calicut's pepper, or Timbuktu's gold could move. This lesson begins from their position.

## 2. The Stage (Geography / Climate / Society / Institutions / Events)

- **Geography**: each network had its own "terrain script."
  - Silk Roads: from China, past the **oasis cities** at the edge of the Taklamakan Desert and over the Pamir mountains, through Central Asia (Samarkand, Bukhara) to Persia and the Mediterranean. Oases were the lifeline — no water, no city.
  - Indian Ocean: a "crescent" of coasts — the East African **Swahili coast** (today's Kenya and Tanzania), Arabia, India's west coast (the **Malabar** coast, where **Calicut** sits), the **Strait of Malacca** in Southeast Asia, and on to China.
  - Trans-Saharan: to the south, West Africa's **Sahel** (the grassland edge below the Sahara) and the gold-rich forests; to the north, Mediterranean North Africa; in between, the world's largest hot desert.
- **Climate / setting**:
  - **The monsoon is the engine of Indian Ocean trade**: roughly April-September the southwest monsoon blows ships northeast (toward India and China); October-March the northeast monsoon blows them back southwest (toward Arabia and East Africa). Merchants had to follow this "wind timetable," often living for months in a foreign port waiting for next season's wind — which created **diaspora communities** (groups of merchants settling in foreign ports).
  - The Sahara was a world where water was life. Caravans relayed from oasis well to oasis well; the camel, the "ship of the desert," could go days without drinking and was the only way across.
  - Central Asia on the Silk Road had a continental climate — fierce summers, brutal winters. The **caravanserai** (a walled roadside inn every so often) was the safe node where caravans and camels rested, watered, and traded.
- **Society**: the network created a new kind of person — the **intermediary**. Since no one traveled the whole way, wealth pooled in the "cities where goods changed hands" and in the "people who handled the swap": the Central Asian merchants of Samarkand, the Indian and Arab middlemen of Calicut, the gold-and-salt brokers of Timbuktu. These cities held diaspora communities from many lands, speaking many languages, following different religions, often in their own quarters. At the bottom were the **hauling laborers**: porters, sailors, camel drivers — and the **enslaved people** bought and sold along the very same routes (both the trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean networks trafficked human beings).
- **Institutions**:
  - **Pax Mongolica (the Mongol Peace)**: in the 1200s the Mongol Empire spanned Eurasia and unified security and relay stations along the Silk Road, sharply lowering the risk of long-distance trade. (A famous exaggeration of the time said a girl could carry gold across the whole route unrobbed.) This was the golden age of the Silk Road.
  - **Financial tools**: long-distance trade created credit — bills of exchange and partnership contracts (like the *commenda*) let merchants travel without carrying gold and silver on their backs.
  - **Islam as a "business operating system"**: from West Africa to Southeast Asia, Islam spread along the trade routes and gave Muslim merchants from different places a **shared set of laws, a shared language (Arabic), shared trust, and a shared moral code**. Reciting the same *shahada* (the Islamic declaration of faith) and keeping the same contracts, even strangers could do business.
- **Events**: there is no single "start day," but a few landmark moments: the Mongol conquests of the 1219-1260s built the Pax Mongolica; in **1324**, the West African emperor **Mansa Musa** of Mali made a stunning gold-laden pilgrimage (**hajj**) to Mecca, putting Mali on the world map; from **1325 to 1354**, the Moroccan traveler **Ibn Battuta** roamed all three networks and left the most detailed eyewitness record; and in **1347-1351**, the **Black Death** rode these same trade routes across Eurasia and Africa, killing perhaps a third of Europe's people — the web that connected the world also became the highway for the plague.

## 3. The Main Characters

> Three perspectives (actor, mediator/witness, receiving-end), including at least one powerless party.

### Mansa Musa (actor) — c.1280-c.1337 · the Mali emperor who "drew" West Africa onto the world map with gold

A real historical person. Born around 1280, he became **Mansa** (the Mali title, meaning "king of kings") in 1312 (some say 1307), and died around 1337. The Mali he ruled sat on top of West Africa's gold-producing lands — gold that, at the time, supplied a large share of the whole Old World. A devout Muslim, in **1324** he set out to complete the **hajj** (the Muslim pilgrimage) to Mecca.

That journey became one of the most famous "displays of wealth" in world history. By the records, his caravan was enormous, with camels carrying great loads of gold, and he handed out gold along the way. Passing through Cairo, he spent and gave away so much gold that the local **price of gold was said to stay depressed for years** afterward. This is not a small detail in a legend — it really changed a region's prices, and it made Europe and the Arab world realize for the first time that there was a fabulously rich empire south of the Sahara. In **1375**, a famous European map (the **Catalan Atlas**) simply drew Mansa Musa as a king holding up a nugget of gold.

After returning, he built mosques in Timbuktu and Gao and turned Timbuktu into a **city of learning** — the **Sankore** mosque-school drew scholars and held rich collections of books. This perspective lets you view trade from "the winner at the top of the network": how gold, faith, and fame made an inland empire a world player. But do not forget — his wealth, too, rested on the labor of gold miners and trafficked people.

### Ibn Battuta (mediator / witness) — 1304-1368/69 · the Moroccan traveler who connected the world into one travel-book

A real historical person. Born in 1304 in Tangier, Morocco. In **1325**, the 21-year-old only meant to make the hajj to Mecca once — but he ended up traveling for **nearly 30 years and about 100,000 kilometers**. He touched almost every corner of the three networks: North Africa, the Sahara, the Mali of West Africa, the East African **Swahili coast** (he called **Kilwa** one of the most beautiful cities in the world), Arabia, Persia, India (where he served as a judge in the Delhi Sultanate), Southeast Asia, and all the way to **Quanzhou** in China.

That he could travel so far is itself **living proof** of how this network worked: because he was an educated Muslim jurist (trained in Islamic law), in every Muslim trading town the local Muslim community treated him as one of their own — feeding him, giving gifts, offering posts. One shared faith and one shared law let a stranger cross half the earth and find "his own people" everywhere. His travel-book, the **Rihla** (meaning "the Journey"), is one of historians' most precious eyewitness records of this era's trade networks.

This perspective is connectivity itself made human: he did not sell goods or fight wars — he **traveled, watched, and recorded**. Through his eyes you can see that the three networks were really **one network** — the same faith, the same money logic, the same port life, stretching from Morocco all the way to China.

### A camel-leading porter / a mast-climbing sailor (receiving-end · DEFAULT) — the people who held up the whole web with their bodies and left no name

A bridging character, with a fully real role (this is the DEFAULT perspective — it challenges the assumption that "trade history = kings and great merchants"). He has no name — because history left **almost no names** for these people, and that, exactly, is part of the problem.

Picture him as two figures at once. In the Sahara, he is the porter in a caravan, leading camels and hauling slabs of salt, his lips cracked by the desert wind, kept alive by one well at the next oasis, knowing which dune hides water and which stretch of road buried men a few years back. On the Indian Ocean, he is a sailor on a **dhow**, his palms calloused from planks sewn together with coconut fiber (a dhow's hull really was "sewn" with coconut rope, not nailed with iron), reading the stars and smelling the wind to find his way, able to sail only with one direction of monsoon for half a year. He may not even be a "worker" — on these two networks, **people themselves were goods to be bought and sold**: enslaved people marched chained across the Sahara, or loaded onto ships across the Indian Ocean, in no small numbers.

Mansa Musa's gold, Calicut's pepper, Samarkand's silk — every inch they moved was moved by people like him, with their feet, their hands, their lives. This perspective lets you see the whole web from the lowest, most nameless position: the reward of connection flowed up to the emperors and great merchants, while the cost of connection (exhaustion, danger, being trafficked) pressed down below. It also makes you face a hard truth — the same "magnificent web of the world," seen from the bottom up, is a different story.

## 4. The Story (Plot Beats)

1. **The three networks each grew first (before ~1200).** The Silk Roads had existed off and on since the Han dynasty; monsoon-driven Indian Ocean trade had run for over a thousand years; the trans-Saharan gold-salt swap took shape in the medieval era as camels spread. What was new around 1200: all three networks entered an **unusually busy, interlocking** peak at once.
2. **Technology made the web denser.** Three "small but decisive" technologies: ① the **compass** (which spread out from Chinese ships, so navigation no longer needed clear skies and stars), ② the **lateen sail + dhow** (which let a ship zigzag *against* the wind, not just wait for a tailwind), and ③ the **camel saddle** (especially the North Arabian type, which let camels carry heavy loads and be steered well, making Sahara crossings a routine business). Technology was not background scenery — it was a direct reason the web could grow.
3. **Pax Mongolica lights up the Silk Road (1200s).** The Mongol Empire unified a vast stretch from China to the Middle East, using relay stations and military order to push Silk Road risk to a historic low. Merchants, missionaries, and envoys (including the later-famous Marco Polo) moved along this "safe corridor." This was the golden age of the overland Silk Road.
4. **The Indian Ocean beats to the rhythm of the monsoon.** A timetable of east wind for half the year and west wind for the other half decided everyone's schedule. Merchants stayed for months in ports like Calicut, Kilwa, and Malacca waiting for the wind, which created **multilingual, multi-faith port cities** and settled **diaspora communities** — Arabs, Persians, Gujaratis, and Chinese putting down roots in foreign ports, marrying, building mosques.
5. **Islam spreads along the routes and becomes the "common language of business" (1200s-1300s).** From the West African Sahel to Southeast Asian ports, ruler after ruler and merchant after merchant converted to Islam — not only faith, but a whole set of **cross-regional shared law, writing, and trust networks**. Swahili culture in East Africa, Mali in West Africa, India's ports, and the sultanates of Southeast Asia were all reshaped by this tide.
6. **1324 — Mansa Musa's pilgrimage.** The Mali emperor crossed the Sahara to Mecca with stunning quantities of gold, handing out so much it stirred Cairo's prices, formally connecting West Africa's wealth to the Islamic world and to European awareness. The trans-Saharan network was now highlighted on the "world map."
7. **1325-1354 — Ibn Battuta's great journey.** A Muslim jurist traveled almost all of the three networks, welcomed everywhere by a shared faith, and left the *Rihla* — so that later generations could "see with their own eyes" how this web worked.
8. **The middleman cities reach their peak.** Samarkand (Silk Road), Calicut (Indian Ocean), and Timbuktu (trans-Saharan) became the star hubs of their networks: goods changed hands here, cultures mixed here, wealth pooled here.
9. **Not just goods: technology, religion, crops, and people moved together.** Papermaking, numerals (the Indian-Arabic numerals), sugar, cotton, and citrus crops, along with religions and stories, spread along the web; at the same time, enslaved people were trafficked along the very same routes — the network magnified both the reward and the cruelty at once.
10. **1347-1351 — the Black Death erupts along the web.** The plague bacterium most likely jumped from rodents on the Central Asian steppe, rode the Mongol relay roads and caravans, then transferred to ships, and spread along the same trade web across Eurasia and Africa, killing perhaps a third of Europe's people. **The web that made the world rich also became the web that made the world die** — connectivity's darkest lesson.
11. **The networks reshuffle (mid-to-late 1300s).** The Black Death, the breakup of the Mongol Empire, and changes of rulers everywhere reshuffled the networks — but **trade did not stop**; it kept running with new lead players. After China entered the Ming dynasty, **Zheng He** led giant fleets on seven voyages across the Indian Ocean (**1405-1433**), projecting Chinese state power directly into the ocean. But remember: **before Zheng He, China's private sea merchants had already sailed the Indian Ocean for centuries** — Zheng He nationalized an existing network; he did not open virgin seas.
12. **The network sets the stage for the next era.** It was exactly this already-rich, hugely profitable Old World trade web that made Europeans desperate with envy — they wanted to bypass the middlemen and reach the East's spices and wealth directly. The ocean voyages of Portugal and Spain after 1450 were, at heart, an attempt to splice a **new entrance, controlled by themselves**, onto this old web. The 1200-1450 networks were the direct cause of the post-1450 "Age of Exploration."

**An irony to keep:** the most powerful thing in this era was not any emperor — it was "connection" itself. It set gold, silk, and faith in motion, and it also set an invisible germ in motion, which took more lives in a few years than any war.

## 5. Reading the Documents (Primary Sources)

### A. Ibn Battuta's Rihla on Kilwa (the East African Swahili coast)
> Gist (as commonly rendered in English translations of his travel-book): he called Kilwa one of the "most beautiful and well-built cities" he had seen, full of devout Muslims and fine buildings.
> Plain reading: a man who traveled half the world said this city on the East African coast was one of the most beautiful in the world.
> Many-sided reading: this reminds us that many of the era's "most advanced, richest" cities were not in Europe but in East Africa, India, and Central Asia. But the same Swahili city-state's wealth also rested, in part, on the trade in ivory, gold, and enslaved people from the interior. Prosperity and cost share one frame.

### B. The earliest record of the compass used for navigation (China, c.1119)
> Source: Zhu Yu's *Pingzhou Ketan* ("Pingzhou Table Talks," written around 1119) records that a ship's pilot, when overcast skies hid the stars, used a magnetized needle to find direction. This is the **earliest written record in the world** of the compass used for sea navigation.
> Plain reading: nearly 900 years ago, a Chinese book wrote down that sailors used a magnetic needle to find their way on cloudy days when they couldn't see the stars.
> Reading: this turns "China was a technology engine of the trade web" from a slogan into evidence. The compass was not an abstract label on a list of "great inventions" — it was a concrete technology that **spread out from Chinese ships and changed how the whole world sailed**.

### C. The logic of a "monsoon timetable" (synthesized from several navigation traditions)
> Content: Indian Ocean merchants had to wait for the southwest monsoon (about April-September) to sail east, and the northeast monsoon (about October-March) to sail back west; miss the wind season and you waited half a year in a foreign port.
> Plain reading: the wind only blows each direction for half the year, so you have to follow the wind's "timetable" — miss it, and you live half a year in someone else's city waiting for the next "wind shift."
> Reading: a **natural phenomenon** (the monsoon) shaped a **human society** (diaspora communities, multi-faith port cities) — exactly the kind of "environment shapes history" argument the AP loves. The months of waiting for the wind turned strangers into neighbors and ports into multilingual worlds.

### D. Mansa Musa on the Catalan Atlas (1375, made in Europe)
> Content: on this famous map made in Europe in 1375, Mansa Musa of Mali is drawn as an enthroned king holding up a nugget of gold.
> Plain reading: on a world map Europeans drew over 600 years ago, the king of West Africa holds up gold — Europeans already knew there was gold over there, and enough of it to belong on the map.
> Reading: note the perspective — this is evidence of **how Europeans saw West Africa**, not West Africa's own account of itself. It both proves the real fame of Mali's wealth and reminds us that much of the record of this network was left by outsiders (Ibn Battuta, European mapmakers). The voices of the overlooked themselves are the rarest of all.

## 6. Consequences (Short / Medium / Long Term)

- **Short term (1200-1450, at the time):** the Old World was stitched into an unusually connected whole. Technology (compass, lateen sail, camel saddle, papermaking, Indian-Arabic numerals), crops (sugar, cotton, citrus), religions (Islam, Buddhism), stories, and knowledge spread along the web faster than in any earlier period. Middleman cities (Samarkand, Calicut, Timbuktu) and diaspora communities flourished as never before.
- **Medium term (the Black Death and after):** the 1347-1351 plague spread along the same web, killing perhaps a third of Europe's people, gutting the Middle East, and shaking economies and societies everywhere — connectivity's backlash. The Mongol Empire broke apart and the lead players reshuffled, but trade itself did not stop; it kept running with new routes and new lead players.
- **Long term (pointing past 1450):** this high-profit Old World trade web was the most direct cause of Europe's "Age of Exploration" — Europeans wanted to bypass the middlemen and control their own entrance to the East, so came the post-1450 ocean expansion of Portugal and Spain. Seen this way, Columbus "looking for India" and finding the Americas, and da Gama rounding Africa to India, were both **sequels** to the 1200-1450 web. At the same time, the slave trade that ran along this web is one prehistory of the later, far larger trans-Atlantic slave trade. The prosperity, mixing, plague, and exploitation that connection brought are different faces of the same web.

## 7. AP DBQ Thinking Practice

- **Three-network comparison grid (the tested "comparison" skill):** put the Silk Roads / Indian Ocean / Trans-Saharan into one table and compare each: which technology? main goods (high-value low-bulk vs bulk)? who are the intermediaries? which religion spread along it? how did the environment shape it (oasis / monsoon / desert)? — AP Unit 2's Topic 2.7 directly tests comparing the three networks.
- **"It's not just goods that move" (cross-cultural exchange):** argue with evidence that the trade web also moved technology (compass, lateen sail), religion (Islam along all three networks), crops, knowledge (Indian-Arabic numerals, papermaking), people (diaspora communities and the enslaved), and germs (the Black Death). This is the "trade ≠ just swapping things" argument graders reward.
- **Environment shapes history (causation):** how did the monsoon "timetable" directly create diaspora communities and multilingual port cities? How did Pax Mongolica lower risk and light up the Silk Road? How did the Sahara's water decide caravan routes? Write the natural factors into the causal chain.
- **Continuity & change:** from 1200 to 1450, what **continued** (monsoon trade, the basic logic of the gold-salt swap, the role of middleman cities) and what **changed** (the rise and fall of the Mongol Peace, the reach of Islam's spread, the post-plague reshuffle, technology upgrades)?
- **The double edge of connectivity (the nuance to practice):** the same web brought prosperity and cultural mixing AND plague and the slave trade. The AP does not want a 0/1 answer — it wants you to argue the reward **and** the cost at the same time.

## 8. China in the Same Era (Structural Comparison · Rule 0 veto dimension)

The years 1200-1450 span China's **Southern Song, Yuan, and early Ming**. This section is not "mention China in passing" — it makes one fact concrete: **Song-Yuan China was one of the biggest engines of this sea-and-land trade web, and China was deeply connected to the Indian Ocean long before Zheng He.**

Concrete evidence (not slogans):

1. **The shibosi + Quanzhou and Guangzhou: a state-run foreign-trade machine.** In 1087 the Northern Song set up the **shibosi** (市舶司 — the office that managed and taxed overseas trade, like an ancient "maritime customs") at Quanzhou. In the Song-Yuan period Quanzhou became **one of the largest and busiest ports in the world**, crowded with foreign ships, called "the greatest port of the East." China did not passively wait for buyers — it actively ran maritime trade through state institutions.
2. **Porcelain and silk exports: Chinese goods were the hard currency of this web.** From the Song dynasty on, **porcelain** replaced silk as China's largest export, and kilns near the coastal ports fired specifically for export. Song-era porcelain shards have been dug up at sites as far away as the **East African coast** — physical proof that Chinese goods traveled the maritime Silk Road all the way to Africa.
3. **The compass: the navigation technology China gave the world.** Around 1119, Zhu Yu's *Pingzhou Ketan* left the world's earliest record of "using a magnetic needle to navigate at sea" (see §5B). The compass spread out from Chinese ships and ultimately changed how the whole world sailed — one of China's most concrete technological contributions to the entire trade web.
4. **Before Zheng He, China's private sea merchants had sailed for centuries.** In 1405-1433, the Ming admiral Zheng He led giant fleets on seven voyages, reaching as far as East Africa — but this "nationalized and scaled up" an **already-existing** private sea network; it did not open empty seas. Song-Yuan Chinese sea merchants and Arab-Persian merchants had been traveling between Quanzhou and the Indian Ocean for centuries before Zheng He.

Rule 0: this section is meant to let Chinese-heritage students see that "China was not an isolated block in the East, but one of the beating hearts of the world trade web" — but it does **not** therefore say China was "the center / the most advanced." Calicut, Kilwa, Samarkand, and Timbuktu each held their own central roles. This was a **multi-centered** web. China was a hugely important engine within it, not the single hub.

## 9. What Historians Argue About (Historiography)

- **The "world system" school (Janet Abu-Lughod):** her 1989 book *Before European Hegemony* argued that long before Europe rose (about 1250-1350), there was already a "world system" made of several overlapping trade circuits — **multi-centered, with no single master**. This school treats the era as a pre-Eurocentric prototype of globalization.
- **The technology-environment reading:** stresses the compass, lateen sail, camel saddle, and monsoon, arguing that the network's reach and prosperity were decided first by "could you get there and move the goods," not by rulers' will alone.
- **The Islam-integration reading:** stresses that the shared law, language, and trust networks Islam provided were the key "software" that glued scattered ports into one web — the commercial network and the faith network were two faces of the same web.
- **The "cost of connectivity" reading (including the history of disease and slavery):** recent work does not avoid the Black Death's spread along the routes, nor the trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean slave trades, and argues for holding both "the reward of connection" and "the cost of connection," refusing to romanticize the era into a pure "Silk Road legend."
- **A neutral synthesis:** the 1200-1450 Old World was both humanity's first near-"globalization" — a dazzling experiment — AND a complex system where reward and cost (plague, slavery, inequality) lived together. Both are true at once; do not make it either/or.

## 10. Common Misconceptions (Myth-Busting)

1. ❌ "The Silk Road was one road, and merchants walked from China to Rome." → ✅ It was **countless short relay legs**; goods changed hands city by city, and almost no one walked the whole way. "One road" is a later simplification made to tell a tidy story.
2. ❌ "The most important sea network was the Silk Road." → ✅ The **largest sea network of the era was the Indian Ocean monsoon trade**, not the overland Silk Road. The Indian Ocean is the piece most current courses ignore and most need to add.
3. ❌ "The compass and lateen sail are minor background details." → ✅ Technology was a **direct cause** of the network's growth — without the compass and lateen sail, ocean-going Indian Ocean trade could not run; without the camel saddle, crossing the Sahara could not become routine business.
4. ❌ "A trade network is just swapping goods." → ✅ Along the web also moved technology, religion (Islam along all three networks), crops, knowledge, people (diaspora communities and the enslaved), and germs (the Black Death). "It's not just goods that move" is a core AP argument.
5. ❌ "The Black Death was only a European disaster and had nothing to do with trade." → ✅ It most likely spread from the Central Asian steppe along this trade web across all of Eurasia and Africa — it was **connection itself** that made the plague a cross-continental highway.
6. ❌ "Zheng He was the first to connect China to the Indian Ocean." → ✅ Before Zheng He (from 1405), Song-Yuan China's private sea merchants had already sailed the Indian Ocean for **centuries**; Zheng He nationalized and scaled up an existing network, he did not open virgin seas.
7. ❌ "This was a web centered on China (or on any one country)." → ✅ It was **multi-centered** — Samarkand, Calicut, Kilwa, Timbuktu, and Quanzhou were each star hubs of different networks, with no single center.
8. ❌ "Mansa Musa's gold story is a later exaggeration." → ✅ His pilgrimage handed out enough gold to **really depress Cairo's gold price for years**, and he was drawn onto a 1375 European map — a real, recorded, physically-attested impact, not pure legend.

## 11. Thinking Questions (Open-Ended, No Conclusion)

1. The most powerful thing in this era was not any emperor — it was "connection" itself. A web that made the whole world rich then, in a few years, used a plague to take more lives than any war. If connection brings prosperity and disaster at once, is "being connected" a good thing or a bad thing — or is that the wrong question to ask?
2. Samarkand, Calicut, Timbuktu — some of the richest, most learned cities in the world back then — are names many people today have never heard; while Western Europe, still relatively marginal at the time, later dominated the world. Why did "the center of now" and "the center of then" swap places? Does this mean the "center" of history is fixed, or that it moves?
3. Mansa Musa's gold and Ibn Battuta's travel-book were written down, but the porter leading camels, the sailor climbing the mast, and the person marched chained across the desert left almost no names. When history remembers only the people at the top of the network, what exactly do we lose? Who gets to be "remembered"?
4. China was one of the biggest engines of this web — the shibosi, Quanzhou, porcelain, the compass. But this section also said China was not "the single center." When you feel both proud that "China was a heart of the world trade web" and willing to admit "it was one of several centers," can both feelings be true at once?
5. This time you mostly saw the whole web from the position of the nameless camel-leader and mast-climber. How would Mansa Musa tell this web? How would a pepper middleman in Calicut tell it? How would a Chinese official at the Quanzhou shibosi tell it? Whose voice have you not heard yet?

## Appendix A: Key Timeline

- ~1119: Zhu Yu's *Pingzhou Ketan* is written, leaving the world's earliest record of "using a magnetic needle to navigate at sea."
- 1087: The Northern Song establishes the shibosi (the overseas-trade office) at Quanzhou.
- 1200s (from c.1219-1260s): the Mongol conquests build the Pax Mongolica; the Silk Road enters its safest golden age.
- 1271-1295: Marco Polo is said to travel to and from China along the Silk Road (route details are debated by historians).
- c.1280: Mansa Musa is born.
- 1312 (some say 1307): Mansa Musa becomes Mansa of Mali.
- 1324: Mansa Musa makes the hajj to Mecca, handing out gold that depresses Cairo's gold price for years.
- c.1327: Mansa Musa builds mosques in Timbuktu (the Sankore area); Timbuktu becomes a city of learning.
- 1325-1354: Ibn Battuta's great journey across almost all three networks; he writes the *Rihla*.
- c.1310-1333: the Husuni Kubwa palace and Great Mosque of Kilwa (East Africa) are expanded; the Swahili coast peaks.
- c.1337: Mansa Musa dies (death year given variously as 1332/1337).
- 1342: the title "Zamorin" for Calicut's ruler first appears in Ibn Battuta's record.
- 1347-1351: the Black Death spreads along the trade web; perhaps a third of Europe's people die.
- 1368-1369: Ibn Battuta dies.
- 1405-1433: the Ming admiral Zheng He leads giant fleets on seven voyages, reaching East Africa (nationalizing an existing sea network).
- After 1450: European ocean exploration rises, essentially an attempt to bypass the middlemen and tap into this Old World trade web.

## Appendix B: Core Vocabulary

- **Silk Roads** — the overland relay trade network (not one single road)
- **Indian Ocean trade** — the era's largest sea network (monsoon-driven)
- **Trans-Saharan trade** — the gold-for-salt exchange across the Sahara
- **monsoon** — the seasonal wind, the "timetable" of Indian Ocean trade
- **caravan / caravanserai** — the merchant train / the walled roadside inn (safe nodes of the Silk Road and Sahara)
- **dhow** — the triangular-sailed ship (sewn with coconut rope, no iron nails)
- **lateen sail** — the triangular sail (lets a ship zigzag against the wind)
- **compass** — the navigation device that spread out from Chinese ships
- **Pax Mongolica** — the Mongol Peace (the 1200s order that made the Silk Road safer than ever)
- **Sahel** — the grassland edge below the Sahara
- **Swahili coast** — the East African belt of trade city-states (including Kilwa)
- **Mansa** — the Mali emperor's title ("king of kings")
- **hajj** — the Muslim pilgrimage to Mecca
- **diaspora community** — a settlement of merchants living in a foreign port
- **shibosi (市舶司)** — China's office for managing and taxing overseas trade
- **Zamorin** — the title of the ruler of Calicut, India
- **Black Death** — the plague pandemic that spread along the trade web
