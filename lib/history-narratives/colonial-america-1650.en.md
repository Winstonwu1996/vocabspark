# Colonial America Takes Shape · c.1650-1750

> **Lock status**: narrative kernel v0 — pending review
>
> **AP anchor**: APUSH Period 2 (1607-1754), Topics 2.4-2.7
> **Through-line**: how a fragile string of outposts became a finely-tuned machine — a ledger (the slave economy), a pulpit (the Great Awakening), and a face with no name on the ledger (the enslaved).

## 0. Open Your Textbook First (Textbook Anchor)

> This belongs to **APUSH Period 2 (1607-1754)**, Topics 2.4-2.7 — the **origins-of-slavery unit** of AP U.S. History — and to **CA HSS Grade 8.1** (the political, religious, social, and economic foundations of the United States).
>
> Your textbook will tell you:
> - The colonies built a rich **transatlantic trade** in sugar, tobacco, rum, and enslaved people.
> - A huge religious revival called the **First Great Awakening** (about the 1730s-1740s) swept all the colonies.
> - Slavery grew fast, especially in the South.
>
> **What your textbook may NOT make clear** (this is why we need this lesson):
> - **Slavery was not natural and did not appear overnight. It was built step by step — by law.** A 1662 Virginia law (**partus sequitur ventrem** — Latin for "status follows the womb") made slavery inherited through the mother. A 1705 Virginia code locked it into a full racial system. (This is the AP skill of **causation** — a multi-step chain.)
> - **The triangular trade was a SYSTEM, not just one trip.** Rum, sugar, tobacco, and people moved in a money-making circle across the whole Atlantic.
> - **The enslaved were not passive cargo.** In 1739, at the **Stono Rebellion** in South Carolina — the largest slave uprising in British North America — enslaved people beat drums, raised flags, and shouted "**Liberty!**" This is the AP angle called **black agency** (the enslaved as drivers of history).
> - **The piety and the slave-trading were both real at the same time.** Puritans crossed the ocean to build a holy "**city upon a hill**." A century later, one of that land's most profitable businesses was turning people into rum and rum into people. This lesson holds both. It does not pick a final verdict for you.

## 1. One-Sentence Anchor (Many Factions at Once)

In the hundred years from about 1650 to 1750, the English colonies on North America's Atlantic coast grew from a string of fragile outposts into a finely-tuned machine. From the outside, it had three faces. One was a **ledger** (an account book) — sugar, tobacco, rum, and people turning a money-making circle across the Atlantic. One was a **pulpit** — around 1740, a preacher named **George Whitefield** (WIT-field) stood on a hillside in Philadelphia and preached to tens of thousands, and the whole colonies seemed to catch fire. And one face had no name on the ledger:

- **Plantation owners and Atlantic merchants** (actors / the people who profited) — they got rich from the **triangular trade**, and locked their labor force into "property" with one law after another. To them, this century was "prosperity" and "order."
- **Puritans and ordinary churchgoers** (souls caught in the middle) — they crossed the ocean to build a "**city upon a hill**" (a holy model society). A century later, they found their children growing more worldly and money-minded — until the Great Awakening hit like an earthquake, reheating cold faith and splitting the church in two.
- **Enslaved Africans** (receiving-end · DEFAULT) — no contract, no end date, no names left in history. From a 1662 Virginia law on, their children were **born enslaved**. But they were not passive goods. In 1739, by the Stono River in South Carolina, a group beat drums, raised flags, and marched south toward Spanish Florida shouting "**Liberty!**"

In the same century, on the other side of the earth, China's Qing dynasty had just won control of its land: in **1684**, after conquering Taiwan, the **Kangxi** (KAHNG-shee) emperor **lifted the sea ban** and opened four customs ports, and silver poured into China from the Manila galleons (huge Spanish trading ships). The Atlantic world ran its wealth machine by capturing people; the great eastern empire plugged into the **same** world economy through silver, porcelain, and silk — it just turned a different kind of wheel. This time, we start from the layer with no name on the ledger and ask: what was this machine fed with?

## 2. The Stage (Geography / Climate / Society / Institutions / Events)

- **Geography**: The English colonies lined the Atlantic coast — but the real "stage" was not the land. It was the **whole Atlantic Ocean**. The triangular trade tied four lands into a triangle: Europe (manufactured goods, money for rum) → West Africa (captured people) → the Caribbean and North America (sugar, tobacco, rice) → back to Europe. North America was **one corner of the triangle**, not an island apart.
- **Climate / scene**: The hot, wet tobacco and rice fields of the South; the boiling sugar works of the Caribbean; the twenty-odd rum distilleries (places that make rum) of New England's ports running day and night — **Newport** was called "the rum capital of the world." And one hidden stage: the **Middle Passage** (the ship's crossing of the Atlantic). Packed, sick, and chained in the hold, about **12%-15%** of the enslaved did not survive the voyage.
- **Society**: By 1750 the colonies held about **1 million-plus** people, of whom about **240,000 were enslaved** — a jump from a few thousand to a quarter-million in a century, almost all in the South. In some places (like the South Carolina lowlands) the enslaved outnumbered whites. At the top of the pyramid were planters and Atlantic merchants; at the bottom, Africans recorded as "property."
- **Institutions**: Two systems took shape this century —
  - **In the economy**: England's **mercantilism** (a policy that the colonies feed raw materials to the home country and buy its finished goods) plus the **Navigation Acts** (laws that colonial goods move on English ships) locked the colonies into being a supply zone. The **Royal African Company (1672)** once held a monopoly (sole legal control) on English slave trading. After it lost the monopoly around 1698, English slave trading **exploded**, and England became the world's largest slave-trading nation.
  - **In law**: Slavery was not there from the start. It was **built law by law**. In 1662 Virginia passed **partus sequitur ventrem** ("status follows the womb") — a child's enslaved status was inherited from the mother, generation after generation. In 1705, Virginia's **act concerning Servants and Slaves** wrote it down that "non-Christian outsiders = slaves, to be bought and sold." This is how **chattel slavery** (lifelong, inherited, defined by race) was made.
- **Events**: Three pivot events —
  - **Bacon's Rebellion (1676)**: poor whites and enslaved blacks joined to burn the capital, Jamestown — terrifying the elite → speeding up the use of "race" to split poor whites from enslaved blacks.
  - **The First Great Awakening (about the 1730s-1740s)**: Jonathan Edwards and George Whitefield led a religious revival across all the colonies, reheating faith and splitting the church into **New Lights** and **Old Lights**.
  - **The Stono Rebellion (1739)**: the largest slave uprising in British North America — the enslaved were not passive property, but people who picked up weapons and shouted "Liberty."

## 3. The Main Characters

> Three perspectives (actor/perpetrator, in-the-middle mediator, receiving-end), including the most powerless party. High-sensitivity topic — no side is demonized or romanticized.

### The Atlantic merchant / planter (actor / perpetrator) — an English colonial trader working the triangle (c.1700-1740)

A bridging character, but the role is fully real (no name — he stands for a whole class; what is certain is how this kind of merchant/planter lived and decided, not one specific person). He might live in Newport or Charleston. His warehouse holds barrels of rum, bales of tobacco, sacks of rice. He can read, he keeps accounts, he goes to church. One page of his ledger is the rum and sugar going in and out; another page — he tries not to dwell on it — lists the buying and selling prices of "people."

His business runs like this: ship rum from New England to West Africa; trade it for captured people; pack them into the bottom of the hold across the Atlantic (the **Middle Passage**); sell them in the Caribbean and the South for sugar, molasses (the thick syrup left from making sugar), and tobacco; ship the molasses back to New England to distill into rum; start another loop. Each loop earns him money. He does not think he is evil — he thinks he is "doing business, keeping order, feeding his family." After the Royal African Company lost its monopoly around 1698, independent traders like him flooded into the slave trade and business got even better.

He was not yet born during Bacon's Rebellion in 1676, but his whole class remembered the lesson: **never let poor whites and enslaved blacks join hands again**. So he and his peers in the assembly were glad to see laws split "white indentured servants" ever further from "black slaves" — giving poor whites a little privilege of "at least I am white," and pushing all the lifelong bondage onto black people. This perspective lets you stand inside someone who profited, and watch how an order he thought was natural ran — and how law kept tightening it.

### A second-generation Puritan minister (the soul caught in the middle / lonely mediator) — a town congregation's minister in New England (c.1730)

A bridging character with a fully real role (this generation of New England ministers is well documented). His grandparents came on the ship *Arbella* in 1630 with **John Winthrop**, vowing to build in the wilderness a "**city upon a hill**" — a holy model society with all eyes on it. The **covenant** (a sacred agreement) was their root: they believed they had made a covenant with God, and had to live holy lives, or the whole world would see them fail.

But three generations later, he stands at the pulpit and sees a **city gone cold**. The young are busy with farming, business, and saving money. Church membership shrinks. Fewer people can describe the "conversion" experience needed to be a full member. Fearing faith would die out, the elders had created the **Halfway Covenant (1662)**, letting the children of people without a full conversion be baptized — a compromise, and a sign of anxiety. He is caught in the middle: the burning ideal of the ancestors on one side, the increasingly worldly congregation on the other.

Then the earthquake came. In 1739-1740, an English **itinerant** (traveling) preacher, George Whitefield, swept the colonies, preaching outdoors to thousands at once. In 1741, Jonathan Edwards preached "**Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God**," and his congregation wept on the spot. Faith reheated — but it also split. Those who backed the revival were called **New Lights**; those who attacked it as "too emotional, too disorderly" were **Old Lights**, and the church tore in two. This perspective puts you in the middle of everyone: he both longs for that fire and fears it will burn through the order he knows. He speaks for the "soul" face of this century.

### An enslaved African on a Southern plantation (receiving-end · DEFAULT) — a West African captured to a South Carolina rice field (c.1700-1739)

A bridging character, but every detail is anchored to the record (this is the DEFAULT perspective). No exact name — because history left **no names** for most such people, and that, exactly, is part of the problem. What is certain is his situation: in West Africa he had a name, a family, and a skill in growing rice. One day he was captured, sold to coastal slave traders, and chained into the bottom of a ship across the Atlantic (the **Middle Passage**) — weeks packed in a hold too low to stand in, chained, sick, with people dying beside him and thrown into the sea. About **12%-15%** did not survive the crossing.

Ashore, he was sold at auction. The buyer wrote him in the ledger beside the livestock and the tools — a piece of "property," not a person. No contract, no term, no end. The cruelest part was that 1662 law (partus sequitur ventrem): from the moment his children were born, they were enslaved — bondage became something **inherited**. The irony: the great wealth South Carolina made from rice came largely from the rice-growing and water-managing knowledge he carried from West Africa. His skill made the owner rich; he himself had nothing.

But he was not passive cargo. On September 9, 1739, by the Stono River, a group of enslaved people (many from Angola/Kongo, possibly soldiers in their homeland) took up weapons, beat drums, raised flags, and marched south toward Spanish-held Florida — which promised freedom to those who escaped — shouting "**Liberty!**" This was the largest slave uprising in British North America. It was crushed, and South Carolina then passed the 1740 Negro Act to tighten control. This perspective is the heart of the lesson: the enslaved were never "objects waiting to be rescued." They were people who resisted, fled, and shouted for freedom. It also makes you face the hardest part — this "prosperity machine" was fed with lives like his, counted as less than human.

## 4. The Story (Plot Beats)

1. **A fragile outpost becomes a money machine (c.1650).** A century earlier, the colonies were a string of outposts kept alive by England's supplies, always near starvation. By around 1650, the South had found its money-printing machine — cash crops (tobacco, rice) — and lacked only one thing: a huge, cheap, never-leaving labor force.
2. **The triangular trade takes shape (later 1600s).** The Atlantic world tied Europe's goods, West Africa's people, and the Americas' sugar and tobacco into a money-making triangle. New England found its place: distill the Caribbean's molasses into **rum** (Newport became the "rum capital," with dozens of distilleries), then ship the rum to West Africa for people. North America was not on the side of the triangle — it **was one corner** of it.
3. **The Royal African Company and the explosion of slave trading (1672 → 1698).** England chartered the Royal African Company in 1672 with a monopoly on slave trading. After it lost the monopoly around 1698, independent traders flooded in, England's slave trading soared, and it became the world's largest slave-trading nation. The number of enslaved people sent to North America rose sharply.
4. **Bacon's Rebellion (1676) — the elite's fear.** In Virginia, poor whites (many former indentured servants who never got good land) and enslaved blacks joined together — angry at Governor Berkeley and wanting Native land to the west — and burned the capital, Jamestown. Poor whites and enslaved blacks united: the elite's worst nightmare.
5. **From indentured servitude to racial slavery (1662-1705) — law builds slavery step by step.** This did not happen overnight: 1662 Virginia partus sequitur ventrem (status follows the mother, bondage inherited) → a chain of laws giving poor whites privileges and stripping black people of rights → 1705 Virginia's act concerning Servants and Slaves writing racial slavery into a full code. Bacon's Rebellion was the catalyst (the trigger) of this legal chain.
6. **The Middle Passage and turning "people" into "property."** The enslaved crossing had a very high death rate (about 12%-15%); ashore, they were auctioned and recorded as movable property. Across the whole 16th-19th centuries, about **10 million-12.5 million** Africans were forced across the Atlantic (the share going to British North America was one part of this — most went to the Caribbean and Brazil).
7. **The First Great Awakening (about the 1730s-1740s) — an earthquake of the soul.** Jonathan Edwards started a revival in Northampton (1734-35) and preached "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" in 1741; George Whitefield toured the colonies in 1739-40, preaching outdoors to thousands (a Philadelphia crowd of around ten thousand; even the skeptical, money-counting **Benjamin Franklin** was stunned — and later printed Whitefield's sermons). For the first time, one event swept **all** the colonies at once — many historians say it gave the scattered colonies their first "shared experience."
8. **The church tears in two (New Lights vs Old Lights).** The New Lights backed the revival, stressing personal conversion, emotion, and each person facing God directly; the Old Lights disliked it as emotional and disruptive. Gilbert Tennent's 1741 sermon "The Danger of an Unconverted Ministry" set off a split in the Presbyterian church. The revival also spawned new colleges (such as the future Princeton).
9. **The Stono Rebellion (1739) — black agency.** By the Stono River in South Carolina, a group of the enslaved — starting around twenty and growing to dozens — beat drums, raised flags, and marched toward Florida shouting "Liberty," in the largest slave uprising in British North America. After it was crushed, South Carolina passed the 1740 Negro Act, tightening control (limiting literacy and gatherings).
10. **A machine running ever more smoothly (c.1750).** By the mid-1700s, the three regions were set, the triangular trade flourished, racial slavery was written into law, and religion after the Great Awakening was both hotter and more divided. On the surface: prosperity, piety, self-government. Underneath: a system fed with captured lives.

**An irony to keep:** The Puritans crossed the ocean to build a "city upon a hill" with all eyes on it (a moral model). A century later, one of that land's most profitable businesses was distilling people into rum and using rum to buy people. Piety and slave-trading were both real on the same soil.

## 5. Reading the Documents (Primary Sources)

### A. Virginia's 1662 law: status follows the mother (partus sequitur ventrem)
> Original (excerpt): "Whereas some doubts have arisen whether children got by any Englishman upon a negro woman should be slave or free, Be it therefore enacted... that all children born in this country shall be held bond or free only according to the condition of the mother."
> Plain reading: People weren't sure — is the child of an Englishman and an African woman a slave or free? This law decided: the child's status, slave or free, follows the **mother**.
> Many-sided reading: It flipped England's old tradition of "status follows the father," and the goal was cold — a child of an enslaver and an enslaved woman would still be a slave, still the owner's property. It is the legal source of "inherited slavery," the most crucial building block of chattel slavery.

### B. Virginia's 1705 "An act concerning Servants and Slaves"
> Original (excerpt): "All servants imported and brought into this country, by sea or land, who were not Christians in their native country... shall be accounted and be slaves, and as such be here bought and sold notwithstanding a conversion to Christianity afterwards."
> Plain reading: People brought from overseas who were not Christians at home count as slaves and can be bought and sold — even if they convert to Christianity later.
> Reading: It fixed enslaved status by **origin and race**, and closed off the path of "convert and be freed." Together, 1662 and 1705 are the black-and-white evidence of the turn from indentured servitude to racial slavery — slavery was **built by law**, not fallen from the sky.

### C. Jonathan Edwards, "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" (1741 sermon)
> Original (excerpt): "The God that holds you over the pit of hell, much as one holds a spider, or some loathsome insect over the fire, abhors you, and is dreadfully provoked..."
> Plain reading: God holds you over the pit of hell the way someone holds a spider over a fire — one moment from letting go.
> Reading: This is the most famous sermon of the Great Awakening. It worked not by reasoning but by **emotional shock** — making listeners feel fear, weep, and seek conversion on the spot. This "straight to the individual heart" style is the mark of the New Lights revival — and exactly what the Old Lights hated most.

### D. Benjamin Franklin on George Whitefield preaching in Philadelphia (1739, recalled in his *Autobiography*)
> The account as it is passed down: Franklin doubted Whitefield could be heard clearly by so huge a crowd, so he walked to the edge of the crowd to measure the distance, and estimated Whitefield could be heard clearly by **twenty or thirty thousand** people. He had planned to give nothing — and ended up emptying his pockets of copper, silver, and gold.
> Plain reading: Even the rational, money-counting, not-very-religious Franklin was so moved by Whitefield that he emptied his pockets.
> Reading: Note the layers — Franklin is a **firsthand eyewitness** (high reliability), but the crowd figure (twenty or thirty thousand) is his own **estimate**, not a head count. This source is powerful because it proves, from a skeptic's view, how strong the Great Awakening felt in person — even the least believing could not resist it.

## 6. Consequences (Short / Medium / Long Term)

- **Short term (c.1750):** The triangular trade flourished; racial slavery was written into Southern law and the enslaved population soared (about 27,000 in 1700 → about 240,000 in 1750); the Great Awakening made religion both hotter and more divided (New Lights / Old Lights) and spawned new colleges.
- **Medium term (later 1700s → the eve of revolution):** ① The Great Awakening planted two seeds — the idea that "each person can face God directly, without going through existing authority" loosened obedience to worldly authority too, which some historians see as a distant groundwork for revolutionary thinking; it was also the first event to sweep **all** the colonies at once, giving them a "shared experience." ② Racial slavery and the triangular trade bound the Southern economy together, a line that ran all the way to the 19th-century Civil War.
- **Long term (to today):** This century laid the legal foundation of American racial slavery (the "blood decides status" logic set by partus 1662 and the 1705 code), with consequences running through the Civil War, Reconstruction, and race today; and the American evangelical religious culture shaped by the Great Awakening is still a key to understanding American society. The story of "prosperity / piety / freedom" and the question "who was all this built on" are one of the central tensions of American history education.

## 7. AP DBQ Thinking Practice

- **Stakeholder grid:** Atlantic merchant / Southern planter / New England minister / ordinary churchgoer / indentured servant / enslaved African / Native peoples / the home country, England — between 1650 and 1750, what did each want, and what cards did each hold in the triangular trade, the legal turn, and the Great Awakening?
- **Causation (the core skill):** Slavery **was not natural**. Lay out the chain: cash crops' need for labor → indentured servants too few / they expire → Bacon's Rebellion (1676) exposes the "poor white + enslaved black" threat → law divides by race (1662 partus → 1705 code) → chattel slavery is built. This is the multi-step causation AP rewards most.
- **Black agency:** The Stono Rebellion (1739) shows the enslaved as drivers of history, not passive cargo. At the same time, face the cost: agency met brutal suppression and the tightening 1740 Negro Act.
- **Continuity & change:** From mostly indentured servitude in 1650 to racial slavery in 1750 — what changed completely (term → lifelong, class line → race line, sellable → inheritable), and what stayed the same (the structure of the bottom feeding the top)?
- **The two sides of the Awakening (complexity):** It was both a sincere revival AND a cause of division (the church split, society split); it is called both a distant groundwork for revolution AND criticized as over-emotional. AP asks you to hold both, not to flatten it.

## 8. China in the Same Era (Structural Comparison · Rule 0 veto dimension · connects to the live trade-networks lesson)

China from about 1650 to 1750 was the Qing dynasty, from its entry through the Great Wall (1644) into the early "High Qing" of the Kangxi-Yongzheng-Qianlong reigns. Here we set side by side **two ways of plugging into the world economy** in the same span on two sides of the earth — and this **connects directly to the already-live trade-networks lesson**: the Atlantic triangular trade was a **new corner** that the "world economy web" grew in the early modern age.

**Connecting to trade-networks: how the Atlantic plugged into the world economy**
- trade-networks covered the 1200-1450 Silk Roads / Indian Ocean / trans-Saharan web — three old networks. This lesson's triangular trade is a **brand-new stretch** of that web, grown after 1500 when the Atlantic was opened and the Americas were plugged in. The same web grew a new corner — and that new corner was driven by **forced, captured labor**, the most jarring difference from the old networks.

**The same-era China (late Ming / early Qing) maritime-trade comparison (side by side, no ranking):**
- **How each plugged into the world web:** the Atlantic world plugged in through **capturing people + plantations** (treating people themselves as trade goods); China in the same era plugged in by **exporting porcelain, silk, and tea for silver**. From the late Ming into the early Qing, silver from the Americas (via the Manila galleons) and from Japan poured into China — China was a **huge absorbing end** of the global silver web. Both were deeply embedded in the same early-modern world economy, but one made goods of people and the other drew in silver.
- **The state's stance toward the sea:** the early Qing once enforced a strict sea ban (to starve out the Zheng regime on Taiwan); **in 1684, after conquering Taiwan, the Kangxi emperor lifted the sea ban**, opened customs offices in four places (Fujian, Guangdong, Zhejiang, Jiangsu), allowed private overseas trade, and welcomed foreign merchants into port. In the same span, England used the Navigation Acts and the Royal African Company to use **state power to drive** transatlantic (including slave-trading) commerce. Both states managed and used the sea, but with different aims and tools: one collected customs and kept order, one made slave trading into a state-level business.
- **Who counts as a full person:** on the Atlantic side, slavery sorted some people by race into "movable property," inheritable and sellable; in the same-era Qing, the bottom were tenant farmers, hired laborers, and the legally "mean people" (jianji, such as yuehu and duomin) — this was "unfreedom," but not by skin color, not captured across an ocean, and not in principle locked across generations as sellable property. The two kinds of "unfreedom" differ in nature and **cannot be simply mapped onto each other**.

Rule 0: We cannot say the Atlantic world was "advanced" and the Qing "backward," nor the reverse. Each answered its own situation, each with its own logic and its own blind spots. This section only sets two corners of the same world economy web, in the same span, side by side. (The China part uses real Chinese terms: Kangxi, sea ban, silver, Manila galleons, jianji, tenant farmers — this is the real China.)

## 9. What Historians Argue About (Historiography)

- **The racial-formation reading (Edmund Morgan and others):** in Virginia, "freedom" and "slavery" were made at the same time — giving poor whites freedom (to defuse the class threat of Bacon's Rebellion) was achieved precisely by nailing black people into slavery. "American freedom" and "American slavery" are twins.
- **Atlantic history reading:** sees the North American colonies inside the **whole Atlantic system** — not an isolated "pre-history of America," but part of a Europe-Africa-Americas economic and human network bound together. The triangular trade and Middle Passage are central here.
- **The Great Awakening debate:** the traditional view (seeing it as a "spiritual rehearsal" for revolution) stresses how it loosened authority and gave the colonies a shared experience; the revisionist view (Jon Butler) questions whether "the Great Awakening" is a single unified label invented later, arguing the revivals were actually scattered and disconnected. This is a great AP-level argument over whether a concept itself holds up.
- **Black agency reading:** centers uprisings like Stono, everyday resistance, and cultural survival, refusing to write the enslaved as only passive victims.
- **A neutral synthesis:** colonial America was both a real land of "opportunity / piety / prosperity" (for some) AND a real machine of "capture and extraction" (for others) — both true at once, two faces of one structure, not either/or.

## 10. Common Misconceptions (Myth-Busting)

1. ❌ "Slavery was there from the start of the colonies and was natural." → ✅ The early workforce was mostly indentured servants (many white); lifelong, race-based chattel slavery was **built law by law** in the later 1600s (1662 partus → 1705 code, with Bacon's Rebellion 1676 as catalyst).
2. ❌ "The triangular trade was just a shipping route." → ✅ It was a whole **economic system**: rum, sugar, tobacco, and people circling the Atlantic, tying New England's distilleries, the South's plantations, West Africa's slave ports, and the Caribbean's sugar works into one loop.
3. ❌ "The enslaved were all passive victims waiting to be freed." → ✅ The Stono Rebellion (1739) was the largest slave uprising in British North America; the enslaved resisted, fled, and shouted "Liberty" — black agency is an argument AP rewards.
4. ❌ "The colonists were all pious Puritans seeking religious freedom." → ✅ Only some were; and the second and third generations grew more worldly — it was precisely because faith had cooled that the Great Awakening "re-lit" it (the 1662 Halfway Covenant is evidence of the anxiety).
5. ❌ "The Great Awakening only unified and deepened faith, with no side effects." → ✅ It both lit faith and gave the colonies a shared experience AND split the church into New Lights / Old Lights. Both at once.
6. ❌ "partus / the 1705 code were just minor local matters." → ✅ The logic of "status follows the mother, bondage inherited, race decides status" is the legal bedrock of American racial slavery, with consequences running to the Civil War and today.
7. ❌ "Most captured Africans went to the North American colonies." → ✅ The whole transatlantic slave trade was about 10 million-12.5 million people; **most went to the Caribbean and Brazil**, with the share to British North America smaller (though their descendants formed the bulk of American slavery).

## 11. Thinking Questions (Open-Ended, No Conclusion)

1. Slavery did not fall from the sky — it was built law by law (1662 status follows the mother → 1705 code). If a cruel system was built up "legally," step by step, does the word "legal" still make it just?
2. Early indentured servants were both white and black, and at first the line was "poor vs rich." After Bacon's Rebellion it was redrawn as "white vs black." Why, and how, was a line redrawn from "class" into "race" — and who gained from the new line?
3. The Puritans crossed the ocean to build a moral "city upon a hill," and a century later one of that city's most profitable businesses was slave-trading. On the same soil, the highest ideal and the deepest exploitation were both real at once — what does this tell you about the relationship between "ideal" and "reality"?
4. In the Great Awakening, Whitefield told ordinary people: you can face God **directly**, without going through any existing authority. Besides religion, where else (politics, for example) might "no need to go through authority" grow?
5. This time you mostly heard the one with no name on the ledger — the West African captured to the rice field. How would the Atlantic merchant tell this machine? How would the Puritan minister see this "pious yet slave-trading" land? Whose voice have you not heard yet?
6. In the same span, the Atlantic world plugged into the world economy by capturing people, while China plugged into the same web by exporting porcelain and silk and drawing in silver. Where did each way of plugging in lead? Setting them side by side, do you see better-and-worse, or two different situations?

## Appendix A: Key Timeline

- 1662: New England's Halfway Covenant, responding to shrinking church membership.

- 1675-76: King Philip's War / Metacom's War (New England Native resistance).
- 1676: Bacon's Rebellion (Virginia); Jamestown burned on September 19; Bacon dies October 26 (dysentery).
- 1684: After conquering Taiwan, the Kangxi emperor lifts the sea ban and opens four customs offices (China background, connects to trade-networks).
- 1698: The Royal African Company's monopoly is broken; English slave trading explodes.
- 1705: Virginia's "An act concerning Servants and Slaves" — racial slavery written into a full code.
- 1734-35: Jonathan Edwards starts a revival in Northampton.
- 1739: George Whitefield begins touring (Philadelphia and elsewhere) from November (the height of the Awakening begins).
- 1739.9.9: Stono Rebellion (South Carolina) — the largest slave uprising in British North America.
- 1740: South Carolina passes the Negro Act, tightening control of the enslaved.
- 1741: Edwards preaches "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God"; Gilbert Tennent preaches "The Danger of an Unconverted Ministry," setting off the New/Old Lights split.
- c.1750: Colonial population about 1 million-plus; the enslaved about 240,000.

## Appendix B: Core Vocabulary

- **triangular trade** — the Europe-West Africa-Americas trade loop
- **Middle Passage** — the transatlantic slave-ship crossing (death rate about 12%-15%)
- **chattel slavery** — slavery as movable property (lifelong, inherited, by race)
- **partus sequitur ventrem** — "status follows the womb" (1662; the legal source of inheritable slavery)
- **indentured servitude** — labor under a time-limited contract, with a chance to start over
- **Royal African Company** — England's slave-trade monopoly company (1672)
- **mercantilism / Navigation Acts** — the policy / laws tying colonies to the home economy
- **Bacon's Rebellion** — Bacon's uprising (1676), the catalyst of the turn to racial slavery
- **Stono Rebellion** — Stono uprising (1739), the largest slave revolt in British North America
- **Great Awakening** — the First Great Awakening (religious revival, about the 1730s-1740s)
- **New Lights / Old Lights** — pro-revival vs anti-revival factions in the church
- **Puritan / covenant / Halfway Covenant** — the Puritans / the sacred agreement / the 1662 compromise
- **city upon a hill** — Winthrop's 1630 Puritan ideal
