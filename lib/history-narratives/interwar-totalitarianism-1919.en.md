# The Interwar Years & the Rise of Totalitarianism · 1919-1939

> **Lock status**: narrative kernel v0 — pending review
>
> **AP anchor**: AP World History Modern, Unit 7.3-7.5 (Global Conflict)
> **Through-line**: how, in some of the world's most literate nations, ordinary people handed their freedom to dictators — often with their own votes
> **Difficulty**: 5/5 (high emotional + ethical weight; the goal is to understand the mechanism without ever excusing the atrocities)
> **Expected duration**: 35-45 min

---

## 0. Open Your Textbook First (Textbook Anchor)

> This belongs to **AP World History Modern, Unit 7 (1900-present · Global Conflict)** — Topics 7.3-7.5.
>
> Your textbook will tell you:
> - **World War I ended in 1918.** The **Interwar Years** are the roughly twenty years between the two world wars (1919-1939).
> - **The Treaty of Versailles (1919)** ended WWI and punished Germany.
> - **The Great Depression (started 1929)** was a worldwide economic collapse.
> - **Dictators rose:** Mussolini in Italy, Hitler in Germany, Stalin in the USSR, and the military in Japan.
>
> **What your textbook may rush past** (this is why we need this lesson):
> - **Most textbooks jump in one sentence from "Treaty of Versailles" to "Hitler invaded Poland."** Those twenty years in between — how resentment built, how the Depression set the world on fire, how democracy fell one country after another — are the biggest **cause-and-effect gap** between WWI and WWII. This lesson fills that gap.
> - **The real AP question is not "what year did Hitler take power."** It is the more unsettling one: **how did people, in some of the most literate countries on earth, hand their freedom to dictators — often with legal votes and cheering crowds?**
> - **Totalitarianism caused real catastrophe** — the Great Purge, the Holodomor, and the road to WWII and genocide. We never glamorize it. But we also refuse to flatten it into "a few bad men took over," because then you would never learn to spot the warning signs the next time.
> - **The AP angle is causation:** why did totalitarian regimes appear in **multiple countries at the same time** in the 1930s? Chain together resentment, the Depression, fear, institutional weakness, and the strongman. That is the core skill of this lesson.

## 1. One-Sentence Anchor (Many Factions at Once)

In **1919**, World War I had just ended. More than nine million soldiers were dead. Four empires (German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, Ottoman) had collapsed. Everyone called it "the war to end all wars." **And yet twenty years later, an even bigger war broke out.** Those twenty years — 1919 to 1939 — are the **Interwar Years**. The real question is not "why was there a Second World War." It is the more disturbing one: **in some of the most cultured, most literate countries in the world, how did people hand their own freedom to dictators, step by step, often with votes and cheers?**

This twenty-year collapse was squeezed out of several groups in very different moods:

- **Ordinary Germans on the losing side.** When they signed the **Treaty of Versailles** (vehr-SY) in 1919, they felt deeply humiliated — blamed for "causing the war," stripped of territory, and ordered to pay an enormous bill. Ten years later the Depression wiped out their jobs and savings overnight. A man who blamed it all on "traitors and foreigners" and promised to make Germany great again grew more and more appealing.
- **Italian war veterans and the middle class.** Italy was on the winning side but gained almost nothing. Its economy was a mess; the streets were full of strikes and violence; many feared a "red revolution" like Russia's. A man in a black shirt promising order and glory — **Mussolini** (moo-soh-LEE-nee) — began to look like a savior.
- **Soviet workers and peasants.** In 1917 they had just overthrown the tsar and the old world, promised a new society of equals. After **Stalin** (STAH-lin) took power, he used "Five-Year Plans" to force a farming country into being an industrial power — at the cost of millions of lives in famine and purges.
- **Japan's young officers and countryside.** Japan was on the winning side and now counted as a great power, yet the Depression ruined its farms and threw city workers out of work. A group of young officers who believed "the civilian government is weak and parliament is corrupt" came to think only the army and overseas expansion could save Japan — and they looked toward China.

At the very same moment, on the other side of the earth, China was living through its own storm: on **May 4, 1919**, students in Beijing took to the streets over **the very same Treaty of Versailles** (which handed Germany's rights in Shandong to Japan). **The same global crisis pushed China toward national awakening, and pushed Europe and Japan toward totalitarianism.** Two faces of one era. Outside, an age was winding the spring for an even greater disaster.

## 2. The Stage (Geography / Economy / Society / Institutions / Events)

- **Geography**: This is a **global** stage, not one country. The key nodes: a defeated, dismembered **Germany** (the Weimar Republic, capital Berlin); a victorious but bitter **Italy** (Rome); a **Soviet Union** (Moscow) just emerging from revolution and civil war; and a rising island nation, **Japan** (Tokyo), with its eyes on **Manchuria** (northeast China). The invisible thread linking them all is the **global economy**.
- **The economic "climate"**: In this lesson the "climate" is not weather — it is the **economy**. The 1920s had a brief "Roaring Twenties" boom, but the foundation was thin: Germany paid its reparations with money borrowed from America; America's confidence rested on a stock-market frenzy. In **October 1929**, the **Wall Street Crash** hit (Black Thursday, Oct 24; Black Tuesday, Oct 29). Because the world economy was tied together by the **gold standard** (a money system pegged to gold), when America sneezed, the world caught a cold: this was the **Great Depression**. By 1932, about one in four American workers had no job, and German unemployment reached about **six million**. **Hunger and despair are the best fertilizer for totalitarianism.**
- **Society**: A postwar generation carried deep wounds — the "Lost Generation." Veterans could not find work and felt their sacrifice had been wasted. The middle class saw its savings wiped out twice — first by inflation (Germany suffered **hyperinflation in 1923**, when money became nearly worthless), then by the Depression. The streets were full of strikes, marches, and armed clashes between the political left and right. In all that "nothing is safe anymore" anxiety, a strongman promising "I can make everything orderly again" looked more attractive than a reasoning democratic politician.
- **Institutions**: This is the most important part of the lesson — **the weak spot inside democracy itself**. Germany's Weimar constitution had an **Article 48**, which let the president issue decrees directly, bypassing parliament, during an "emergency." Meant for crises, it later became the **tool that dismantled democracy**. Totalitarianism rarely fell from the sky. It often climbed **a staircase of legal steps**: take power legally → create or use a crisis → seize special powers in the name of "emergency" → get parliament to "voluntarily" hand over its lawmaking power. Understanding that staircase is the heart of this lesson.
- **Events**: Four nearly simultaneous threads — Italy's **March on Rome (October 1922)** → the USSR's **First Five-Year Plan (from 1928) and Great Purge (1936-1938)** → Germany's **Hitler appointed chancellor (January 30, 1933) → Reichstag fire → Enabling Act** → Japan's **Mukden Incident (September 18, 1931) and the seizure of Manchuria**. They were not isolated. Behind each lay the same mechanism: crisis + resentment + fear + a strongman promising order.

## 3. The Main Characters

> Three perspectives (actor, lonely mediator, receiving-end), including at least one powerless party. The main characters here are NOT a particular dictator — they are **the ordinary people who let the dictator rise**, and **those who tried to stop it and failed**.

### The Strongman (actor) — "the man who promised to make the nation great again"

This character is not one specific dictator. It is the **same kind of figure** who appeared again and again in several countries in the 1920s-30s: Mussolini, Hitler, Stalin, the Japanese military leadership — different in their details, but sharing one playbook. What is certain is the playbook itself. He appears at people's most desperate moment — joblessness, inflation, street chaos, fear of the future. He wins hearts not with complex policy but with **emotion**: he names an "enemy" (foreigners, traitors, Jews, rich peasants, communists, weak civilian politicians — who, exactly, differs by country) and tells the suffering "your pain is not your fault — *they* did this to you." He promises order, glory, revival. With uniforms, flags, rallies, and radio, he creates the thrill of "at last we are one whole."

His real "genius" is not in ideas but in **reading the moment and the cracks in the system**. Mussolini did not actually conquer Rome in 1922 — he bluffed, and the king, to avoid bloodshed, *invited* him to be prime minister (one of history's famous political bluffs: he seized power not by defeating the state, but because the state refused to defend itself). Hitler in 1933 was **legally appointed** chancellor, and only then dismantled democracy step by step. This perspective lets a student see, from inside the perpetrator, that totalitarianism rarely comes by an overnight coup — it more often climbs a legal staircase, carried by the crowd's own cheers. **That is exactly what makes it so frightening.**

### The Democrat Who Tried to Stop It (lonely mediator) — a politician in Weimar Germany trying to hold the republic together

A bridging character, but the situation is fully real. He is a moderate democratic member of parliament in Weimar Germany in the 1920s-30s, who believes in parliament, in law, in reasoning things through. He has no specific name, because he stands for a whole generation that **watched its own republic fall and could not stop it**. What is certain is the dilemma he faced: on the left, radicals who wanted a Soviet-style revolution; on the right, Nazis and nationalists who wanted to smash parliament. Both kept armed gangs in the streets, fighting each other. The rule-following democrats in the middle were squeezed thinner and thinner.

He faced democracy's cruelest paradox: **democracy lets people vote for those who want to destroy democracy.** In 1932, at the depth of the Depression, the Nazis became the largest party in parliament — not by a coup, but by votes. He and his colleagues tried again and again to form governments, to compromise, to use constitutional tools to block Hitler. But the economy was collapsing, the streets were burning, the voters were furious, and his words — "patience," "procedure," "compromise" — sounded thin against hunger. In **March 1933**, when the **Enabling Act** came to a vote, he was among the minority who voted *no* — but there were far too few. Months later, opposition parties were banned, and many like him went into exile, into prison, or into silence. This perspective puts a student in the place of "the gatekeeper of the system" and raises the heaviest question: **when most people, out of fear and anger, want a strongman, can the rules themselves hold him back?**

### The Ordinary Person Who Voted "Yes" — or Just Stayed Silent (receiving-end · DEFAULT) — a young man in an ordinary German family during the Depression

A bridging character, with a fully real role (this is the DEFAULT perspective, and the most important one in this lesson). He has no exact name, because the point is not "who he was" but that "he could be anyone — he could be us." What is certain is his situation: a young man in an ordinary German small town in the early 1930s. His father served at the front in WWI and came home feeling the country had been "stabbed in the back." After 1929 his father lost his job, and the family lived on potatoes and bread; the little money saved before 1923 had long since been burned to nothing by inflation. He is not an evil man. He does not even care much about politics. He is just cold, hungry, afraid, and sees no future.

Then he starts going to the rallies. There are flags, music, and the belonging of thousands of people just like him shouting together; the man on the stage tells him: none of this is your fault — it is Versailles, the traitors, *them* — and I will make Germany great again. For the first time, he feels he is not a lonely failure but part of something great. He votes. Or — more commonly — he does nothing at all, simply does not object, telling himself "all politicians are the same," "it'll settle down once things stabilize," "it's not my place to get involved."

This perspective is the heart of the lesson, and its heaviest, most valuable part. AP World keeps testing the **causes and agency** of totalitarianism's rise: it was not forced by a few demons onto a passive mass — its fuel came precisely from the choices, and the non-choices, of millions of frightened, angry, or merely exhausted and silent ordinary people. This perspective lets a student feel, from the most ordinary, most "innocent" position: **freedom is rarely snatched away in one grab — far more often it is handed over, a little at a time, by ordinary people in fear.** This is not so we can judge the people of the past. It is so we can recognize that voice — the next time it speaks.

## 4. The Story (Plot Beats)

1. **The ruins of war and a treaty of resentment (1918-1919).** WWI ended; about nine million soldiers were dead; four empires had collapsed. On **June 28, 1919**, the victors made Germany sign the **Treaty of Versailles** in the Hall of Mirrors at the Palace of Versailles. Its **Article 231** (the "war guilt clause") made Germany accept full responsibility for causing the war, and on that basis demanded enormous reparations (the 1921 London Schedule set the figure at about **132 billion gold marks**), plus the loss of German land and colonies. Germans called it a "Diktat" (a dictated command). Resentment was planted at that moment.
2. **A fragile prosperity (the 1920s).** The "Roaring Twenties" looked lively but rested on thin ground: Germany paid reparations with borrowed American money. In **1923 Germany also suffered hyperinflation** — a loaf of bread cost a wheelbarrow of cash, and the middle class's savings vanished. That memory gave Germans a deep, bone-level fear of "chaos."
3. **Italy: the first fascist regime (1922).** Victorious-but-bitter Italy was full of strikes and left-right street violence, and many feared "red revolution." Mussolini built his Blackshirts and launched the **March on Rome** in October 1922. It was really a bluff, but **King Victor Emmanuel III, to avoid bloodshed, appointed him prime minister on October 30**. After the anti-fascist deputy **Matteotti** (mah-teh-OH-tee) exposed election fraud, he was assassinated (June 1924); in January 1925 Mussolini openly declared dictatorship. Fascism now had its first model.
4. **The USSR: building a great power out of steel and famine (1928-1933).** Stalin launched the **First Five-Year Plan** (from 1928), forcing peasants into collective farms and pouring everything into heavy industry. The cost was terrible: forced collectivization and grain seizures caused mass famine, including **Ukraine's Holodomor (1932-1933)**, with an estimated millions of deaths across the USSR. The Soviet Union did turn from a farming country into an industrial power — but that "achievement" was paid for with countless lives.
5. **The Great Depression: the match that set the world on fire (1929).** In October 1929 the Wall Street stock market crashed. Because the gold standard tied the world's economies together, the crisis spread fast. Germany was hit hardest — once America pulled back its loans, the German economy collapsed, with unemployment reaching about **six million** by 1932. In their despair, voters began abandoning the moderate center.
6. **Germany: how a democracy handed itself over (1930-1933).** During the Depression, the Nazi Party became the largest party in parliament — by votes. On **January 30, 1933, President Hindenburg legally appointed Hitler chancellor.** This is the key point: he did not come to power by a coup.
7. **The Reichstag fire and the "emergency" seizure (Feb-Mar 1933).** On the night of **February 27, 1933**, the **Reichstag** (RYKHS-tahg, the German parliament building) caught fire. The Nazis immediately blamed the communists. The next day Hindenburg, using constitutional **Article 48**, issued the **Reichstag Fire Decree**, suspending free speech, assembly, the press, and other civil liberties in one stroke. "Emergency" had become the key to power.
8. **The Enabling Act: democracy's suicide (March 23, 1933).** In an atmosphere of intimidation and arrests, parliament passed the **Enabling Act**, transferring lawmaking power from parliament to Hitler's cabinet — letting the government make laws without parliament, even against the constitution. **Democracy voted itself out of existence using its own procedures.** Within months, other parties were banned and Germany became a one-party dictatorship.
9. **Japan: the military overpowers the civilians (1931-1936).** The Depression hammered Japan's countryside. On **September 18, 1931**, the Kwantung Army staged the **Mukden Incident** — it blew up its own railway and blamed China, using that as a pretext to seize Manchuria. In 1932 it set up the puppet state of **Manchukuo** (mahn-CHOO-kwoh), installing the last Qing emperor, Puyi, as a figurehead. The civilian government in Tokyo could not control the army in the field. A prime minister was assassinated in 1932, and in 1936 the **February 26 Incident** (an attempted military coup) showed the army steadily overpowering the civilians — Japan slid into militarism.
10. **The common recipe of totalitarianism (running throughout).** Lay the four threads side by side and the same mechanism appears: ① a crisis (defeat / inflation / Depression / rural ruin); ② resentment and fear (the humiliation of Versailles / fear of communist revolution / fear of chaos); ③ a strongman who names an "enemy" and promises order and glory; ④ seizing special powers in the name of "emergency," often by climbing legal steps; ⑤ using propaganda, rallies, and a single party to manufacture an excited "unity" while crushing all dissent.
11. **Sliding toward the abyss (1936-1939).** The three fascist/militarist regimes (Germany, Italy, Japan) moved toward alliance and expansion: Germany tore up Versailles, rearmed, and annexed Austria and Czech lands; Japan launched a full invasion of China with the **Marco Polo Bridge Incident (July 7, 1937)**. In September 1939, Germany invaded Poland and World War II began. The Interwar Years ended — meant as a breathing space after "the war to end all wars," they had instead been the run-up to an even greater catastrophe.
12. **The cost and the lesson (looking back).** Totalitarianism promised order and revival; what it delivered was the largest war and the worst atrocities in history (the Great Purge, the Holodomor, then WWII and genocide). This lesson draws no "who was right" verdict for the student, but it makes one fact crystal clear: **the entrance to these catastrophes was often not a violent coup, but ordinary people in a crisis handing their freedom over, step by step — sometimes by voting for it.**

**An irony to keep:** the war called "the war to end all wars" produced a treaty whose resentment became the seed of an even greater war. And Germany — one of the most literate, most cultured nations in Europe — saw its democracy ended not by a foreign enemy, but by its own constitutional procedures and its own votes.

## 5. Reading the Documents (Primary Sources)

### A. Treaty of Versailles, Article 231 — the "War Guilt Clause" (1919)
> Original (common English excerpt): "...Germany accepts the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage... as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies."
> Plain reading: "Germany accepts that it and its allies are to blame for causing this war and all the loss and damage it caused."
> Many-sided reading: the victors needed a legal basis to demand payment. But to ordinary Germans, this clause said in black and white, "all of this is your fault." A line of legal text became the spark for years of resentment and a thirst for revenge — it did not start the next war, but it fed those who wanted to.

### B. The Weimar Constitution, Article 48 (1919)
> Content in brief: when public safety and order are seriously disturbed or threatened, the president may take the measures needed to restore order, using armed force if necessary, and may temporarily suspend certain basic civil rights.
> Plain reading: the constitution gave the president an "emergency button" — in a crisis he could bypass parliament and suspend some freedoms.
> Reading: this article was designed for emergencies, and its authors meant well. But its most dangerous feature is one question: **who decides what counts as an "emergency"?** In 1933, by claiming "the Reichstag fire is an emergency," civil liberties were suspended overnight. An article meant to protect democracy became a tool to tear it down — the lesson most worth remembering here: **be wary of anyone who demands special powers in the name of "emergency."**

### C. The Enabling Act (March 23, 1933)
> Content in brief: it authorized Hitler's cabinet to make laws without parliament, even against the constitution, for a period of four years (later renewed again and again).
> Plain reading: parliament voted to pass a law whose content was "we hand the power to make laws to the government, and we no longer need to take part."
> Reading: this is one of the most astonishing political moments in human history — **a democratic parliament, by a completely legal vote, ended itself.** It tells us that the fall of democracy need not come with tanks and gunfire; it can happen in a chamber with the constitution on the wall, by men in suits raising their hands.

### D. A widely circulated reflection (note: often attributed to Martin Niemöller — a POSTWAR reflection in many versions, NOT a contemporary interwar document)
> The saying as it is passed down: "First they came for the socialists, and I did not speak out — because I was not a socialist. ... Then they came for me — and there was no one left to speak for me."
> Plain reading: "First they came for the socialists, and I said nothing, because I was not one of them... and when they finally came for me, there was no one left to speak up for me."
> Reading: this passage is a **postwar reflection that exists in several versions — it is NOT an original document from the interwar period itself** — but it names the heart of the receiving-end view: totalitarianism rarely takes everyone at once; it comes group by group. Every silence of "this is not my concern" paves the way for the next step. This is exactly what the DEFAULT perspective of this lesson (the silent ordinary person) is meant to make a student feel.

## 6. Consequences (Short / Medium / Long Term)

- **Short term (1939-1945):** the Interwar Years led directly to **World War II** — the deadliest war in human history (tens of millions dead) and the genocide (the Holocaust) that came with it. The "order and glory" that totalitarianism promised was delivered as catastrophe on an unprecedented scale.
- **Medium term (designing the postwar order):** precisely because they could see the chain — "a humiliating Versailles-style peace + economic collapse → resentment → totalitarianism → war" — the victors of WWII learned the lesson and built different things afterward: the **Marshall Plan** (help defeated nations rebuild rather than crush them), the **United Nations**, the **Bretton Woods** economic system, all trying not to repeat the interwar mistakes. To understand the postwar world, you must understand the Interwar Years.
- **Long term (the core of civic education to this day):** "how a democracy ends itself" and "why ordinary people embrace totalitarianism" have become the cases that civic education worldwide returns to again and again. What they offer is not a comforting conclusion ("the bad guys lost") but an uneasy warning: **the greatest threat to freedom is often not a foreign enemy, but people's own fear and silence in a crisis.** When the economy collapses, when someone points at an "enemy" and says "it's all their fault," when someone demands special powers in the name of "emergency" — those warning signs are the most important legacy the Interwar Years left for the future.

## 7. AP DBQ Thinking Practice

- **Multi-layered causation (the key tested skill):** why did totalitarianism appear in **multiple countries at the same time** in the 1930s? Argue by chaining the factors — the resentment of Versailles (a long-term cause) + the global shock of the Depression (the trigger) + fear of communist revolution and street chaos (emotion) + the cracks in democratic institutions (such as Weimar's Article 48, a structural condition) + the agency of the strongman (the catalyst). AP graders reward separating "long-term structures" from "short-term triggers" and showing how they interact.
- **Agency vs. structure:** was the rise of totalitarianism forced by a few demons, or the result of millions of ordinary people choosing or allowing it out of fear? Argue with the evidence: "the Nazis became the largest party by votes," "the March on Rome was the king handing over power," "the Enabling Act was passed by parliament's own vote." This is exactly the angle the DEFAULT perspective serves.
- **Continuity & change:** from the Weimar democratic constitution of 1919 to the one-party dictatorship of 1933 — what changed completely (freedom, the rule of law, parliament), and what grew out of older soil (the resentment of Versailles, the craving for order, pre-existing institutional cracks like Article 48)? Was the change a "rupture" or "old factors activated by crisis"?
- **Cross-case comparison:** Germany (Nazism) / Italy (Fascism) / USSR (Stalinism) / Japan (militarism) — where is the "totalitarian recipe" the same (crisis + enemy + strongman + single party + propaganda + seizing power via emergency), and where does it differ (left vs. right? ideology? the path to power — votes, a bluff, or the army overpowering civilians)? An excellent AP comparison exercise.

## 8. China in the Same Era (Structural Comparison · Rule 0 veto dimension)

These twenty interwar years (1919-1939) were exactly the twenty years from China's May Fourth Movement to the eve of full-scale war with Japan — **the same global crisis pushed China toward "national salvation" while pushing Europe and Japan toward totalitarianism. These are two faces of one era — and the Japanese-militarism thread of this lesson directly decided China's fate.**

- **1919, May Fourth: the same Treaty of Versailles, the opposite echo.** Europeans moved toward resentment and revenge over the humiliation of Versailles; but **the very same treaty** refused to return defeated Germany's rights in **Shandong** to China and handed them to Japan instead. When the news arrived, on **May 4, 1919**, students in Beijing flooded the streets (the May Fourth Movement) — protesting, striking, boycotting Japanese goods — and finally forced China's delegation **to refuse to sign the treaty**. The same treaty lit a fire of revenge in Germany and a fire of national awakening in China. This is the best contrast for understanding the "two faces of a global crisis."
- **The warlord era: China's version of an "order vacuum."** In Europe, democracy collapsed in crisis; in China during the same period (roughly 1916-1928), there was a different kind of "order vacuum" — a weak central government and rival **warlords** fighting one another. Both faced the dilemma of "the old order has fallen, the new order is not yet built" — except Europe slid from democracy into totalitarianism, while China, after the collapse of the empire, fell into division and then sought reunification.
- **The attempt at reunification: the Northern Expedition (1926-1928).** To end the warlords' division, the Nationalists (Kuomintang) launched the **Northern Expedition**, nominally reunifying China in 1928. This forms a subtle contrast with Europe's demand for "a strongman to end chaos through concentrated power" — both were responses to "chaos," but the paths and outcomes differed. The complex on-again, off-again relationship between the Nationalists and Communists is a tension unique to China's thread.
- **The blade of totalitarian expansion fell on China (1931-1937).** The Japanese-militarism thread of this lesson is not an abstract "distant totalitarianism" — its blade fell directly on China: in 1931 the Mukden Incident, with Japan seizing the northeast and setting up the puppet Manchukuo; on **July 7, 1937**, the **Marco Polo Bridge Incident**, with Japan launching a full-scale invasion and China entering its long, bitter War of Resistance. **Europe's totalitarianism eventually ignited the European theater; Japan's militarism eventually ignited the Chinese theater — they were two fuses of one global catastrophe.**

Rule 0: we cannot romanticize the China side as "the only clear-eyed victim," nor flatten the Europe side as "collective madness." Both were different responses by different societies, each from its own situation and history, to the same global crisis (the legacy of WWI + the Great Depression). This section only sets two fates, on two sides of the earth in the same twenty years, side by side — and points out how, in 1937, they actually collided.

## 9. What Historians Argue About (Historiography)

- **The debate over "totalitarianism" itself:** putting Nazi Germany and Stalin's USSR into one "totalitarianism" frame is itself disputed. One line (the Hannah Arendt approach) stresses their shared structure (a single party, leader worship, terror, propaganda, the destruction of private life). Another stresses the fundamental differences in ideology and social base between the left (USSR) and the right (Nazis), warning that the frame may blur important distinctions.
- **Why Germany went Nazi: a "special path" vs. a general crisis.** One line (the *Sonderweg*, or "special path," thesis) argues German history had its own peculiar track toward Nazism; another stresses that the Nazi rise was more the result of a **general crisis** like the Depression combined with Weimar's institutional cracks, rather than a fate unique to Germany — which bears on whether "this could happen elsewhere."
- **"Top-down" vs. "bottom-up" (how much ordinary people took part):** how much of the atrocity was deliberately designed and imposed from the top, and how much depended on ordinary people's participation, cooperation, and silent consent? Recent decades of research increasingly stress "the complicity of ordinary people" — exactly the scholarly basis for this lesson's DEFAULT perspective.
- **The role of the Depression:** was the economic crisis the "root cause" of totalitarianism's rise, or a "catalyst"? One line stresses that without the Depression the Nazis would not have taken power (an economic-determinist leaning); another stresses that resentment, fear, and institutional factors mattered just as much, and the economy merely set them alight.
- **A neutral synthesis:** the rise of totalitarianism had deep structural conditions (defeat, the treaty, economic collapse, institutional cracks) *and* could not have happened without the agency of leaders and the choices/consent of millions of ordinary people — no single factor explains it. Flattening it into "bad men took over" or "pure economic inevitability" would both keep us from learning the real lesson.

## 10. Common Misconceptions (Myth-Busting)

1. ❌ "Hitler seized power by a violent coup." → ✅ In 1933 he was **legally appointed** chancellor by the president, and only then (the Reichstag Fire Decree, the Enabling Act) dismantled democracy step by step. The most frightening part is precisely that it was "legal."
2. ❌ "All Germans were tricked or forced — no one truly supported it." → ✅ The Nazi Party became the largest party in parliament by **votes**. The fuel of totalitarianism came from millions of ordinary people's fear, anger, and consent in a crisis, not only from a few demons forcing it. Admitting this is not excusing the past — it is how we learn to recognize the warning signs.
3. ❌ "Mussolini really marched in and conquered Rome." → ✅ The "March on Rome" was largely a **bluff**; the king, to avoid bloodshed, invited him to be prime minister. He seized power because the state refused to defend itself.
4. ❌ "The Treaty of Versailles directly caused WWII." → ✅ The **resentment** the treaty planted was one long-term cause, but without the Depression, without the cracks in democracy, and without specific people choosing to exploit that resentment, the outcome need not have followed. It was many factors stacked together, not one straight line.
5. ❌ "Stalin's Five-Year Plans were a pure success / a pure disaster." → ✅ The USSR did turn from a farming country into an industrial power (that is real), but the cost was forced collectivization and famine (such as the Holodomor) and millions of deaths in the Great Purge. The "achievement" and the catastrophe share one body — you cannot tell only one side.
6. ❌ "Totalitarianism = a few bad men, nothing to do with ordinary society." → ✅ Totalitarianism is deeply embedded in ordinary society — it needs ordinary people to vote, to attend the rallies, to report on a neighbor, or simply to stay silent. That is what should make us most watchful.
7. ❌ "Japan's invasion of China was an isolated, local event." → ✅ It was part of the global wave of totalitarian/militarist expansion, sharing the same roots as the rise of European fascism (the Depression + the military overpowering civilians + seeking a way out through expansion), and it ultimately fed into the same WWII.
8. ❌ "This was all long ago and has nothing to do with today." → ✅ The whole point of this lesson is that these mechanisms — fear in a crisis, naming an "enemy," demanding special powers in the name of "emergency" — do not belong to one particular era. Recognizing them is the work of every generation.

## 11. Thinking Questions (Open-Ended, No Conclusion)

1. Germany — one of the most literate, most cultured nations in Europe — saw its democracy ended not by a foreign enemy but by its own votes and constitutional procedures. If it could happen even there, what does that tell you about how fragile democracy can be?
2. Article 48 of the Weimar constitution was meant well — to give the government a tool for emergencies — yet it became the key to dismantling democracy. How can a power designed to protect freedom end up destroying it? Why are the words "state of emergency" worth every generation's wariness?
3. The cold, hungry young German who went to the rallies was not an evil man — he was just afraid, angry, and longing to belong. If you stood in his place, are you sure you would have chosen differently? Is understanding him harder — and more important — than judging him?
4. Totalitarianism promised "order and glory," and many supported it precisely because they craved order. When a society falls into chaos and fear, the longing for a "strongman" is understandable — yet that longing is exactly the dangerous entrance. Between "order" and "freedom," how should a society in crisis choose?
5. The same Treaty of Versailles lit revenge in Germany and the awakening of May Fourth in China; the same global crisis sent Europe toward totalitarianism and China toward national salvation. This time you mostly heard the voice of "the ordinary person who voted yes, or stayed silent." How would the democrat who tried to stop it tell these twenty years? How would a Chinese farmer in the northeast, crushed under Japanese boots in the Depression, tell it? Whose voice have you not heard yet?

## Appendix A: Key Timeline

- 1918.11: World War I ends (about 9 million soldiers dead; four empires collapse).
- 1919.5.4: China's May Fourth Movement (protesting Versailles handing Germany's Shandong rights to Japan).
- 1919.6.28: Treaty of Versailles signed in the Hall of Mirrors (includes Article 231, the war guilt clause).
- 1921: London Schedule sets German reparations at about 132 billion gold marks.
- 1922.10.28-30: Italy's March on Rome; Oct 30 the king appoints Mussolini prime minister.
- 1923: German hyperinflation (the middle class's savings wiped out).
- 1924.6.10: Italian anti-fascist deputy Matteotti assassinated; Jan 1925 Mussolini declares dictatorship.
- 1928: Soviet First Five-Year Plan begins (forced collectivization + heavy industry).
- 1929.10.24/10.29: Wall Street Crash (Black Thursday / Black Tuesday) → the Great Depression spreads worldwide.
- 1931.9.18: Mukden Incident; Japan seizes Manchuria.
- 1932: German unemployment about 6 million; the Nazi Party becomes the largest party in parliament.
- 1932-1933: Soviet famine (including Ukraine's Holodomor), estimated millions of deaths.
- 1932: The puppet state of Manchukuo is set up, with Puyi as figurehead.
- 1933.1.30: Hindenburg legally appoints Hitler chancellor.
- 1933.2.27: The Reichstag building catches fire; Feb 28 the Reichstag Fire Decree is issued (under Article 48).
- 1933.3.23: The Enabling Act passes — parliament hands lawmaking power to Hitler's cabinet.
- 1936.2.26: Japan's February 26 Incident (the military further overpowers the civilians).
- 1936-1938: The Soviet Great Purge (Great Terror).
- 1937.7.7: Marco Polo Bridge Incident — Japan's full-scale invasion of China; the War of Resistance begins.
- 1939.9: Germany invades Poland; World War II begins (the Interwar Years end).

## Appendix B: Core Vocabulary

- **the Interwar Years** — the years between the two world wars (1919-1939)
- **Treaty of Versailles** — the 1919 peace treaty that ended WWI
- **Article 231 / war guilt clause** — made Germany accept full responsibility for the war
- **reparations** — the huge payments the treaty demanded from Germany
- **the Great Depression** — the worldwide economic collapse from 1929
- **gold standard** — the money system that tied economies together and spread the crisis worldwide
- **totalitarianism** — the state trying to control nearly all of society and personal life
- **fascism** — extreme nationalism + strongman dictatorship + rejection of liberal democracy (Italy / Germany)
- **the Duce / the Führer** — "the leader" (Mussolini's / Hitler's title)
- **March on Rome** — 1922, the model event of Mussolini's seizure of power
- **Article 48** — the Weimar constitution's presidential emergency-powers clause (later abused)
- **Reichstag** — the German parliament building (site of the 1933 fire)
- **Enabling Act** — 1933, parliament handing lawmaking power to Hitler
- **Five-Year Plan** — Stalin's forced industrialization plan
- **collectivization / Holodomor** — collective farming / Ukraine's man-made famine (the terrible cost of the plan)
- **the Great Purge** — 1936-1938, Stalin's terror against dissent
- **kulak** — "rich peasant," targeted by Stalin as a "class enemy"
- **gulag** — the Soviet forced-labor camp system
- **Kwantung Army / Mukden Incident** — Japan's army in China / the staged event used to seize Manchuria
- **Manchukuo** — the Japanese-installed puppet state in northeast China
