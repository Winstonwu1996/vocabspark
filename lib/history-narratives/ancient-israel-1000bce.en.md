# Ancient Israel & the Origins of Monotheism · c. 1000 BCE

> **Lock status**: narrative kernel v0 — pending review
>
> **Anchor**: CA HSS Grade 6, 6.3 (Ancient Hebrews) · HS World History (Ancient Near East)
> **Through-line**: the shared source of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam
> **Difficulty**: 4/5 (abstract idea — how an *idea* (monotheism) is born and changes over time)
> **Expected duration**: 35-45 min
>
> **Rule 0 (read first):** This lesson teaches ancient Israel and monotheism **as a historical and civilizational phenomenon.** It does not judge faith. It never decides for you whether God exists or which religion is "true." For every event in the Hebrew Bible, it holds **two readings side by side** — the reading inside the faith tradition, and the scholarly/archaeological reading. Both are presented; neither cancels the other.

## 0. Open Your Textbook First (Textbook Anchor)

> This belongs to **CA HSS Grade 6, unit 6.3 (Ancient Hebrews)** — a **standalone unit**, alongside Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece, and Rome.
>
> Your textbook will tell you:
> - Where the Hebrews **came from** (migration).
> - What they **believed** — **monotheism** (one God) vs. the **polytheism** (many gods) of their neighbors.
> - The **Hebrew Bible** as both law and history.
> - How, after losing their land, they **survived** (diaspora and identity).
>
> **What makes this lesson uniquely important** (this is its place in the whole world-history course):
> - **It is the shared source of three world religions.** About **half the people alive today** follow Judaism, Christianity, or Islam — and all three trace their roots to the one-God faith of this single small ancient-Near-Eastern people. Understanding this unit is a **key** that unlocks two thousand years of later religious history.
> - **The current course barely covers it.** Right now only *babylonian-exile* touches the tail end of this story. But the exile is the **ending**, not the start. This lesson supplies the **start**: how the idea of one God grew, step by step, out of a real people in real history.
> - **It is a blind spot for many Chinese-heritage families.** Mainland Chinese history curricula barely cover ancient Israel, so many parents have no ready frame for "monotheism vs. polytheism," "covenant," or "diaspora identity." This is exactly the kind of topic kids meet daily in American classrooms but parents cannot help with.

## 1. One-Sentence Anchor (Many Viewpoints at Once)

In the early **6th century BCE**, on a summer day, the army of Babylon broke into Jerusalem, burned down a building called the **First Temple**, and marched the city's priests, nobles, and craftspeople a thousand miles away to Babylon. By the common sense of every people in the ancient Near East, the story should have ended right there. A small kingdom crushed by a great empire, its temple burned, its people carried off — that meant **this people's god had lost, and so this people should vanish from history**, like dozens of small peoples swallowed by Assyria and Babylon around them.

But this time, something almost without precedent in ancient-Near-Eastern history happened: the captives did **not** conclude that their god had lost. Instead they reached a stunning explanation — our god is not weak; **we** broke the covenant first, and he used Babylon to punish us; and this god is **not only** our tribe's god, he is **the one and only God of the whole world**, with Babylon itself in his hands. It was exactly here — by the rivers of a foreign land, with no temple and no homeland — that a brand-new form of faith was forged: **one transcendent, single, invisible God who makes a covenant with people.**

In the very same era, on the other side of the earth, China was in its **Zhou** period. People there also looked up to **Heaven (Tian)**, also honored **Shangdi** (a high god), and also had the idea of the **Mandate of Heaven** — but in the Chinese cosmos, Heaven, the ancestors, and the many gods of mountains and rivers were a **layered web**, each with its role; the link between "our people" and "Heaven" ran through **bloodline and virtue**, not a written **covenant**. Two cosmic visions — "a transcendent single God + covenant" and "Heaven-and-human resonance + ancestral bloodline" — took their lasting shapes at the two ends of the first millennium BCE. They would go on to color the spirit of West and East for two thousand years.

## 2. The Stage (Geography / Climate / Society / Institutions / Events)

- **Geography**: Ancient Israel sat on a narrow strip along the eastern Mediterranean coast — the **Levant** (or **Canaan**). It was wedged between two great civilizations: Egypt to the south, Mesopotamia (Assyria, Babylon) to the northeast. This strip was the **only land bridge** linking Africa and Asia, so every army marching from the Nile to the Two Rivers (or back) had to step across it. "A small land caught between great empires" is the **geographic baseline** of this whole history — too important not to be fought over, too small not to be crushed.
- **Climate / terrain**: The south was dry and hilly, living off seasonal "early rains" and "late rains" to grow wheat and olives and graze sheep. Rain was unreliable and the land was poor. This fragile, **rain-dependent** life made "will the god send rain?" and "is the god keeping the covenant?" daily, life-and-death questions — and pressed them deep into the religion.
- **Society**: At first there were several kinship-based tribes (the biblical tradition calls them the "twelve tribes"). Society was built around families, villages, and a priestly class. Like their neighbors, early ordinary Israelites were **not** the pure monotheists of later times — both archaeology and texts show many people worshipping YHWH **and** local Canaanite gods (such as Baal and Asherah) at the same time. **The move from "our people worships only YHWH" to "there is only one God in all the world" was a long evolution, not there from the start** — this is the single most important scholarly point in the lesson.
- **Institutions**: The core institution was the **covenant** (Hebrew *brit*) — a **contract** between the god and "his people": you worship me alone and keep my law (the **Torah**), and I will be your god and protect you. This "covenant" structure was ancient Israel's most distinctive invention: the bond between god and people was not mere master-and-servant or buying-and-selling, but a **two-way pact with obligations on both sides** — break it and be punished, keep it and be protected. The Torah was at once religious commandment, social law, and ethics — here, religion and law were **one body**.
- **Events (key nodes — note the two-layer reading)**: The traditional narrative includes the Exodus (the god leads Israel out of slavery in Egypt), the covenant at Mount Sinai (the god gives the law), David founding a kingdom c. 1000 BCE, and Solomon building the First Temple c. 957 BCE. The **scholarly reading** cautions: the Exodus and the Sinai covenant currently **lack direct archaeological evidence**, and most scholars treat them as foundational narratives written down later; whether David and Solomon ruled a single powerful kingdom is **fiercely debated** among archaeologists (see §9). The hard nodes most scholars accept as "historical ground" are: c. 1208 BCE, the Egyptian **Merneptah Stele** gives the first written mention of "Israel"; c. 9th century BCE, the **Tel Dan Stele** gives the first mention of the "House of David" outside the Bible; 722 BCE, the northern kingdom of Israel falls to Assyria; 586 BCE, the southern kingdom of Judah and the First Temple fall to Babylon; 539/538 BCE, the Persian king **Cyrus** lets the captives return.

## 3. The Main Characters

> Three perspectives (actor, mediator, receiving-end), including at least one powerless party. In this topic a "character" means "how a different standpoint sees the same history," because the core clash here is not person against person but **worldview against worldview**.

### The Babylonian victor (actor) — an officer of Nebuchadnezzar marching captives back to Babylon

A bridging character, but the role is fully real, standing for the **Neo-Babylonian Empire** that in the 6th century BCE swallowed half the ancient Near East. He has no name, because he stands for a whole set of "imperial common sense." What is certain is that common sense itself: to ancient-Near-Eastern eyes, a war was also a **contest of god against god** — whoever won had the stronger god. Babylon's chief god was **Marduk**, with a magnificent temple, elaborate festivals, and myths written across clay tablets. Breaking into Jerusalem and burning down that "temple" — a temple to a single invisible god, with no idol at all — seemed perfectly normal to him: YHWH lost, Marduk won, and the captives would sooner or later kneel before Babylon's idols, melt away, and vanish like every other conquered small people.

He marched a long line of captives across the desert toward Babylon — towering walls, networks of canals, hanging gardens, a tower (a **ziggurat**) stabbing at the sky — one of the most spectacular cities in the world. He was sure this grandeur would break the captives' spirit. This perspective lets you look out from **inside a victorious polytheist empire**: to him this was the most ordinary conquest imaginable. What he **could not possibly foresee** is that this very exile would force the captives' faith into something brand-new — something able to live on without a homeland or a temple.

### The prophet, Jeremiah / Ezekiel (mediator) — standing between "disaster as punishment" and "disaster as the end"

A real historical type (Jeremiah was active c. late 7th to early 6th century BCE and lived through Jerusalem's fall; Ezekiel was carried to Babylon and preached among the exiles). A prophet (Hebrew *navi*) in ancient Israel was not a "fortune-teller" but **someone who, in a moment of crisis, speaks for the god and explains what history means.** He stood in a deeply lonely place: Babylon's sword on one side, on the other his own people, despairing enough to abandon the faith. On the ruins of a destroyed nation he had to answer a nearly impossible question — **if the god is real and mighty, why did his people lose so badly?**

His answer changed the whole civilization's direction. He did not say "the god lost." He said: **this is exactly the proof the god is right** — Israel broke the covenant, worshipped other gods, did injustice, so the god used Babylon's hand to punish his own people; the punishment itself is evidence the covenant still holds. Going further, Ezekiel declared in the foreign land that the god is **not trapped by any temple or any homeland** — he is just as present by the rivers of Babylon. This mediator perspective is the lesson's hinge: they re-read a disaster that looked like "the god's defeat" as "proof of the god's justice and universality," so that a people, after losing its nation, believed **more firmly and more purely** than before. This is one of the rarest moves in human history — **using a defeat to strengthen a faith.**

### An ordinary Judean captive (receiving-end · DEFAULT) — a young person by the rivers of Babylon who has lost everything

A bridging character with a fully real role (this is the DEFAULT perspective, deliberately the most powerless place, the one most ground down by history). No exact name — because the ordinary captives **left no names in history.** What is certain is the situation: he was probably a child of a craftsman or priestly family in Jerusalem who watched the Temple collapse in flames, was roped into a line, and walked a thousand miles to Babylon. Now he sits by a canal in a foreign land, looking at its grand temples and towers, hearing people live in an unfamiliar tongue around him. **Psalm 137** is believed to come from exactly this situation: "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion."

For him the deepest pain is not only losing home, but a question that nearly crushes a person: **has our god, too, been defeated?** Everyone around him says: your god lost, kneel and worship Marduk. Two roads lie before him — like other conquered small peoples, slowly forget his god, melt into Babylon, and vanish from history; or, listen to the prophet's reading, take the disaster as "the god disciplining us," and hold on tighter. He chose the second — and it was countless such choices, by countless such people, that let a people without a homeland invent, for the first time, a faith that could survive **on scripture and identity alone** — no land, no temple needed. This perspective is the heart of the lesson: that "single, transcendent, everywhere" God of monotheism was, in large part, thought-through and gripped tight **in the hands of the lowest, most hopeless exiles.**

## 4. The Story (Plot Beats — two readings throughout: traditional narrative / scholarly reading, kept distinct)

1. **A small people on the corridor (first appears c. 1208 BCE).** The earliest **documentary proof** that Israel existed is the c. 1208 BCE stele of Egypt's pharaoh Merneptah, inscribed "Israel is laid waste, its seed is not" — a boastful war report that became the earliest hard evidence for the name "Israel." At this point it was still just a cluster of tribes in the Canaanite hills, its religion not much different from its Canaanite neighbors'.
2. **The traditional founding stories: Exodus and the Sinai covenant.** The Hebrew Bible says: Israel was once enslaved in Egypt, the god used Moses to lead them out, and on Mount Sinai gave the law and made a covenant with them. **Scholarly reading**: the Exodus and the Sinai covenant currently have no direct archaeological evidence, and most scholars think they were written down later (likely during the kingdom period and after the exile) as a **core narrative** to anchor national identity — whether they happened "word for word" is in doubt, but their power as **the story that shaped this people's self-understanding** is real and enormous. Teach both layers; neither cancels the other.
3. **The kingdom of David and Solomon (c. 1000-930 BCE).** Traditional narrative: David united Israel c. 1000 BCE and made Jerusalem the capital; his son Solomon built the First Temple c. 957 BCE. **Scholarly reading**: David did exist (the 9th-century Tel Dan stele's mention of the "House of David" shows the dynasty was real), but **just how large and strong** this "united kingdom" was is a famous "battle royal" in archaeology (see §9) — some see a fair-sized early state, others a small hill-country chiefdom enlarged by later texts.
4. **The split: northern Israel, southern Judah (c. 930 BCE).** After Solomon's death the kingdom split into Israel in the north (capital Samaria) and Judah in the south (capital Jerusalem, holding the Temple). This is **accepted by most scholars**: by c. 9th century BCE, the two kingdoms existed as separate political bodies.
5. **The northern kingdom falls to Assyria (722 BCE).** The Assyrian Empire destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel and carried off and scattered its elite — the origin of the later legend of the "ten lost tribes." From then on only the southern kingdom of Judah carried on.
6. **The religious reforms of Hezekiah and Josiah (c. 8th-7th century BCE).** Two kings of the south pushed a "purifying" drive: tearing down local shrines and the worship of other gods (Baal, Asherah) and **concentrating sacrifice in the single Temple in Jerusalem.** Around 622 BCE, Josiah, repairing the Temple, "discovered" a scroll of law (most scholars think the core of Deuteronomy) and used it to push reforms for worshipping **only YHWH and sacrificing in one place only.** **This is the key turning point from "worshipping one god (monolatry)" toward "there is only one God (monotheism)"** — monotheism did not fall from the sky; it was forged step by step in political and religious reform.
7. **The southern kingdom and the First Temple fall to Babylon (586 BCE).** The Neo-Babylonian Empire broke into Jerusalem, burned the First Temple, and carried off Judah's elite to Babylon — the "Babylonian Exile." By ancient-Near-Eastern common sense, this was the moment "this people should vanish."
8. **The idea is forged in exile (586-539 BCE).** In Babylon, the captives who had lost land and Temple, led by the prophets' reading (Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and the "Second Isaiah" line), re-read the disaster as "the god's discipline" rather than "the god's defeat," and pushed the faith into a **thorough monotheism**: YHWH is not only Israel's god but the **one true God** who created and rules the whole world, Babylon included. With no Temple for sacrifice, the center of faith shifted toward **reading and copying scripture** — seen by many scholars as the seed of a "religion of the book," and the source of the deep reverence for scripture in later Judaism, Christianity, and Islam.
9. **The Persian return and the Second Temple (from 539/538 BCE).** The Persian king Cyrus destroyed Babylon in 539 BCE, and in 538 BCE ordered the captives free to return to Jerusalem and rebuild the Temple (the Second Temple, completed c. 516 BCE). The returning community rebuilt itself in the small Persian province of **Yehud** and, around this time, very likely **edited and shaped** the various old traditions into the core of the later Hebrew Bible (scholars widely hold that the Torah took its final shape from the exile to the Persian period).
10. **The birth of diaspora and identity (running on from here).** Not everyone returned — many stayed in Babylon and elsewhere, forming the **diaspora** ("the scattering"). A people with no single homeland, held together for centuries by **shared scripture, law, festivals, and identity**, took shape. This way of surviving — "lose the nation but not the people" — is another deep legacy ancient Israel left the world.

**An irony to keep:** by everyone's expectation, the fire of 586 BCE should have been this people's end. Instead it became the true beginning of mature monotheism — **the most total defeat gave birth to the most solid faith.** The people who burned the Temple ended up helping the captives invent a God who is "present even without a temple."

## 5. Reading the Documents (Primary Sources)

### A. The Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BCE) — the earliest hard evidence for "Israel"
> Original (common scholarly English): "Israel is laid waste, its seed is not."
> Plain reading: "Israel is destroyed, its descendants are no more." This is an Egyptian pharaoh boasting of his victory.
> Many-sided reading: the Egyptian who wrote this meant "I wiped Israel out." And yet 3,200 years later, the "Israel" he wiped out is still here, while the boasting pharaoh lives only on this stone. Its value is not in its content but in this: it accidentally gives the name "Israel" its **earliest documented date.**

### B. Deuteronomy 6:4-5 (the Shema — traditionally the core declaration of Israel's faith)
> Original (common English): "Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one. You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart..."
> Plain reading: "Hear, O Israel: YHWH is our God, YHWH is one. Love your God with all your heart."
> Two-layer reading: the **faith tradition** holds this as eternal truth spoken by the god himself, the heartbeat of Jewish faith. The **scholarly reading** notes that wording this thorough ("YHWH is one") is dated by most scholars to around the 7th-century Josiah reform and the time around the exile — meaning the sentence itself is the crystal of monotheism **being forged in history**, not present from the start. Present both readings; neither tears down the other.

### C. Psalm 137:1 (the voice of the exiles)
> Original (common English): "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion."
> Plain reading: "By the rivers of Babylon we sat down and wept when we remembered Zion (Jerusalem)."
> Reading: this is the most direct first-hand voice of the receiving-end perspective — a people's grief in a foreign land after losing home. It is precious because it is not a victor's monument but the **tears left by the people who were ground down.** To feel this grief is to understand why this group, in the end, chose to grip the god tighter rather than let go.

### D. The Cyrus Cylinder (c. 539 BCE, found in Babylon)
> Content: a clay-cylinder inscription issued by the Persian king Cyrus after conquering Babylon, announcing the restoration of local temples and the return of peoples relocated by the previous regime.
> Plain reading: the new ruler Cyrus says he will let conquered peoples go home and rebuild their own temples.
> Two-layer reading + myth-busting: the Bible (Ezra) writes Cyrus's release of the Judeans as "God stirred the heart of Cyrus." **Note**: the Cyrus Cylinder itself does **not** single out the Judeans or Jerusalem by name — it is a **general statement of tolerance toward all conquered peoples.** This is a perfect teaching point: from the **same** event, the Judeans read "our god is in control" through **their faith frame**, while the Persian Empire treated it, through a **statecraft frame**, as a routine policy to calm many peoples. Both frames are true and do not contradict — it teaches students: **always ask of a source who wrote it, for whom, and why.**

## 6. Consequences (Short / Medium / Long Term)

- **Short term (6th-5th century BCE):** the captives did not vanish. After the Persian return they rebuilt their community in Yehud, built the Second Temple, and edited their old traditions into shape. A small people who, by ancient-Near-Eastern rules, should have disappeared, instead survived — believing more purely and more thoroughly in one God than before the nation fell.
- **Medium term (classical era):** this faith — "single, transcendent, covenant-bound, scripture-centered" — became the foundation of later **Judaism**; out of it grew **Christianity** in the 1st century CE; and in the 7th century CE **Islam** continued the same one-God line (all three honor Abraham as a shared ancestor, hence "Abrahamic religions"). The faith of one small corridor people in the ancient Near East spread, along the Mediterranean and the Silk Road, across the world.
- **Long term (to today):** the religions of about half the people alive today have roots here. The deeper effect is at the level of ideas: monotheism's notions — that **history has a direction, a meaning, driven by one transcendent will**; that **all people are equal before the one God**; that **law stands above the king** — deeply shaped later Western law, ethics, politics, and even modern ideas like "progress" and "human rights." At the same time, the exclusiveness of "the one true God" is also one root of later religious conflict — the light and the tension share one body. Understanding it as a **civilizational phenomenon** is, whether or not you personally believe, a required course for reading today's world.

## 7. AP DBQ Thinking Practice

- **Stakeholder grid:** ordinary Judean captives / prophets (the interpreters) / the stay-behind vs. the returnees / the Babylonian conquerors / the Persian Empire (the releasers) / polytheist Canaanite neighbors — between 586 and 539 BCE, how did each read "what this disaster means"? Whose reading "won" in the end, and why?
- **The evolution of an idea (key practice):** monotheism was **not there from the start.** Lay out its evolution chain with evidence: early monolatry (worship only the people's god, but admit other gods exist) → Josiah's centralization of worship (c. 622) → thorough monotheism in exile (one true God, creator of the whole world). This is the "change over time" argument AP graders reward — **an idea has a history; it is made, not handed down from the sky.**
- **Continuity & change:** from the fall of 586 to the return of 538 — what **changed completely** (loss of land and Temple, a turn toward scripture, the move to thorough monotheism, the birth of a diaspora), and what was **instead strengthened and kept** (national identity, exclusive devotion to YHWH, the centrality of the law)? Why did "losing the nation" **strengthen** identity, the opposite of most conquered peoples' fate?
- **Source-frame analysis (this topic's signature drill):** take the Cyrus Cylinder — from the same "release" event, Ezra reads "God stirred Cyrus," the Cyrus Cylinder reads "the empire's routine policy to calm many peoples." Train students to ask of a source **who wrote it, for whom, and to achieve what** — and so learn that "different frames can be true at the same time."

## 8. China in the Same Era (Structural Comparison · Rule 0 veto dimension)

The first millennium BCE was exactly China's **Zhou** period (Western Zhou c. 1046-771 BCE, Eastern Zhou 770-256 BCE). This is the key section for seeing this topic's core — "the cosmic vision behind monotheism" — clearly, in contrast.

Structural comparison (side by side, no praise or blame, no ranking):

- **What the highest god / Heaven looks like:** the Israelites forged a **transcendent, single, invisible, image-less** God (YHWH) who stands **outside** the world and creates and judges it. The Chinese of the same era also looked up to a highest power — the Shang called it **Shangdi**, the Zhou honored **Tian (Heaven)** even more — but "Heaven" was not a single creator standing outside the world. It was more like **cosmic order and moral will itself**, coexisting in layers with the ancestors and the many gods of mountains and rivers, each with its role. One side is "**one God vs. all things**"; the other is a "**Heaven-ancestors-many gods**" continuous web. This is the most basic fork between the two cosmic visions.
- **What links humans to the highest:** Israel's core mechanism is the **covenant** — the god signs a **two-way pact with obligations** with a people: break it and be punished, keep it and be protected, with the Torah as its terms. China's core mechanism is the **Mandate of Heaven plus the ancestors** — Heaven "grants" the right to rule to the virtuous, and "withdraws" the Mandate if the ruler loses virtue (the Zhou used exactly this logic to explain why they could replace the Shang); and the personal link to the transcendent ran more through **ancestor worship** along the bloodline (sacrifice to ancestors, divination asking the ancestors, as in Shang oracle bones). One side runs on a "**covenant**," the other on "**virtue plus bloodline**."
- **"Who is counted into the relationship with the highest":** Israel's covenant was made with "**a people**," and was later pushed in exile toward "**the one God of all humanity**." China's Mandate was granted to "**the Son of Heaven**," one person / one house, who then ruled all under Heaven on Heaven's behalf — a **top-down pyramid**, not "a god making an equal covenant with each person." This point deeply shaped later, different Eastern and Western imaginations of the bond between "the individual" and "authority."

Why this "key" matters: the later Western ideas — that "history has an ultimate purpose," that "people are equal before the one God," that "law / covenant stands above the king" — have their source in Israel's monotheism; while China's ideas — "Heaven-and-human resonance," "match Heaven with virtue," "revere Heaven and honor the ancestors," "family and state share one structure" — have their source in the Zhou Mandate and ancestor faith. To understand how these two cosmic visions each took their lasting shapes in the first millennium BCE is to hold a key to many later East-West differences in law, politics, and the view of the individual.

Rule 0: we cannot say monotheism was "more advanced" and the Mandate-and-ancestors "more primitive," nor the reverse. Both are deep answers each civilization gave to the ultimate question — "how does a person find a place in the cosmos, and what makes power legitimate?" — each with its own wisdom and its own blind spots. This section only sets the two cosmic visions, at the two ends of the earth in the same era, side by side. It makes no judgment of faith or of civilizational rank.

## 9. What Historians Argue About (Historiography)

- **The "minimalist" vs. "conservative" archaeology battle (the United Monarchy debate):** whether David and Solomon ruled a **single, powerful** kingdom is the most famous debate in modern biblical archaeology. **Israel Finkelstein** (the minimalist / low-chronology school) argues that the grand buildings once credited to Solomon (such as the gates at Megiddo, Hazor, and Gezer) should be **moved down to the 9th century BCE**, that 10th-century Jerusalem was only a small village, and that the "united kingdom" is largely an enlargement by later texts. **Amihai Mazar** (the modified-traditional-chronology school) and others use large amounts of radiocarbon dating to argue that a fair-sized early state did exist in the 10th century. Most scholars today accept a compromise "in between, leaning modified-traditional." **The key neutral point:** the fight is over "**scale and strength**," not "whether David existed" — the Tel Dan stele mentions the "House of David," and on that basis most scholars accept that the **Davidic dynasty was real.**
- **When monotheism took shape (the emergence of monotheism):** mainstream scholarship holds that thorough monotheism (not just "worship only our people's god" but "there is only one God in all the world") was a **product of evolution**, with key stages around the 7th-century Josiah reform and the Babylonian exile, not handed down once on Mount Sinai. This school stresses that religious ideas **have a history and can be studied with evidence.**
- **How the Torah was written (the Documentary Hypothesis and its successors):** the classic "Documentary Hypothesis" (four sources, JEDP) holds that the Torah was edited step by step from sources of different eras and standpoints, taking final shape mostly from the exile to the Persian period; today scholars revise and debate the exact division of the four sources, but "the Torah was edited over a long time, not written by one person at one time" is a broad consensus.
- **"Where Israel came from" (the origins of Israel):** was early Israel an **outside conqueror of Canaan**, or **a group that emerged from within Canaanite society itself**? Today's archaeological evidence supports more the latter (the Israelites came largely from local Canaanite populations), which fits the view that "early Israelite religion was not clearly distinct from Canaanite religion."
- **A neutral synthesis:** at each of these debates, the lesson presents **multiple standpoints plus their kinds of evidence** (text / archaeology / inscription), so students see "how the discipline of history argues and how it uses evidence," rather than memorizing one standard answer.

## 10. Common Misconceptions (Myth-Busting)

1. ❌ "Ancient Israelites were pure monotheists from the start." → ✅ Many early Israelites worshipped YHWH **and** local Canaanite gods (Baal, Asherah). Thorough monotheism was **forged step by step over centuries**, through reform and exile — not inborn.
2. ❌ "Archaeology has confirmed the Exodus, the Sinai covenant, and a grand kingdom of Solomon." → ✅ The Exodus and the Sinai covenant **lack direct archaeological evidence** and are mostly seen as foundational narratives; the **scale and strength** of Solomon's kingdom is fiercely debated. Their power as **identity-shaping stories** is huge and real, but "it happened word for word" is not the same as "proven."
3. ❌ "The Bible was written all at once by one person (Moses)." → ✅ Mainstream scholarship holds the Torah was **edited over a long time from sources of different eras**, taking final shape mostly from the exile to the Persian period.
4. ❌ "The Babylonian Exile was this people's end." → ✅ The opposite — it was the **true beginning of mature monotheism**, and the start of surviving "on scripture and identity, not on land."
5. ❌ "The Cyrus Cylinder specifically records the release of the Judeans." → ✅ The Cylinder is a **general tolerance policy toward all conquered peoples** and does **not** single out Judah or Jerusalem. It is the Judeans who read it, through their own faith frame, as "our god is in control."
6. ❌ "Judaism = Christianity = Islam, all the same" or "they are totally unrelated." → ✅ All three trace their roots to this same ancient-Near-Eastern one-God tradition and share Abraham (hence "Abrahamic religions"), yet are **separate religions with huge differences.** Same source does not mean same thing.
7. ❌ "This lesson is teaching or judging a religious faith." → ✅ The lesson understands monotheism as a **historical and civilizational phenomenon**, holding the faith-tradition reading and the scholarly/archaeological reading side by side, and **does not judge whether God exists or which religion is truer.** Whether to believe is personal; understanding how it shaped the world is everyone's work.

## 11. Thinking Questions (Open-Ended, No Conclusion)

1. By the common sense of everyone in the ancient Near East, the burning of the Temple and the carrying-off of the people in 586 BCE meant "this people's god lost and the people should vanish." Instead the captives gripped their faith tighter and thought it through more thoroughly. Why would the most total defeat forge the most solid faith? What does this tell you about the link between belief and circumstance?
2. From the same "Cyrus release" event, the Judeans read "God is in control" through a faith frame, and the Persian Empire read "routine policy to calm many peoples" through a statecraft frame. Both frames are true and do not contradict — so what is the first question you should ask when reading any historical source?
3. Monotheism's "transcendent single God + covenant" and same-era China's "Mandate of Heaven + ancestral bloodline" are two deep answers humanity gave to the ultimate question — "how does a person find a place in the cosmos, and what makes power legitimate?" If you traveled back to the 6th century BCE and stood, in turn, beside the Judean by the rivers of Babylon and the Chinese person in Eastern Zhou, what two different "shapes of the world" would you see?
4. This people lost its homeland yet survived for centuries on shared scripture and identity — while the vast majority of conquered small peoples vanished. What exactly made "lose the nation but not the people" possible? What does that suggest for today's question of "where does identity come from?"
5. This time you mainly heard one position — the young person by the rivers of Babylon who lost everything and chose, in the end, to hold the faith tight. How would the Babylonian officer who burned the Temple tell that night? How would the Persian Empire that released the captives see what it did? And what about the people who stayed in Babylon and never went home — what would they think? Whose voice have you not heard yet?

## Appendix A: Key Timeline

> Note: a larger BCE number is earlier. "c. / traditional" marks a traditional narrative or scholarly estimate; "✅" marks a hard node accepted by most scholars.

- c. 1208 BCE: the Egyptian Merneptah Stele gives the first written mention of "Israel." ✅ (earliest hard evidence)
- Traditional narrative: the Exodus, Moses, the covenant at Mount Sinai (no direct archaeological evidence; mostly seen as a foundational narrative).
- c. 1000 BCE: (traditional) David founds a kingdom and makes Jerusalem the capital.
- c. 957 BCE: (traditional) Solomon builds the First Temple.
- c. 930 BCE: (mostly accepted) the kingdom splits into northern Israel (capital Samaria) and southern Judah (capital Jerusalem).
- c. 9th century BCE: the Tel Dan Stele gives the first mention of the "House of David" outside the Bible. ✅
- 722 BCE: the northern kingdom of Israel falls to the Assyrian Empire; its elite carried off and scattered (source of the "ten lost tribes"). ✅
- c. 622 BCE: Josiah's religious reform, the "discovery" of the law scroll (most think the core of Deuteronomy), centralizing worship in Jerusalem. ✅ (key monotheism turning point)
- 586 BCE: southern Judah and the First Temple fall to the Neo-Babylonian Empire; the elite carried off — the Babylonian Exile begins. ✅
- 586-539 BCE: the exile period; prophets re-read the disaster, the faith is pushed toward thorough monotheism, the center shifts to scripture.
- 539 BCE: the Persian king Cyrus destroys Babylon. ✅
- 538 BCE: Cyrus orders the captives free to return and rebuild the Temple. ✅
- c. 516 BCE: the Second Temple is completed. ✅
- Exile-to-Persian period: (most scholars) the core of the Torah takes final shape; the diaspora pattern forms.

## Appendix B: Core Vocabulary

- **YHWH** — the name of Israel's god (traditionally not spoken aloud out of reverence)
- **monotheism / monolatry** — belief in one and only God / worship of only one's own god while admitting others exist (the difference is the lesson's core)
- **covenant (brit)** — the two-way pact, with obligations on both sides, between the god and his people
- **Torah** — the first five books of the Hebrew Bible; both religion and law
- **Hebrew Bible** — Christians call it the "Old Testament," but the neutral scholarly term is "Hebrew Bible"
- **Canaan / Levant** — the corridor strip along the eastern Mediterranean coast
- **First / Second Temple** — Jerusalem's center of sacrifice (burned 586 BCE / rebuilt c. 516 BCE)
- **Babylonian Exile** — the carrying-off to Babylon from 586 BCE
- **diaspora** — "the scattering"; surviving while spread out yet keeping identity
- **prophet (navi)** — one who, in crisis, speaks for the god and explains what history means
- **Shema** — the core declaration (Deuteronomy 6:4, "YHWH is one")
- **Yehud** — the name of the Persian-ruled province of Judah
- **Merneptah / Tel Dan / Cyrus** — the three key inscriptions (earliest mention of Israel / earliest of the House of David / the return policy)
- **Mandate of Heaven (Tian) / Shangdi** — the Mandate of Heaven (Heaven) / a high god (for the §8 China comparison)
