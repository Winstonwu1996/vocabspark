# Imperialism in Asia · The Opium Wars & British India (1839-1860)

> **Lock status**: narrative kernel v0 — pending review
>
> **AP anchor**: AP World History Modern, Unit 6.2-6.4 (Consequences of Industrialization)
> **Through-line**: an industrial empire's two faces in Asia — informal control of China by treaty, formal conquest of India from company to crown
> **Difficulty**: 4/5 (two theaters + a drug war + a powerful China-heritage connection)
> **Expected duration**: 35-45 min

---

## 0. Open Your Textbook First (Textbook Anchor)

> This belongs to **AP World History Modern, Unit 6 (1750-1900)** — Topics 6.2-6.4, the **Consequences of Industrialization**: state expansion and the responses of colonized peoples.
>
> Your textbook will tell you:
> - The **Industrial Revolution** gave Europe (especially Britain) steam-powered gunships, repeating firearms, and a hunger for raw materials and markets. It pushed that pressure all the way to **China** and **India**.
> - **The First Opium War (1839-1842)** ended with the **Treaty of Nanking (1842)** — the first of the "**unequal treaties**."
> - In India, the **East India Company** ruled until the **1857 Rebellion**, after which the **British Crown** took over directly (the **British Raj**) in 1858.
>
> **What your textbook may NOT make clear** (this is why we need this lesson):
> - **The Opium War was, at its core, a war for the right to sell opium (a drug).** Britain fought to protect a trade that was poisoning millions of Chinese people and draining China's silver.
> - **You don't need to occupy a country to dominate it.** China kept almost all of its territory, yet lost its sovereignty piece by piece through a **treaty system** (extraterritoriality + agreed tariffs + most-favored-nation). Historians call this the **imperialism of free trade**, or **informal empire**.
> - **A *company* conquered a subcontinent — using local people.** The East India Company (EIC) had its own army of Indian soldiers (**sepoys**). A few thousand British ruled hundreds of millions of Indians only because **local elites and Indian soldiers collaborated.**
> - **This is the most China-connected topic in the course.** For many Chinese-heritage students, the Opium War is what their family calls the start of China's "**Century of Humiliation**" — and the American classroom calls "unequal treaties." Same history, two languages.

## 1. One-Sentence Anchor (Many Factions at Once)

In the spring of **1839**, on the coast of southern China at **Canton** (today's Guangzhou), a Qing official named **Lin Zexu** (lin tsuh-SHOO) ordered more than 20,000 chests of British merchants' opium dumped into great pits laced with lime and washed out to sea. He was trying, on the orders of the **Daoguang Emperor** (dow-GWAHNG), to shut down a business that was poisoning millions of Chinese and draining China's silver. Three years later, the most powerful industrial empire on earth answered with steam-powered gunships: China was forced to sign its first "**unequal treaty**" — pay an indemnity, open five ports, hand over Hong Kong. This was a **war fought for the right to sell a drug** — but it pulled in far more than two countries:

- **Britain (actor / the one applying pressure):** fresh out of the Industrial Revolution, hungry for markets and raw materials, and desperate to reverse the silver it was losing to buy Chinese tea. Opium was its most profitable — and most shameful — bargaining chip. What it wanted was not necessarily China's land, but the **right to "do business on my terms."**
- **The Qing empire (an old order under shock):** a vast empire that had long seen itself as the "Celestial Empire that possesses all things," discovering for the first time that its forts, navy, and entire worldview were helpless against an industrialized rival.
- **Ordinary Chinese / Bengali farmers in India (receiving-end · overlooked · DEFAULT):** Chinese people whose families were destroyed by opium addiction, and Indian farmers forced by the East India Company to grow opium (sold to China). They were the fuel at both ends of the machine — and history left them almost no names.
- **Local collaborators (lonely / overlooked):** the **compradors** who handled business for the British, the Indian **sepoys** (SEE-poys, native soldiers) who fought for the East India Company, the Indian princes and landlords who worked with the British. **Without their collaboration, a few thousand British could never have ruled hundreds of millions of Indians** — the layer of imperialism most often left out, and the most important.

At that same moment, on the other side of the world in India, the **East India Company** — a *company*, not a nation — already ruled most of the subcontinent through Indian soldiers and local allies. Put China and India side by side and you see one industrial empire's **two faces**: in China it used "free trade" as the excuse and squeezed without planting a flag; in India it grew, step by step, from a trading company into a government.

## 2. The Stage (Geography / Economy / Society / Institutions / Events)

- **Geography:** two stages. ① **South China's coast** — Canton (Guangzhou) was the only port the Qing opened to the West (the "**Canton System**"); all China-West trade went through this one gate, with only officially licensed merchants. Outside the Pearl River mouth stood the **Humen** (hoo-MUN, "Bocca Tigris") forts. ② **The Indian subcontinent** — **Bengal** was the East India Company's earliest base and India's main opium-growing region; the Ganges plain was the heart of the 1857 rebellion; in Delhi sat the now-powerless **Mughal** emperor.
- **Economy:** one **triangular trade** is the key to the whole picture. The British loved Chinese tea, so silver flowed into China, and Britain hated the deficit. The fix: the East India Company grew **opium in India** → smuggled it into **China** for silver → used the silver to buy Chinese tea. Opium reversed the deficit — at the cost of China's silver draining out, millions of Chinese addicted, and Indian farmers forced to grow a crop they could not eat.
- **Society:** in China, opium spread from the coast inland, from officials to soldiers to commoners — addicts numbered in the millions, hollowing out society and state finances. In India, society was cut by Britain's "**divide and rule**" into countless pieces: Hindus and Muslims, dozens of princely states, castes, landlords (**zamindar**, zuh-meen-DAR) and tenants — and the East India Company sat on those cracks, using one group against another.
- **Institutions:** ① the **unequal-treaty system** — the 1842 Treaty of Nanking opened it, and later treaties stacked on **extraterritoriality** (foreigners who break Chinese law are not tried by China), **agreed tariffs** (China cannot set its own tax rates), and **most-favored-nation** (any privilege one power wins, all powers automatically share). This did not occupy China, yet peeled away its sovereignty layer by layer — the **imperialism of free trade**. ② the **East India Company (EIC)** — a chartered company with its own army, the power to tax, and the power to make war, the extreme case of "company as government." It fought with **Indian soldiers (sepoys)** and governed by **collaborating with local princes and landlords**.
- **Events:** 1839 destruction of opium at Humen → 1839-42 First Opium War → 1842 Treaty of Nanking → 1856-60 Second Opium War (Anglo-French, 1860 burning of the Old Summer Palace). India line: 1757 Battle of Plassey (EIC takes Bengal) → a century of company rule → 1857 Indian Rebellion → 1858 Government of India Act, the company abolished, the British Crown ruling directly (British Raj).

## 3. The Main Characters

> Three perspectives (actor/perpetrator, lonely mediator/collaborator, receiving-end), including at least one powerless party.

### Lin Zexu (actor on the Chinese side — but also a man trapped by an old worldview) — 1785-1850 · the commissioner who dumped opium into the sea yet could not stop the gunships

A real historical person. Born August 30, 1785, in Houguan (today's Fuzhou), Fujian province; died November 22, 1850. He was a famously honest and capable Qing official. In 1838 the Daoguang Emperor named him **Special Imperial Commissioner** and sent him south to Canton to stamp out opium. In the spring of 1839 he forced British and American merchants to surrender more than 20,000 chests of opium, and destroyed it in public at **Humen** in great pits mixed with lime and salt — the work took over twenty days as the opium dissolved into the sea.

He was not a fool — quite the opposite. He was one of the earliest Qing officials to try to understand the outside world. He had Western newspapers and books translated to figure out who these foreigners really were. He even wrote an open **letter to Britain's Queen Victoria**, arguing on **moral** grounds: your own country bans opium, so why sell something you know is poison to harm China? Britain ignored the letter entirely.

Lin Zexu's tragedy: what he did was **almost morally unassailable** — banning a business that was poisoning his own people — yet he and the whole Qing court **misjudged the scale of their opponent's power.** He thought he faced "merchants" who could be reasoned with. He actually faced an industrial empire newly armed with steam and steel. Once war began, the Qing lost battle after battle, and Lin Zexu became the scapegoat: dismissed and exiled to Xinjiang. This perspective shows you how an honest, intelligent, dutiful man can do the right thing and still lose the whole board, in an age he cannot fully see.

### The local agent who worked for the British (collaborator / overlooked) — the comprador in Canton & the sepoy in India

A composite perspective, with a fully real role, and no single name — because they stand for the **most crucial and most uncomfortable layer** of imperialism.

**In China:** the **comprador** (kom-pruh-DOR) — the Chinese middleman who connected, translated, kept books, and sourced goods for foreign firms. He lived off the foreigners, yet his fellow Chinese saw him as "the one who helps the foreigners." The opium business could never have reached the interior on British effort alone — it took a long chain of Chinese smugglers, merchants, and local officials looking the other way to push the drug into countless homes. Imperialism was never a one-sided invasion from outside; it needed local collaborators.

**In India:** the **sepoy** — an Indian who soldiered for the East India Company. Here is the most startling fact: at the 1757 Battle of Plassey, and in the company's later conquest of all India, **the main force was Indians fighting Indians.** Under one British officer stood hundreds or thousands of Indian soldiers drawing British pay. A few thousand British could rule hundreds of millions of Indians only because of this army of Indians, plus collaboration with local princes and landlords (zamindar).

This perspective forces you to face the hardest part of Rule 0: **oppression is not a simple "them vs. us."** Without local collaboration, the system could not run for a single day. But collaborators had situations of their own — survival, profit, finding a place when the old order was collapsing. In 1857, when Britain's new cartridges (rumored to be greased with pig and cow fat) insulted the soldiers' religion, it was these very depended-upon sepoys who turned their guns around — the layer imperialism relied on most could also be the layer that overturned it.

### The Chinese man in an opium den & the Bengali farmer forced to grow opium (receiving-end · overlooked · DEFAULT)

A composite perspective, with a fully real role (this is the DEFAULT). No exact names — because history left them almost none, and that, exactly, is part of the problem. This lens links the **fuel at both ends** of the machine.

**One end of the machine:** a Chinese man in an opium den in Canton. He might have been a porter, a shop hand, a minor clerk. The pipe roasts a burnt-sweet smoke over the oil lamp, and in the moment of inhaling, the exhaustion and pain melt away — then come the growing addiction, the emptying purse, the pawned and sold land, the sold children, and at last a ruined family. He is not a statistic. He is the lowest-down bearer of this "trade."

**The other end of the machine:** a farmer in Bengal, India, growing opium. The East India Company bound him with advance payments and contracts, forced him to plant poppies on land that should have grown food, and bought the whole crop at a low fixed price to process into opium and ship to China. What he grew, he could not eat or use; the price was the company's to set; and in a bad year, with no grain in the field, people starved.

This perspective is the conscience of the lesson. AP World keeps testing the **agency and the cost borne by colonized peoples** — but before "agency," students must first hear the people who left **no names at all.** Behind the word "war" in "Opium War" are two of the most powerless groups on earth, caught between two countries, ground from both ends by the same machine.

## 4. The Story (Plot Beats)

1. **Tea, silver, and the deficit (18th century).** The British loved Chinese tea, and silver poured into China. Under the Canton System, the West could trade only through Canton, only with licensed merchants. Britain could not stand the silver deficit and searched for something to sell China to reverse it.
2. **Opium became the cure (and the poison).** The East India Company grew opium on a huge scale in India (Bengal), smuggled it into China for silver, then used the silver to buy tea. The triangle turned — the deficit reversed, at the cost of millions of Chinese addicted, silver draining out, and Indian farmers forced to grow poppies.
3. **Daoguang resolves to ban opium and sends Lin Zexu (1838-39).** Opium was hollowing out the treasury and poisoning soldiers and civilians. The Daoguang Emperor resolved on a strict ban. In 1839 Lin Zexu reached Canton, forced the surrender of more than 20,000 chests of opium, and destroyed it in public at **Humen**.
4. **The First Opium War (1839-1842).** Britain went to war on the grounds that "merchants' property was destroyed and trade was blocked." Steam gunships and repeating firepower against the Qing's old-style navy and forts — the Qing lost battle after battle. Lin Zexu was dismissed and exiled, the scapegoat.
5. **The 1842 Treaty of Nanking — the first unequal treaty.** Signed August 29, 1842: China paid an indemnity of **21 million silver dollars**, opened **five ports** (Canton, Amoy, Foochow, Ningpo, Shanghai), and ceded **Hong Kong Island** to Britain "**in perpetuity**." This began the age of "unequal treaties."
6. **The treaty system stacks up.** Later treaties added **extraterritoriality** (foreigners not tried by Chinese law), **agreed tariffs** (China could not set its own tax rates), and **most-favored-nation** (one power's gain, all powers share). China's territory was not occupied, yet its sovereignty was peeled away piece by piece — the imperialism of free trade / informal empire.
7. **The Second Opium War (1856-1860).** Britain (with France) went to war again to demand more trade rights. The 1858 Treaty of Tientsin was signed and then refused ratification by the Qing emperor, so fighting resumed.
8. **1860 — the burning of the Old Summer Palace.** Anglo-French forces entered Beijing. Because members of the Anglo-French negotiating mission had been captured and some killed, the British commander **Lord Elgin** ordered the looting and burning of the **Old Summer Palace (Yuanmingyuan)** — the fire burned for three days and nights. The Xianfeng Emperor fled north; Prince Gong stayed to negotiate and signed the **Convention of Peking** on October 24, 1860. Russia exploited the moment, using both treaties to seize vast territories in China's northeast and Far East (later building Vladivostok).
9. **The India line: from company to crown (running in parallel).** As early as the 1757 **Battle of Plassey**, the East India Company took Bengal through local allies and Indian sepoys. Over the next century, a *company* — through sepoys and local collaborators — came to rule most of India.
10. **The 1857 Indian Rebellion (Sepoy Mutiny / First War of Independence).** The trigger: the new Enfield rifle's paper cartridges were rumored to be greased with pig and cow fat — and biting them open offended both Muslims (pig) and Hindus (cow). On March 29, 1857, the sepoy **Mangal Pandey** rose first; on May 10 the mutiny exploded at **Meerut**, and the rebels made the aged last Mughal emperor **Bahadur Shah Zafar** their nominal leader.
11. **Suppression and reckoning (1857-1858).** The rebellion was crushed with bloodshed, with massacres on both sides. The last Mughal emperor was deposed and exiled to Rangoon, Burma, where he died in 1862 — ending the Mughal dynasty.
12. **The 1858 Government of India Act — the British Raj is born.** On August 2, 1858, the British Parliament passed an act abolishing the East India Company and transferring rule of India from the *company* directly to the **British Crown.** From then on Britain ruled India directly — the British Raj — until independence in 1947.

**An irony to keep:** an empire that called itself the champion of "free trade" and "civilization" went to war for the right to **sell opium**; and a *company* that owned an army conquered a subcontinent — using soldiers who were mostly Indians themselves.

## 5. Reading the Documents (Primary Sources)

### A. Lin Zexu's open letter to Queen Victoria (1839)
> Meaning (paraphrased): Your own country, Britain, strictly bans the smoking of opium — so you know it harms people. If you know it is poison, why ship it to harm China? Please ban it in your land and cut off this trade.
> Plain reading: Lin Zexu wrote to Britain's queen with the simplest argument — you don't even allow this in your own country, so what gives you the right to sell it to harm us?
> Many-sided reading: the letter is morally almost unanswerable, but it rests on an old worldview — that the other side was a reasonable "merchant" who could be persuaded by morality. Britain never replied. The letter's "failure" itself measures the gap in power and logic between two worlds.

### B. The 1842 Treaty of Nanking — core terms (in spirit)
> Content: China pays 21 million silver dollars; opens five ports (Canton, Amoy, Foochow, Ningpo, Shanghai); cedes Hong Kong Island to Britain "in perpetuity."
> Plain reading: the China that lost the war was forced to pay a huge sum, forced to open five ports for trade, and forced to hand Hong Kong Island to Britain.
> Reading: this is the literal source of the phrase "unequal treaty" — the obligations are badly lopsided, all giving by China, all gaining by Britain. Its deepest effect is not the indemnity or the lost island, but the **treaty system** it opened: the extraterritoriality, agreed tariffs, and most-favored-nation that stacked on later were what truly peeled away China's sovereignty.

### C. The "extraterritoriality + agreed tariff + most-favored-nation" package (the logic of the treaty system)
> Content: extraterritoriality = foreigners who break Chinese law are not tried by China; agreed tariff = China's tax rates must be negotiated with the powers, not set freely; most-favored-nation = any benefit one country gets from China, all the others automatically share.
> Plain reading: foreigners breaking the law in China couldn't be judged by China; China couldn't even decide its own customs rates; and once one country got a deal, every country got it too.
> Reading: the "cleverness" of this machinery is that it could **control China without occupying Chinese territory** — the essence of the imperialism of free trade. Understanding this package explains why China calls this period "semi-colonial" rather than "colonial."

### D. The trigger of 1857: greased cartridges (note: this is a motive-level reading, not the only cause)
> The account as it is passed down: the new Enfield rifle's paper cartridges were rumored to be greased with pig and cow fat, and loading required biting them open with the teeth — offending both Muslims (who view pigs as unclean) and Hindus (who view cows as sacred). Soldiers believed Britain was deliberately humiliating them and trying to force a religious conversion.
> Plain reading: the new cartridges had to be bitten open and were said to be coated in pig and cow fat — which hit the taboos of two great religions at once, and the soldiers had had enough.
> Reading: the greased cartridge was the **trigger**, not the **root cause.** What it set off was a long-packed powder keg: aggressive annexation of princely states, disrespect for religion and custom, the stripping of taxes and land rights, and discrimination against Indian soldiers. To reduce 1857 to "a single cartridge" is to miss all the tensions that imperial rule itself had piled up.

## 6. Consequences (Short / Medium / Long Term)

- **Short term (1842-1860s):** China entered the age of "unequal treaties" — five open ports, Hong Kong ceded, extraterritoriality and agreed tariffs in place; the burning of the Summer Palace became a symbol of national humiliation. In India, the 1857 rebellion was crushed, the Mughal dynasty ended, the East India Company was abolished, and from 1858 the British Crown ruled directly (the British Raj).
- **Medium term (later 19th century):** China was surrounded by ever-mounting treaties and "spheres of influence" from one power after another (Britain, France, Russia, later Japan, Germany, the U.S.), its sovereignty steadily bleeding away, which drove waves of self-strengthening attempts from the Self-Strengthening Movement to the 1898 Reforms. In India under the Raj, Britain built railways and telegraphs, unified administration, and spread English education — but also caused **deindustrialization** (local handicrafts crushed by British factory goods), recurring **great famines**, and a **drain of wealth.** Modernization and exploitation lived in the same body.
- **Long term (to today):** ① For China, the Opium War is recognized as **the start of China's modern history** and the **starting point of the "Century of Humiliation" narrative** — deeply shaping modern China's national feeling and its acute sensitivity to sovereignty and outside interference, to this day. Hong Kong only returned in 1997. ② For India, 1857 was later claimed by nationalists as the "First War of Independence," and the Raj's unified administration and English unexpectedly gave the later all-India national movement (up to independence in 1947) a shared framework. ③ For world history, China (an "informal empire" with no land taken) and India (formal colonization, from company to crown) became the classic contrasting samples of imperialism's **two models.**

## 7. AP DBQ Thinking Practice

- **Stakeholder grid:** the British government / British merchants and the East India Company / the Qing government / Chinese compradors and smuggling networks / Chinese opium addicts / Bengali opium farmers / Indian sepoys / Indian princes and landlords / the last Mughal emperor — between 1839 and 1858, what did each want, and what cards did each hold? Whose interests briefly overlapped (hint: the local collaborators)?
- **The imperialism of free trade (the key tested angle):** does imperialism require occupying land and planting a flag? Use China to argue for **informal empire** — no occupation, control through unequal treaties (extraterritoriality + agreed tariffs + most-favored-nation) and local agents; then contrast India as **formal empire** (company → direct crown rule). This is an argument AP graders reward highly.
- **Responses to imperialism (the key three-way split):** resistance (Lin Zexu's opium ban, the 1857 rebellion) / adaptation and reform (the Self-Strengthening Movement, reformers) / collaboration (compradors, sepoys, princes). All three coexisted — not either/or. Use evidence to show the splits *within* a single colonized society.
- **Continuity & change:** from 1839 (China still seeing itself as the Celestial Empire, India still under company rule) to 1860/1858 (China caught in the treaty system, India under direct crown rule) — what changed completely (sovereignty, who held power, place in the world order), and what stayed the same or got worse (the continuation of exploitation, the situation of those at the bottom)?
- **Cross-regional comparison · against the "Scramble for Africa" (1880s-1900s):** Asia (informal control of China; gradual company conquest of India) vs. Africa (formal partition and direct, drawn-border occupation after the 1884 Berlin Conference). All driven by industrialization — so why different methods for China / India / Africa? (Hint: existing state strength, existing trade relations, the presence or absence of local collaborators.)

## 8. China in the Same Era (Structural Comparison · Rule 0 veto dimension)

> Note: in this lesson China is not a "distant comparison group" — **China is one of the main stages.** The job of this section is to connect "the Opium War the American classroom teaches" with "the Opium War a Chinese-heritage student hears at home," and to contrast it sharply with **Meiji Japan (already a live topic).**

1839 was the 19th year of the Daoguang reign. At this moment the Qing still saw itself as the "Celestial Empire that possesses all things in abundance" — the very phrase the Qianlong Emperor used in 1793 to politely refuse Britain's Macartney trade mission. Half a century later, the Opium War's gunships punctured that self-image: the "foreigners'" power was simply no longer in the same class.

**The two languages — home and school** (this is the part that touches Chinese-heritage students directly):
- **The language at home:** many Chinese-heritage students' grandparents and parents can name "**the destruction of opium at Humen**," "**the cession of Hong Kong**," "**the burning of the Summer Palace**," "**the Century of Humiliation**" — a narrative full of national feeling, about how China fell from "Celestial Empire" to rock bottom, and how it rose again.
- **The language at school:** the American AP classroom teaches "unequal treaties," "treaty ports," "extraterritoriality," "imperialism of free trade" — a colder, more structural language stressing "this is one case of industrialized global expansion."
- **What this section does:** not to make students choose one language over the other, but to see that **both describe the same history, just from different positions and with different feelings.** The "humiliation" at home is real; the "structural analysis" at school is real too — and AP precisely asks for the ability to hold both at once.

**A sharp contrast with the Meiji Restoration** (a live topic — teach them together):
- China and Japan were pushed to their doors by Western gunships at almost the same time (China 1839-42, Japan 1853 "Black Ships"). They faced **almost the same shock.**
- **But the responses were opposite:** Japan, through the **Meiji Restoration (1868)**, **actively and thoroughly** learned from the West and remade its entire state from the top down — and within decades joined the great powers and built its own empire (defeating the Qing in the 1894-95 war). China's self-rescue (the Self-Strengthening Movement, the 1898 Reforms) was repeatedly blocked and never thorough.
- **This is one of the most charged contrasts in world history:** the same outside shock — so why did one turn into a "great power" and the other keep sinking? The answer involves political structure, the thoroughness of reform, and the resistance of vested interests — a comparison AP loves to test.

Rule 0: we cannot say China "deserved to fall behind," nor that the West was "advanced and therefore right to sell opium." In the Opium War, **the injustice of the aggression** (going to war to sell a drug) and **the rigidity of the Qing** (failing to see the world, failing to reform) **can both be true at once** — one is a moral judgment, the other a structural analysis; they are on different levels and do not cancel out.

## 9. What Historians Argue About (Historiography)

- **The "imperialism of free trade" school (Gallagher & Robinson, 1953):** the classic article *The Imperialism of Free Trade* argues that the goal of 19th-century British imperialism was always to open markets on free-trade terms — preferring collaboration with local elites (informal empire) and annexing formally only when necessary (formal empire). China is the model of "informal empire." This school overturned the old idea that "imperialism = taking territory."
- **China's "Century of Humiliation" national historiography:** sets the Opium War as the start of China's modern history and the beginning of humiliation and national salvation, stressing the structural harm of foreign aggression. This is the framework Chinese-heritage students know best.
- **Revisionist / internal-factors school:** stresses the Qing's own institutional rigidity, financial and military backwardness, and misreading of the outside world as internal reasons China was beaten — not blaming everything on outside aggression. (Not mutually exclusive with the above; it is a debate of "external" vs. "internal" causes.)
- **Collaboration studies:** examines the role of compradors, sepoys, and local princes, arguing that colonial rule depended on local collaboration and that the "oppressor vs. oppressed" binary is too simple. This school is also often criticized for risking the "downplaying" of aggression — and that very argument is Rule 0 in action.
- **The debate over colonialism's "complicated consequences":** was the British Raj "plunder and famine" or "railways and modernization"? The mainstream synthesis: both are true at once — colonial rule brought infrastructure and unified administration AND caused deindustrialization, drain of wealth, and great famines. You cannot take only one side.
- **A neutral synthesis:** Asian imperialism was both the naked aggression and exploitation of an industrial power, AND a process involving local collaboration, internal division, and complicated modernizing consequences. The injustice of the aggression and the complexity of its effects are true at the same time; do not make it either/or.

## 10. Common Misconceptions (Myth-Busting)

1. ❌ "The Opium War was just a trade dispute, Britain wanting to sell a bit more." → ✅ At its core it was a war an industrial empire fought **to protect its right to sell opium (a drug) and to force unequal trade** — far more than a "trade dispute."
2. ❌ "China was directly colonized by Britain and became a colony." → ✅ China kept almost all of its territory (apart from a few ceded or leased places like Hong Kong); it was a "**semi-colony**" / "informal empire," stripped of sovereignty from inside through the treaty system — exactly its key difference from India.
3. ❌ "Lin Zexu was a stubborn bureaucrat who didn't understand the world." → ✅ He was one of the earliest Qing officials to try to see the world clearly and to organize translations of Western material; his failure came not from ignorance but from the whole Qing **misjudging the scale of the opponent's power.**
4. ❌ "India was conquered directly by the British army." → ✅ Early conquest and daily rule depended mainly on **Indian soldiers (sepoys) and local collaborators**; a few thousand British ruled hundreds of millions of Indians through this collaboration.
5. ❌ "The 1857 rebellion was about one greased cartridge." → ✅ The greased cartridge was only the **trigger**; the root causes were annexation policy, disrespect for religion, the stripping of tax and land rights, and discrimination against Indian soldiers.
6. ❌ "British India = pure plunder" OR "British India = progress that brought modernization." → ✅ Both faces share one body: railways, telegraphs, unified administration, and English, AND deindustrialization, drain of wealth, and recurring great famines. You cannot take only one side.
7. ❌ "The Opium War is something Chinese people exaggerate out of grudge." → ✅ It is the **scholarly-recognized** start of China's modern history and the real starting point of the "Century of Humiliation" — the unequal treaties, ceded land, and indemnities are all documented facts.
8. ❌ "China and Japan faced the same shock, so the different outcomes prove one people was superior." → ✅ The difference lies in **analyzable factors** — political structure, the thoroughness of reform, the resistance of vested interests — not any ranking of peoples.

## 11. Thinking Questions (Open-Ended, No Conclusion)

1. What Lin Zexu did was morally almost unassailable — banning a business that poisoned his own people — yet he lost, and was made the scapegoat. When a person "does the right thing" but "misreads the age," is the fault his, or the age's? Can the two even be separated?
2. What imperialism relied on most was the local collaborators (compradors, sepoys, princes). Without them, a few thousand British could not rule hundreds of millions of Indians. How do you see these "collaborators" — as traitors, as survivors, or as another group trapped by the same machine?
3. The British Raj built railways and unified administration, and it caused famine and a drain of wealth. If one kind of rule brings both "modernization" and "catastrophe," how should you "score" it? Or is "scoring" itself the wrong way to ask the question?
4. Pushed to their doors by the same Western gunships, Japan turned into a great power and China kept sinking. Setting the two paths side by side, do you see "a ranking of peoples," or "different structures reacting differently to the same shock"?
5. Your family elders may describe the Opium War with "humiliation" and "ceded land"; your school textbook may describe it with "unequal treaties" and "free trade." Are these two languages describing the same history? Which is more "true"? Or do they each grasp one side of the truth?
6. This time you mostly heard the bearers and the collaborators — the man in the opium den, the farmer forced to grow poppies, the sepoy who turned his gun around. How would Lin Zexu tell this war? How would a British merchant defend himself? Whose voice have you not heard yet?

## Appendix A: Key Timeline

- 1757: Battle of Plassey — the East India Company takes Bengal through local allies and Indian sepoys, beginning company rule in India.
- 1785.8.30: Lin Zexu born in Houguan (today's Fuzhou), Fujian.
- 1793: Britain's Macartney mission seeks trade with China and is politely refused by the Qianlong Emperor ("the empire possesses all things in abundance").
- 1820-1850: the Daoguang Emperor reigns.
- 1838-39: Daoguang names Lin Zexu Special Imperial Commissioner and sends him to Canton to ban opium.
- 1839 (spring): destruction of opium at Humen — Lin Zexu publicly destroys more than 20,000 chests of seized opium.
- 1839-1842: the First Opium War.
- 1842.8.29: the Treaty of Nanking is signed (21 million silver dollars indemnity; five ports opened; Hong Kong Island ceded "in perpetuity") — the first unequal treaty.
- 1850.11.22: Lin Zexu dies.
- 1856-1860: the Second Opium War (Anglo-French, also called the Arrow War).
- 1857.3.29: the sepoy Mangal Pandey rises first (the greased-cartridge affair).
- 1857.5.10: mutiny at Meerut — the 1857 Indian Rebellion breaks out in full; rebels make the last Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar their nominal leader.
- 1858.8.2: the British Parliament passes the Government of India Act, abolishing the East India Company; India comes under direct British Crown rule — the British Raj is born.
- 1860: Anglo-French forces enter Beijing, loot and burn the Old Summer Palace in October; the Convention of Peking is signed October 24 (Russia seizes vast northeast and Far East territories).
- 1862: the last Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar dies in exile in Rangoon, Burma — the Mughal dynasty ends.

## Appendix B: Core Vocabulary

- **Opium Wars** — First 1839-42, Second 1856-60
- **Destruction of opium at Humen** — Lin Zexu's 1839 public destruction of opium
- **Treaty of Nanking** — 1842, the first unequal treaty
- **unequal treaties** — badly lopsided treaties forced on China by foreign powers
- **extraterritoriality** — foreigners who break Chinese law are not tried by China
- **agreed tariff** — China could not set its own customs rates
- **most-favored-nation** — a privilege one power wins, all powers automatically share
- **treaty ports** — ports forced open to foreign trade (the first five)
- **imperialism of free trade** — control without occupation, via treaties and agents (Gallagher & Robinson)
- **informal empire** — opposed to formal empire (direct colonization)
- **East India Company (EIC)** — a chartered British company with its own army and taxing/war powers
- **sepoy** — an Indian who soldiered for the East India Company
- **British Raj** — direct British Crown rule of India from 1858
- **Mughal** — India's old dynasty, powerless by 1857
- **comprador** — a Chinese middleman working for foreign firms
- **collaboration** — the local cooperation imperialism depended on
- **Century of Humiliation** — China's national narrative framework for its modern history
