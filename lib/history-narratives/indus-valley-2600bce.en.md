# Indus Valley (Harappan) Civilization · c. 2600 BCE

> **Lock status**: narrative kernel v0 — pending review
>
> **Anchor**: CA HSS Grade 6, Standard 6.5 (Ancient India begins here) · early river civilizations
> **Through-line**: a top-tier ancient city civilization with NO visible palace, no great temple, almost no war — and a script no one can read
> **Difficulty**: 4/5 (heavy on "arguing from what is missing" + radical uncertainty)
> **Expected duration**: 35-45 min

---

## 0. Open Your Textbook First (Textbook Anchor)

> This belongs to **CA HSS Grade 6, Standard 6.5 (Ancient India)**. California's standards say ancient India **starts with the Indus Valley Civilization** — before the Vedic age, before the Mauryan empire.
>
> Your textbook will tell you:
> - The **Indus Valley (Harappan) Civilization** was one of the **four great early river civilizations**, alongside **Mesopotamia** (Tigris-Euphrates), **Egypt** (Nile), and **China** (Yellow River).
> - Its mature, "golden age" phase ran **c. 2600-1900 BCE**.
> - Its two most famous cities were **Harappa** (huh-RAP-uh) and **Mohenjo-daro** (moh-HEN-joh DAH-roh).
>
> **What your textbook may NOT make clear** (this is why we need this lesson):
> - It was the **largest in area** of the four early civilizations (about **1 million square kilometers**) — yet it is the one we know the **least** about, because its writing has never been read.
> - Archaeologists are stunned not by what it has, but by what it is **missing**: after 100+ years of digging, **no royal palace, no single dominant temple, no giant statue or tomb glorifying a ruler, and almost no signs of large-scale war** have been found.
> - It had advanced **city planning** (grid streets), an advanced **drainage/sewer system**, **standardized weights and measures**, and a **script that is still undeciphered**.
> - **The big question this raises:** does a civilization **have** to have a strong central power (a king, a palace, a great temple) to run? This lesson starts from that mystery of "what's missing."
> - **Chinese parents' biggest blind spot:** Chinese textbooks barely cover this; most parents from a Chinese background cannot name Mohenjo-daro, let alone explain "no palace, no big temple, almost no war." This is a true "parents can't help" topic.
> - **Where it fits on Know U.:** ancient India currently only has the Mauryan course (c. 261 BCE, Ashoka). This lesson fills in the **very first page** of ancient India.

## 1. One-Sentence Anchor (Many Views at Once)

Around **2600 BCE**, on a plain spread along the **Indus River** in today's Pakistan and northwestern India, **pre-planned cities** appeared. Streets ran straight, crossing in a grid like a chessboard. Nearly every house had its own bathing room and toilet, and dirty water flowed away through **covered drains** — a sewer system so advanced that one scholar said you would have to wait more than two thousand years, for the Romans, to find anything comparable.

But what makes archaeologists scratch their heads is not what it **had** — it is what it was **missing**:

- **No royal palace has been dug up.** Other early civilizations have palaces for kings. Not here.
- **No single dominant temple.** Egypt has pyramids; Mesopotamia has towering temple-mountains (ziggurats). Here, there is no one religious building that towers over the whole city.
- **No giant statue, royal tomb, or victory monument glorifying a ruler.** No one left a "look, I built this."
- **Almost no signs of large-scale war.** No piles of weapons, no layers of massacre bones; the walls look more like flood control and management than defense against armies.

At the same time, far away on the Yellow and Yangtze rivers, China was on the eve of the shift from **Longshan** culture toward Erlitou — within a few centuries, cities with clear palace foundations and a sharp ranked order would appear. On the Indus, by contrast, dozens of cities rose to the **same standard**, with weights so uniform it is astonishing — yet **no single king sitting at the top giving orders** can be seen.

So we are left with a question that a 7th grader and a college student can both chew on: **does a civilization have to have a strong central power (a king, a palace, a temple) to work?** This lesson begins with that puzzle of "what's missing."

## 2. The Stage (Geography / Climate / Society / Institutions / Events)

- **Geography**: The core was the great plain built up by the **Indus River** and its tributaries, stretching across today's Pakistan, northwestern India, and the edge of northeastern Afghanistan. At its peak it covered about **1 million square kilometers** — the **largest in area** of the early civilizations of its time (larger in spread than Mesopotamia or Egypt). The two most famous cities: **Harappa** in the north (Punjab) and **Mohenjo-daro** in the south (Sindh). A now mostly dried-up ancient river channel, the **Ghaggar-Hakra**, also has dense clusters of sites along it (some Indian nationalist scholars link it to the legendary Saraswati river — this is disputed; see §9).
- **Climate**: Farming depended on the monsoon rains and the rich silt of river floods — wheat, barley, **cotton** (this is one of the earliest places on earth to grow cotton and weave cotton cloth). Cities were built by the river, where **floods** were both the source of fertility and a threat overhead — the raised platforms (the citadel) of many cities may have been built up exactly for flood protection. Later, the climate turned drier and the monsoon weakened — a key piece of the decline (see §4).
- **Society**: Total population at peak is estimated between **one and five million** (a very wide range, because evidence is limited). Big cities like Mohenjo-daro and Harappa may each have held **30,000 to 60,000 people**. The most unusual point: from the houses dug up, the **gap between rich and poor is not obvious** — most households' homes, goods, and access to sanitation differ far less than in other ancient civilizations. Some scholars say it "may be the most egalitarian early complex society." But remember: **"we can't see an obvious king or super-rich" does NOT mean "everyone was truly equal"** — power and inequality might have existed in forms we cannot read today (see §10).
- **Institutions**: This is the most amazing and most blank box. We see **intense standardization** — cities used the same size of fired brick, streets laid out on a grid, and weights and measures (especially a precise set of **stone weights**) nearly identical across cities hundreds of kilometers apart. That uniformity **must** have required some kind of organizing coordination. But we have found **no document** telling us what that organization was: a king? a council of priests? a merchants' guild? some arrangement we have no word for? **The script is undeciphered, so the institution is a black box.** We can only reason backward from "the bricks are the same size, the weights are the same" to "someone was unifying this" — without being able to say who that someone (or group) was.
- **Events**: Strictly speaking, this civilization has almost **no "events" we can date to a year** — no readable chronicle, no "in year X king Y fought battle Z." What we have are **phases**: early farming villages like Mehrgarh from c. 7000 BCE → Early Harappan from c. 3300 BCE → the **Mature Harappan** phase, c. **2600 to 1900 BCE**, when Harappa, Mohenjo-daro and the other big cities flourished together (the "golden age" this lesson focuses on) → from c. 1900 BCE, gradual **de-urbanization**, cities abandoned, people migrating east. This lesson is about those roughly 700 years, 2600-1900 BCE.

## 3. The Main Characters

> Three perspectives (actor / mediator / receiving-end), including at least one powerless party. This topic's twist: **no named ruler survives**, so all three are realistic composite bridging characters — and the fact that **even the king has no name** is this civilization's single biggest trait.

### The "organizer" of Mohenjo-daro (actor · but no crown) — someone who kept the whole city running to standard

A bridging character, with a fully real role, **deliberately given no royal title** — because to this day no evidence proves this city had a king. What is certain: the city **must** have had someone coordinating. Grid streets, drains reaching every house, hundreds of thousands of fired bricks the same size, weights nearly identical across cities — these do not happen by themselves. There had to be some "organizer": maybe a priest, maybe a group of merchant elders, maybe a committee we have no word for today.

What this person worries about every day is **order and engineering**, not conquest: Will the river flood the city this year? Does the waterproof bitumen layer of the public Great Bath need repair? Do the weights the new caravan brought match the city's standard? The symbols of his power are not swords and armor — they are **the weight, the brick mold, the drainage plan.**

This perspective shows you a **strange kind of power**: it does not show off with a palace and an army, but hides inside "making everything the same" — standardization. It forces you to ask: does power have to look like "king + palace + army"? Or is "making every brick the same size and all the water drain away" itself another kind of rule we are simply not used to?

### A Meluhha merchant trading west to Mesopotamia (mediator) — selling Indus goods at Sargon's docks

A bridging character, but **the trade itself is hard evidence**. Cuneiform clay tablets from Mesopotamia really do mention a distant land called **Meluhha** (meh-LOO-hah) — scholars generally agree Meluhha means the Indus Valley. The Akkadian king **Sargon** (SAR-gon, c. 2334-2284 BCE) left an inscription boasting that "ships from Meluhha, Magan, and Dilmun made fast at the docks of Akkad."

This merchant is the person linking two worlds. He sets out from the Indus cities carrying the region's signature products: beads ground from **carnelian** (a red-orange stone) and etched with white patterns (this craft is an Indus trademark), ivory, cotton cloth, and square **seals** carved with animals and undeciphered writing. These goods really have been dug up in tombs at Ur and Susa in Mesopotamia, dated to 2600-2000 BCE.

He is the "mediator" view: standing in two civilizations, he knows Mesopotamia has tall temple-mountains and statues of kings, and then he looks back at his own city — strange, we have nothing like that, but our drains and our weights are something they cannot match. Through him, you can place the Indus civilization **inside the trade network of its own time**, instead of treating it as an isolated wonder.

### A child in an ordinary household in a grid city (receiving-end · DEFAULT) — an ordinary resident of Mohenjo-daro's lower town

A bridging character, with a fully real role (this is the DEFAULT perspective). No name — because this civilization **left no name for any ordinary person, and not even for its rulers.** And that is the point: in Egypt and Mesopotamia, kings are remembered; here, **even the king is not remembered**, so ordinary people are an even deeper silence.

What is certain is this child's everyday surroundings — and they are stunningly good: a solid fired-brick house, **with its own bathroom and maybe even a flush toilet**, with dirty water flowing away through covered drains in the walls and under the street; public wells on the street; and that mysterious Great Bath in the city. Compared with commoners in many places of the same era, his sanitation is top-tier. He might play with clay toys and a little wheeled ox-cart — these have really been dug up at the sites.

But this perspective also faces our ignorance head-on: we do not know what gods he believed in (no documents), do not know which class he belonged to, do not know whether his family was "middle class" or whether a hidden order we cannot see lay behind all that neatness. He lived in a world astonishingly advanced in engineering and astonishingly silent in record.

This perspective is the heart of the lesson: it lets you stand in the **most ordinary, most nameless, most overlooked** position and experience a strange society where "everyone's sanitation is decent, yet you cannot even find out who the king was" — and honestly admit: **how much of the "equality" we see is real, and how much is just because we cannot read their writing or see their inequality?**

## 4. The Story (Plot Beats)

> Reminder: this civilization has no readable writing. The "story" below is what archaeology **reasoned out from the ground, from the bricks, from the weights** — it is not a chronicle any ancient person wrote down. Every beat rests on physical evidence plus inference.

1. **Farmers come down to the river plain (c. 7000-3300 BCE).** At earlier sites like Mehrgarh, people already grew wheat, raised cattle, and lived in villages. Slowly, population gathered onto the great Indus plain, with its rich flood silt. This is the long run-up before "cities."
2. **Cities grow from a blueprint (c. 2600 BCE, the Mature phase begins).** This is not a village spreading out on its own; it looks like **someone planned first, then built**: grid streets, cities split into a citadel and a lower town, almost all cities using the same size of fired brick. Harappa and Mohenjo-daro peak together.
3. **Drains and the Great Bath: the height of sanitation engineering.** Houses had bathrooms, many had toilets, and dirty water went into the city's **covered drains**. Mohenjo-daro's "Great Bath" (about 12 meters long, sealed with waterproof bitumen) was likely used for some kind of ritual bathing. This was a level of public sanitation unique in the early world.
4. **Weights and measures: an invisible unification.** A precise set of **stone weights**, made in a regular binary-plus-decimal ratio, was nearly identical across cities hundreds of kilometers apart. Brick sizes were highly uniform too. Such cross-city standardization shows a **powerful coordinating mechanism** behind it — but we do not know what that mechanism was.
5. **Seals and writing: written down, but no one can read it.** They carved animal images and rows of symbols onto thousands of square **seals**. But this **Indus script** has **never been deciphered**: there are roughly four hundred signs, each passage is very short, and no "bilingual" Rosetta-stone-style key has ever been found. So we cannot hear their own voice.
6. **Trade reached Mesopotamia (c. 2600-2000 BCE).** Indus carnelian beads, seals, and weights show up in Mesopotamia; Mesopotamian texts call this land **Meluhha**. Coastal sites like **Lothal** may have been ports/dockyards, and **Dholavira** is famous for its huge water reservoirs. This was an **outward-facing, big-trading** civilization.
7. **The list of "missing things" keeps getting longer.** After 100+ years of digging, still **no palace, no single dominant temple, no giant ruler-statue or royal tomb, and no large-scale war layer** have been found. Two famous small statues — the steatite "**Priest-King**" (which has zero evidence that he was a priest or a king — the name was given by later people) and the bronze "**Dancing Girl**" — are both tiny and personal, nothing like a "king's monument."
8. **The climate dries; cities are quietly abandoned (from c. 1900 BCE).** There was no overnight massacre or fall. It looks more like **slow de-urbanization**: the monsoon weakened, rain decreased, and the Ghaggar-Hakra ancient channel — one of the lifelines — gradually dried up, possibly with flood shifts and a shrinking trade network on top. People left the big cities in waves, migrated east, and scattered into smaller villages and farmsteads.
9. **The "Aryan invasion destroyed it" idea is overturned.** Early colonial-era scholars once guessed that an incoming "Aryan invasion" smashed this civilization. **Mainstream archaeology today largely rejects "violent invasion destruction":** more likely, climatic and systemic causes made the cities decline first, and any movement of new peoples happened **after** the cities had already declined. This point is especially politically sensitive in India (see §9).
10. **Forgotten for 4,000 years, rediscovered in the 1920s.** After its decline this civilization was completely forgotten — not even local Indian legend kept a memory of it. Then, in **1920-1922**, the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI) excavated Harappa under **Daya Ram Sahni** and Mohenjo-daro under **R. D. Banerji**; the director, **John Marshall**, announced to the world in **1924** that India's history had to be pushed back more than a thousand years — a brand-new civilization, far older than any known Indian one, had been found.

**An irony to keep:** this civilization led its era in engineering and city planning, yet because its **script was never read**, it became the early civilization we know the least about. It left us the most precise drains, but not one sentence we can read.

## 5. Reading the Documents (Primary Sources)

> Warning: this civilization has **no readable texts**. The "primary sources" below are **objects** (seals, weights, statues, buildings), not written records. This is exactly what the lesson wants students to feel: when a civilization cannot "speak," we can only let **objects** testify — and an object's testimony always has to be interpreted by us, which is easy to get wrong.

### A. A square seal (steatite, carved with an animal + Indus script)
> The object: large numbers of square soapstone seals, typically carved with one animal (often a "unicorn"-like bull) and a row of undeciphered signs.
> Plain reading: this is something they really did "write" — yet we cannot read a single sign of it.
> Many-sided reading: a merchant might have stamped it on a clay seal of goods to prove ownership (like a signature/trademark today); it might also have religious or identity meaning. **The key honesty**: because we cannot read it, any claim that "it spells the name of King So-and-so" is a guess, not a fact. This seal proves both "they had writing" and "we cannot hear them."

### B. A set of stone weights (standardized measures)
> The object: a precise set of cube-shaped stone weights, increasing in regular ratios, nearly identical across cities.
> Plain reading: in two cities hundreds of kilometers apart, "one unit" weighed almost exactly the same.
> Reading: this is **the strongest evidence in the whole civilization for "organized coordination."** Unified weights usually mean someone was managing trade, taxes, or fair exchange. But note — it proves "there was coordination," it does **not** prove "there was a king." Coordination could come from a king, a merchants' guild, a priesthood, or some collective arrangement. The weights can tell you "there were rules," not "who made the rules."

### C. The Great Bath of Mohenjo-daro
> The object: a large public pool about 12 meters long, carefully built with fired brick + gypsum mortar + a bitumen waterproof layer, set lower than the surrounding ground, with steps leading down.
> Plain reading: a large, beautifully waterproofed public bath/pool built in the center of the city.
> Reading: many scholars think it was used for **ritual bathing** (the importance of "purification" in later Indian culture is often used as an analogy — but this is a guess, not a confirmation). It sits next to nothing like a "god statue" or "altar" — so whether it was religious, civic, or something else, we are **not sure**. It is a snapshot of "a civilization that pushed public works to the limit, yet left its beliefs a mystery."

### D. The "Priest-King" statue — an object whose very name is a misunderstanding
> The object: a small steatite male statue from about 2000-1900 BCE, with half-closed eyes, a beard, and a robe with a trefoil (three-leaf) pattern.
> Plain reading: a tiny carved man's head, named the "Priest-King" by later people.
> **The key reading**: this name was given by the **1920s excavators based on their own imagination of "ancient rulers."** There is **no evidence** that this person was a priest, and **no evidence** that he was a king. This is a perfect teaching trap: we are so used to "ancient civilization = has a king" that the moment we dig up a dignified male figure, we shout "king." Take the name off and look again, and you will see: **on the question of whether this civilization had a king, this statue proves nothing.**

## 6. Consequences (Short / Medium / Long Term)

- **Short term (c. 1900-1300 BCE):** after the big cities were abandoned, the civilization did not "vanish into thin air" — it **de-urbanized and fragmented**: people migrated east and scattered into smaller villages and farmsteads, and the standardized urban way of life was gradually lost. Uniform weights, grid cities, and seal-writing all left the stage together.
- **Medium term (the substrate of ancient India):** some elements may have **seeped into later South Asian culture** (certain belief motifs, the importance of water and purification, cotton growing), but because the script cannot be read, this "inheritance" is mostly inference and cannot be traced in a clean line as in Egypt. Its exact relationship to the later Vedic culture, the Ganges civilization, and the Mauryan empire (another course on this platform) remains an open question.
- **Long term (two meanings for today):**
  - **For history as a discipline:** it is an extreme case of "a highly developed civilization can leave almost no readable writing, and therefore can barely speak for itself" — forcing modern people to face how much our knowledge of the past depends on writing, and how easily we misjudge what we cannot read.
  - **For the present:** in both India and Pakistan it is a source of national identity, and the arguments over "whose ancestor is it," "is the Ghaggar-Hakra the Saraswati river," and "were the Aryans invaders or migrants" directly touch today's politics (see §9). A silent civilization from 4,000 years ago is still being fought over for the right to interpret it.

## 7. AP DBQ Thinking Practice

- **How to argue from absence of evidence:** this is the highest-level skill in this topic. Can "we didn't find a palace" prove "there was no king"? — not directly, but **several absences stacked together** (no palace + no single dominant temple + no ruler-statue/royal tomb + no large war layer + relatively even wealth distribution) **together** make a weighty argument. AP does not want a 0/1 "yes/no" answer; it wants **"with limited evidence, how far can we reasonably reason, and where must we stop."**
- **Stakeholder / interpreter grid:** the same body of physical evidence yields different stories for different people — nationalist historians, foreign archaeologists, religious readings, materialist/environmental readings. Have students list: who is interpreting these seals and weights, and how does each one's standpoint shape the conclusion?
- **Continuity & change:** from the standardized big cities of 2600 BCE to the fragmented villages after 1900 BCE — **what changed** (cities, writing, unified weights disappear), and **what may have continued** (cotton, certain belief motifs, the importance of water)? Be careful to separate "confirmed continuity" from "guessed continuity."
- **Cross-civilization comparison (this topic's trump card):** line up Indus vs Mesopotamia vs Egypt vs contemporary China — all early civilizations, so why is the **Indus the only one with no visible center of kingly power**? This is a perfect DBQ prompt for "is a civilization bound to move toward centralized power," feeding directly into the China bridge in §8.
- **The honest boundary:** any essay that writes the Indus as a "certain utopia" or as "certainly having a hidden king" has **gone past the evidence.** AP graders reward arguments that **admit the uncertainty and explain why it is uncertain.**

## 8. China in the Same Era (Structural Comparison · Rule 0 veto dimension)

What was happening in China during the Indus Mature phase (c. 2600-1900 BCE)? Roughly, this corresponds to China's **late Longshan** culture into **Erlitou** culture (which many scholars link to the legendary Xia dynasty — though Xia's status as documented history is still debated) — that is, **the eve and early stage of China's kingly cities and palace architecture taking clear shape.**

Structural comparison (side by side, no ranking — this is the core of the §8 China bridge):

- **The "center" of the city looks different:** China's early large settlements (such as Taosi, and later Erlitou) show archaeology a **clear palace foundation, ranked burials (some graves richly furnished, some nearly empty), and power and ritual placed at the very center of the city** — a "who is on top" structure that is visible at a glance is forming. But **at the same time, Mohenjo-daro and Harappa, dug for a century, show neither a palace nor that kind of extreme ranked burial where "one person is buried with a pile of jades and another has nothing."** Two civilizations, at roughly the same time, with completely different "shapes of power" in their cities.
- **What does unification rest on:** China's early unity and coordination came, more and more clearly over time, to rest on **kingship + ritual order + a ranked hierarchy** (who may use how many bronze vessels, who is buried where, who sacrifices to Heaven). The Indus unity was **just as real** — bricks the same size, weights the same, cities from one mold — but its unity shows **no king sitting at the top.** Both achieved large-scale standardization; one civilization hangs it on a "king," and the other's "hook" we still cannot find.
- **The real question for students:** **does a civilization have to have a strong central power (a king / a palace / a dominant temple) to organize hundreds of thousands of people, unify weights and measures, and build city-wide drainage?** China's early history seems to answer "it moves toward yes"; the Indus seems to answer "at least, on the surface, it can be done without." But **both sides must keep one honesty:** whether China's Erlitou truly had the kind of king we imagine, and whether Xia is documented history, is still debated; and whether the Indus truly had no king, or had a kind of power we cannot read, is also still debated.

Rule 0: we cannot say "the Indus had no king so it was more advanced/freer," nor "China had kingship so it was more mature/more backward." This only sets side by side two **different solutions to "how to organize many people"** on earth in the same era. Both ran successfully for centuries, each with wisdom we can see and blind spots we cannot. This section does not rank; it compares.

## 9. What Historians Argue About (Historiography)

- **"Most egalitarian society" school vs "invisible elite" school:** recent scholars (such as critical work on the "Priest-King" concept) argue the Indus may be **the most egalitarian** of early complex societies — complexity does not require a ruling class. The other side warns: **"we found no elite" does not equal "there was no elite"** — power may have existed in forms we cannot read (religion, kinship, guilds). The heart of this argument is method: **can you use "we didn't dig it up" to argue "it didn't exist."**
- **The cause-of-decline debate:** the old "Aryan invasion destruction" idea has been largely overturned in the mainstream; the mainstream now is **a climatic/systemic collapse** (weakening monsoon, the drying of the Ghaggar-Hakra, floods, shrinking trade — multiple causes stacked). But the weight of each factor is still argued.
- **Ghaggar-Hakra = the Saraswati river? (highly politicized):** part of Indian scholarship (especially with nationalist coloring) argues that dried-up ancient channel is the sacred Saraswati of the Vedas, and uses it to connect the Indus civilization with Vedic culture in one "indigenous continuous" line, countering the idea that "the Aryans were outsiders." Critics see this as **pulling archaeological evidence toward a national narrative.** This argument directly touches present-day identity politics in both India and Pakistan — **a 4,000-year-old civilization is still being fought over for "whose ancestor it is."**
- **The decipherment debate:** is the Indus script even "writing" (recording a language), or some non-linguistic sign system? Different scholars, using statistics, AI, and other methods, disagree, with no settled answer.
- **A neutral synthesis:** for the Indus civilization, the most honest stance is **"we know very little, and many conclusions are shaped by modern politics."** It may have been a strikingly egalitarian society, or it may simply have hidden its inequality in forms we cannot read — until the script is deciphered, both possibilities have to stay on the table.

## 10. Common Misconceptions (Myth-Busting)

1. ❌ "The Indus civilization was definitely an egalitarian utopia." → ✅ It **looks** unusually equal (small gaps in housing/wealth, no palace, no ruler-statues), but "finding no elite" does **not** equal "there was no elite." Before the script is read, this is a **strong possibility**, not a settled fact.
2. ❌ "The 'Priest-King' statue proves they had priest-kings." → ✅ The name was given by 1920s excavators from imagination; there is **no evidence** that the figure was a priest or a king. The name itself is a misunderstanding.
3. ❌ "This civilization was destroyed by an Aryan invasion." → ✅ Mainstream archaeology has largely rejected "violent invasion destruction"; cities were more likely **gradually abandoned** because of a drying climate and drying rivers, with any peoples moving in mostly **after** the decline.
4. ❌ "It mysteriously vanished overnight." → ✅ It did not vanish overnight; it **slowly de-urbanized** — people migrated east and scattered into small villages, and the urban way of life was gradually lost. It "faded out," it did not "evaporate."
5. ❌ "Undeciphered script = they had no writing / were primitive." → ✅ The opposite: they **did** have writing (thousands of seals prove it); it is just that **we** cannot read it (few signs, short passages, no bilingual key). It is our limit, not their backwardness.
6. ❌ "No army or war remains = they were absolutely peaceful." → ✅ "Almost no large-scale war remains" is true and very unusual, but "little evidence" cannot directly equal "absolutely no war/no violence." This is another "absence of evidence" trap — proceed with care.
7. ❌ "It's just 'ancient India = India's.'" → ✅ Many core sites are in today's Pakistan; it is a source of identity for both India and Pakistan, and "whose it is" is itself part of a present-day argument.
8. ❌ "The Indus < contemporary China/Mesopotamia, because it had no king and no big empire." → ✅ No visible kingship does **not** equal backward — its city planning and sanitation engineering were top-tier for the era. "Advanced" has more than one shape.

## 11. Thinking Questions (Open-Ended, No Conclusion)

1. Other early civilizations have kings' palaces, ruler-statues, towering temples; the Indus, dug for 100 years, has none of these. Does this mean it "had no center of strong power," or only that its power took a **shape we cannot read today**? What is the gap between "we did not find it" and "it did not exist"?
2. This is the most engineering-advanced of the four early civilizations, yet the one we know the least about — only because its script has never been read. If a civilization cannot speak for itself, how much of the story we tell on its behalf is theirs, and how much is ours?
3. In the same era, China's cities were growing clear palaces and ranked burials, while the Indus cities show no king at the top — yet they still unified weights and built city-wide drainage. **To organize hundreds of thousands of people, do you need a strong central power?** Or is standardization itself another kind of "rule" we are not used to?
4. The name "Priest-King" was attached by later people from their assumption that "an ancient civilization should have a king." How much of our "common sense" about the ancient world is also stuffed in by our own expectations?
5. This time you mostly stood in the position of that ordinary child — living in a neat house, using advanced drains, yet unable to say whether his city even had a king. How would the "organizer" who kept every brick the same size tell the story of this city? How would the merchant trading to Mesopotamia see his homeland? Whose voice have you not heard yet?

## Appendix A: Key Timeline

> Warning: this civilization has no readable chronicle; all dates below are archaeologically estimated **phases**, not precise events recorded by ancient people, and the ranges have scholarly flexibility.

- From c. 7000 BCE: early farming villages such as Mehrgarh (the run-up before cities).
- c. 3300-2600 BCE: Early Harappan, the transition toward cities.
- **c. 2600 BCE: the Mature Harappan phase begins** — Harappa, Mohenjo-daro and other big cities peak together (the focus point of this lesson).
- c. 2334-2284 BCE: Sargon of Akkad reigns in Mesopotamia; his inscription mentions ships of Meluhha at the Akkad docks (side-evidence of Indus-Mesopotamia trade).
- c. 2600-2000 BCE: Indus carnelian beads and seals appear in tombs at Ur, Susa, etc. in Mesopotamia (hard trade evidence).
- **c. 1900 BCE: the Mature phase ends, gradual de-urbanization** — cities abandoned, people migrating east (decline begins).
- c. 2000-1900 BCE: the approximate date of the "Priest-King" statue (Mohenjo-daro's late period).
- c. 1300 BCE: the Indus civilization phase roughly ends (the fragmented tail-end).
- 1920-1922 CE: the ASI excavates Harappa under Daya Ram Sahni and Mohenjo-daro under R. D. Banerji.
- 1924 CE: John Marshall announces the discovery of this brand-new ancient civilization to the world.

## Appendix B: Core Vocabulary

- **Indus River** — the civilization's lifeline (core area in today's Pakistan / NW India)
- **Harappan Civilization** — named after Harappa, the first site excavated (= the Indus Valley Civilization)
- **Mature Harappan** — the mature phase (c. 2600-1900 BCE, the golden age of the big cities)
- **Harappa / Mohenjo-daro** — the two most famous cities
- **citadel / lower town** — raised ground (often public/management buildings) / the residential district
- **grid plan** — grid layout (streets crossing straight like a chessboard)
- **Great Bath** — Mohenjo-daro's large waterproofed public pool (purpose uncertain)
- **drainage system** — the covered sewer system reaching every house (top-tier in the early world)
- **standardized weights** — precise, cross-city-uniform weights (the strongest evidence of unified coordination)
- **Indus script** — the Indus writing on seals (~400 signs, **still undeciphered**)
- **seal** — square soapstone seal, carved with an animal + writing (possibly used as a trade mark)
- **Priest-King** — the steatite "Priest-King" statue (the name is later imagination; no evidence he was a priest or king)
- **Dancing Girl** — the small bronze "Dancing Girl" statue (lost-wax cast, about 10.5 cm)
- **carnelian** — a red-orange stone; etched carnelian beads are the Indus's signature export
- **Meluhha** — Mesopotamia's name for the Indus Valley region
- **Ghaggar-Hakra** — the ancient river channel dense with sites (key to decline and the "Saraswati dispute")
