# The Early Republic · 1800-1828

> **Lock status**: narrative kernel v0 — pending review
>
> **AP anchor**: APUSH Period 4 (1800-1848) · CA HSS-8.4 / 8.5
> **Through-line**: how a young, indebted republic stood up, doubled its size, and wove its first national market — and who paid for it
> **Difficulty**: 3.5/5 (many moving parts: land, war, economy, politics)
> **Expected duration**: 35-45 min

---

## 0. Open Your Textbook First (Textbook Anchor)

This lesson fills a gap your textbook often skips in one sentence: the whole generation between **Thomas Jefferson taking office in 1801** and **Andrew Jackson being elected in 1828.**

> This belongs to **HMH United States History (Grade 8)**, the **Jefferson Era** chapter (Louisiana Purchase, Lewis and Clark, War of 1812) and the **A New National Identity** chapter (American System, Era of Good Feelings, Missouri Compromise, Monroe Doctrine). It also maps onto **APUSH Period 4 (1800-1848)** and **CA HSS-8.4 / 8.5.**
>
> Your textbook gives you the skeleton: which year a piece of land was bought, which year a war was fought. This lesson hangs three things on that skeleton that textbooks rarely make clear:
> - **"Westward expansion" was standing on top of someone.** The Louisiana Purchase doubled the nation's size overnight — but the land was not empty. Dozens of Native nations lived there. The other side of the nation "standing up" was those nations being pushed off.
> - **The "market revolution" was not factories falling from the sky.** Canals, factories, and westward markets were three threads of one net. It pulled farmers, factory girls, the enslaved, and immigrants all at once into a brand-new economy that measured everything in money — some got rich, some were locked into new kinds of labor.
> - **The "Era of Good Feelings" was not as good as it sounds.** The first party system (Federalists vs. Democratic-Republicans) was falling apart. On the surface, everyone got along; underneath, the Missouri Compromise (1820) had already split the country in two along one line of latitude — slave vs. free. Jefferson heard that line and said it woke him "like a fire bell in the night."
>
> **The blind spot for Chinese parents**: Chinese textbooks cover this era in roughly one phrase — "territorial expansion." They do not teach the Native cost, federalism, or how the market revolution reshaped society. **federalism, the Missouri Compromise, the Monroe Doctrine** — all verbatim AP points — are things most parents cannot tutor. That is exactly where this Topic lives.

## 1. One-Sentence Anchor (Many Sides at Once)

In the spring of **1803**, in a room in Paris, two Americans came only to buy one port city, **New Orleans**. Instead the French said: take the whole thing — **828,000 square miles** for **$15 million.** The Americans were stunned. They signed. One deal, and the young nation's size doubled overnight.

But the word "doubled" pressed down on top of several groups who wanted opposite things:

- **The men in power (Jefferson and his successors):** they needed this young, indebted republic — caught between the two empires of Britain and France — to **survive, stand up, and grow.** Buying land, fighting wars, digging canals, setting foreign-policy rules — all served that one goal.
- **White farmers and speculators rushing west:** they saw "the West" as a blank check that could change their lives — cheap land, a fresh start.
- **Factory owners and canal-builders in the Northeast:** they saw a different future — connect the whole country by water and machine into one market, so cotton, flour, and cloth could move fast.
- **The Native nations being pushed off (Shawnee, Cherokee, and dozens more):** to them, the "blank West" in white people's mouths was their ancestral home. Every inch the nation "grew," they were squeezed an inch.
- **The enslaved carried west:** the market revolution made Southern cotton hugely profitable, so slavery did not fade — it **spread west with the cotton.** More people were sold deeper inland.

In the same era, on the other side of the earth, China's Qing dynasty was at the tail end of its "High Qing" peak. It had the longest artificial waterway in the world — the **Grand Canal** (大运河), over a thousand kilometers, several times longer than the **Erie Canal** the Americans were still digging. Both civilizations used canals to move wealth. But what those canals connected at each end was quietly splitting apart — that is the story of §8.

## 2. The Stage (Geography / Climate / Society / Institutions / Events)

- **Geography**: In 1800 the United States hugged the Atlantic in its thirteen eastern states, with a western edge roughly at the Mississippi River. The 1803 Louisiana Purchase shoved that edge to the foot of the Rocky Mountains — from the Mississippi to the Rockies, from the Gulf of Mexico to today's Canadian border. The size **doubled.** This new land was laced with rivers (the Missouri, the Mississippi), but white Americans knew almost nothing about it — which is why **Lewis and Clark** were sent to explore it.
- **Climate**: The Northeast is temperate, with steep, fast rivers — strong **water power.** That is why factories were built beside New England rivers (the Lowell mills sat by the falls of the Merrimack River). The Midwest is plains and forest, good for growing grain to feed the westward flow of settlers. The South is hot and wet, good for cotton — and the cotton gin turned cotton into a money-printing machine, welding slavery onto that climate.
- **Society**: In 1800 the nation held about 5.3 million people; by 1828 it was nearing 12 million — more than doubling in a generation. People were "running": running west (for land), running to the factories (for wages). The Lowell mills were the first to hire **young, single women aged 15 to 35** in large numbers (the "mill girls") — the first time American women left the farm for wages in groups. Meanwhile, the number of enslaved people in the South soared with cotton, and they were sold west into the new cotton fields in waves.
- **Institutions**: The U.S. Constitution (written in Philadelphia, 1787) bundled the country into a **federal system** — power split between the federal (national) government and the states. The big question for this generation: how far can the federal government reach? Did buying land (the Louisiana Purchase) go beyond what the Constitution allows? Did building canals or running a national bank? How can "free states" and "slave states" live inside one union? There were no ready answers — these 28 years tested them out, bit by bit.
- **Events**: 1803 Louisiana Purchase → 1804-06 Lewis and Clark → 1811 Battle of Tippecanoe → 1812-15 War of 1812 → 1819 Panic of 1819 (the nation's first nationwide economic crisis) → 1820 Missouri Compromise → 1823 Monroe Doctrine → 1825 Erie Canal opens → the "corrupt" election of 1824 → 1828 Andrew Jackson elected, the first party system fully reshuffled.

## 3. The Main Characters

> Three perspectives (actor, lonely mediator, receiving-end), including at least one powerless party. The DEFAULT perspective is the Native nation being pushed off + the enslaved carried west — a prior-challenging lens that centers the powerless.

### Thomas Jefferson and the men of "westward" (actor) — 1743-1826 · the man who wrote "all men are created equal," pushed the nation west, and enslaved people all his life

A real historical person. He became the third president in 1801. He was not a simple man — precisely because he was several things at once. He wrote "all men are created equal" in the Declaration of Independence. He dreamed of a republic of independent small farmers (no factories, no city poor, no big banks). And on his Monticello plantation, he **enslaved more than six hundred people across his life.**

When the Louisiana Purchase landed in his hands in 1803, he fell into a trap he had built himself. He had always argued that "if the Constitution does not say government can do it, it cannot" (**strict construction** — reading the Constitution narrowly). Yet the Constitution said nothing about a president buying a whole continent. Buy, or not? He chose to buy — he later said he swallowed his constitutional doubts for the good of the country. That choice bent his own principle, but it gave the nation half a continent.

He sent **Lewis and Clark** to measure this new land. In his eyes, the West was the republic's "empire of liberty" — room for generation after generation of small farmers, a place for democracy to grow. But the other side of "room for small farmers" he did not say out loud: people already lived on that land. This perspective puts you in a powerful man's seat, watching him make choice after choice to keep the nation alive and growing — and watching whom those choices cost.

### Tecumseh (lonely mediator) — c.1768-1813 · the Shawnee leader who tried to weave dozens of Native nations into one rope against the white advance, and died in battle

A real historical person — leader, warrior, and orator of the **Shawnee** nation. He saw something others did not: white people bought, tricked, and seized land one nation at a time, and **as long as Native nations each fought alone, they would be eaten one by one.** So he did something extremely hard. He traveled across a vast region from the Great Lakes to the South, trying to persuade dozens of Native nations — some with old grudges against each other — to **unite** into a cross-nation **confederacy** holding one shared line: the land belongs to all Native peoples in common, and **no single nation has the right to sell it** to whites.

His brother **Tenskwatawa** (called "the Prophet") called people, from a spiritual side, to return to the ancestors' ways and refuse white liquor and goods. The two brothers were one in war, one in spirit. In **1811**, while Tecumseh was away in the South still recruiting allies, the Indiana territorial governor **William Henry Harrison** marched on the confederacy's central village and fought the **Battle of Tippecanoe (November 7, 1811).** The confederacy was badly hurt. After the War of 1812 broke out, Tecumseh allied with Britain and fought on; on **October 5, 1813**, he was killed at the **Battle of the Thames** (in today's Ontario, Canada). With his death, the confederacy fell apart.

He is the true "lonely mediator" of this era. He was not trying to win one battle — he was trying to build a future where all Native nations could survive, a future that needed old feuds set aside and the common cause put before the tribe. That future kept slipping through his fingers even while he lived. This perspective shows you that the side being pushed off was not passively waiting to die — among them were the clearest strategists of the age, who could still lose when the great tide pressed in.

### The Native mother being pushed off + the enslaved boy carried west (receiving-end · DEFAULT)

> Two powerless voices, combined into one DEFAULT perspective. Both are bridging characters, **fully real in role, with no exact name** — because history rarely left names for these people, and that itself is part of the problem.

**The Native mother being pushed off**: she lives in a village of a Native nation in the Southeast or Great Lakes — perhaps **Cherokee**, with fields of corn, river valleys, and wooden houses, living a settled, farming life (this matters: they were **not** the "tent-dwelling wanderers" of white imagination). Every day she watches the white settlements spill east-to-west like water: first a few families, then a town, then the state government passing a law saying this land is now under the state. With every new treaty, the ground under her feet shrinks one ring. She has heard Tecumseh's name and knows someone wants to unite everyone; she has also heard people say "better to negotiate early and trade for land farther off." She cannot decide the great tide — she can only watch the word "home" grow smaller year by year. Her perspective makes you face the side of "westward expansion" that gets skipped: every piece of land "newly gained" was another people's home "being lost."

**The enslaved boy carried west**: he was born on a Virginia tobacco plantation, treated from birth as property on a ledger. After the market revolution turned Southern cotton into huge profit, he is sold to a slave trader, put in chains, and walked on the long road southwest (to the new cotton fields of Alabama and Mississippi) — the road the enslaved themselves called being **"sold down the river,"** meaning harsher labor and permanent separation from family. He cannot read; no one asked his name. His perspective breaks a common myth: the market revolution and westward expansion did **not** make slavery slowly fade — they gave it a new, bigger appetite. The more the nation "grew" westward, the farther people like him were sold.

These two powerless voices are the heart of this Topic. AP rewards, again and again, whether you can **see the silent majority.** "Westward expansion," "market prosperity" — words that sound like all good news in the textbook — have, standing behind them, exactly this mother and this boy.

## 4. The Story (Plot Beats)

1. **One port, half a continent bought (1803).** Jefferson meant to spend at most $10 million for New Orleans (controlling the mouth of the Mississippi was life-or-death for western farmers). But Napoleon — just defeated in Saint-Domingue (Haiti) and badly needing money to fight Britain — sold the whole of Louisiana, 828,000 square miles, for **$15 million.** Signed in 1803; the Senate approved it in October. The nation's size doubled overnight.
2. **Go see what was bought (1804-1806).** Jefferson sent **Meriwether Lewis** and **William Clark** leading the "Corps of Discovery" out from near St. Louis (May 14, 1804), up the Missouri River, over the Rockies, to the Pacific, and back (returning to St. Louis in September 1806). They got through only with the help of **Sacagawea** (a Shoshone woman) as guide and interpreter. The expedition drew the nation's first map of the West — and paved the way for white settlers.
3. **The second war of independence (1812-1815).** Britain seized American ships at sea and forced American sailors into its navy (**impressment**), and was suspected of backing Native resistance. On **June 18, 1812**, the U.S. declared war on Britain. The war went roughly: in August 1814 British troops burned Washington, even the White House. But it also forged a new national pride.
4. **The victory won after the peace was signed (1814-1815).** On **December 24, 1814**, the two sides signed a peace at **Ghent** (in Belgium), essentially restoring the prewar status quo — neither side gained much. But before the news could cross the Atlantic, Andrew Jackson had already crushed the British at the **Battle of New Orleans (January 8, 1815).** This "victory after the peace" made Jackson a national hero and fed the feeling that "America won."
5. **The peak and collapse of Native resistance (1811-1813).** Tecumseh's cross-nation confederacy was badly hurt at Tippecanoe (1811); allied with Britain in the War of 1812, he died at the Battle of the Thames (1813). Native peoples lost their strongest resistance — setting up the later mass forced removal of Southeastern nations.
6. **A net spreads out: the market revolution (1810s-1820s).** Three threads pulled tight at once: **transportation** (canals, toll roads, later steamboats); **factories** (textile mills on New England rivers, Lowell hiring mill girls at scale); **market expansion** (the West grows grain, the South grows cotton, the Northeast makes goods — the three linked into one national market by canal and river). In 1825 the **Erie Canal** (363 miles) opened, joining New York's harbor to the Great Lakes — freight cost from the West to the East Coast crashed from about **$100 a ton to about $6 a ton.** New York became the nation's number-one commercial city.
7. **The first nationwide economic crisis (1819).** The Panic of 1819: postwar boom, land speculation, and the Second Bank of the United States — first loosening then sharply tightening credit — together triggered America's first nationwide depression. Land prices crashed, banks failed, cities saw mass unemployment. The market revolution showed the country for the first time: being linked on one net means when one part breaks, every part hurts.
8. **One line of latitude splits the country (1820).** **Missouri** asked to join the union as a slave state, breaking the balance of free and slave states. After a fierce standoff, **Henry Clay** brokered the **Missouri Compromise**: Missouri enters as a slave state and **Maine** as a free state together (keeping the balance), and slavery is banned forever in the Louisiana Purchase lands **north of latitude 36°30′.** President Monroe signed it in March 1820. Hearing of that line, Jefferson wrote his famous words — it woke him "like a fire bell in the night" and filled him with terror. He had seen the shadow of civil war.
9. **The "Era of Good Feelings" and the collapse of the first party system (1817-1825).** After the War of 1812, the Federalist Party's standing collapsed and nearly vanished, and the nation entered the so-called **Era of Good Feelings**: in 1820 Monroe was reelected almost unopposed. On the surface, one-party calm — underneath, the Missouri fight and regional interests churned, the old parties dissolving and the new ones not yet formed.
10. **Drawing a line across the hemisphere (1823).** Seeing that European powers might try to step into the newly independent nations of Latin America, Monroe announced the **Monroe Doctrine** in his December 2, 1823, message to Congress: Europe is no longer to treat the Americas as targets for colonization; the Americas mind their own affairs; the Old and New Worlds do not interfere with each other. The real designer was Secretary of State **John Quincy Adams.** America had no power yet to back the words — but they set the tone of U.S. foreign policy for more than a century.
11. **A "corrupt" election (1824-1825).** The 1824 election was a four-man scramble. Andrew Jackson won the most popular and electoral votes but **no majority.** Under the 12th Amendment, the House of Representatives chose from the top three — and House Speaker **Henry Clay** threw his support to **John Quincy Adams**, who, once elected, named Clay his Secretary of State. Jackson's camp denounced it as a **"corrupt bargain."** It tore the fake calm off the Era of Good Feelings.
12. **A new politics is born (1828).** Holding his grudge, Jackson came back in 1828 and won the presidency. The old first party system was fully reshuffled, and a new party politics (the Democratic Party around Jackson) took the stage. This generation's story of "standing up and growing" handed the baton to the next generation's story — the "Age of Jackson."

**An irony to keep:** Jefferson, who argued all his life that "government should not do what the Constitution did not authorize," did the single biggest thing the Constitution never wrote — buy half a continent. He wrote "all men are created equal," yet enslaved people all his life and bought the very West where slavery would spread. The New Orleans victory that made Jackson a legend was actually fought after the peace was already signed — an "unnecessary victory" that made a president.

## 5. Reading the Documents (Primary Sources)

### A. The price of the Louisiana Purchase treaty (1803)
> Fact: about 828,000 square miles, for $15 million (including direct payment and the assumption of French debts owed to Americans). That works out to a few cents an acre.
> Plain reading: with the budget for buying one city, America bought half a continent — a few cents an acre on average.
> Many-sided reading: to the U.S., "the best bargain in history"; to the dozens of Native nations living on that land, "my home was priced like merchandise and signed away by two nations across an ocean, in a room in Paris." Of the two parties to the deal, neither was a Native person of that land.

### B. Jefferson on the Missouri line (1820, private letter)
> Original (common version): "this momentous question, like a fire bell in the night, awakened and filled me with terror."
> Plain reading: "this huge question, like a fire bell ringing in the night, woke me up and filled me with fear."
> Reading: the man who wrote the Declaration of Independence, hearing of the 36°30′ Missouri line in 1820, did not cheer — he feared. He saw that once the nation began splitting along a geographic line into "slave vs. free," that crack could never close again. The Civil War, forty years later, proved him right.

### C. The Monroe Doctrine (1823 message to Congress, excerpt)
> Original (excerpt): "The American continents … are henceforth not to be considered as subjects for future colonization by any European powers."
> Plain reading: "The American continents … from now on are not to be treated as targets for colonization by any European power."
> Reading: the words are bold, but the America of 1823 had no navy to enforce them — what actually made them work was that Britain's navy, too, did not want others stepping into the Americas. It was more a "statement of intent," yet it set the long-term posture of the U.S. treating the whole Western Hemisphere as its backyard. The designer, John Quincy Adams, later became president.

### D. The Erie Canal freight numbers (around 1825)
> Fact: after the Erie Canal opened, freight cost from the West to the East Coast crashed from about $100 a ton to about $6 a ton, and travel time fell sharply too.
> Plain reading: for the same ton of goods, the cost dropped from $100 to $6 — more than ten times cheaper.
> Reading: one number explains why the "market revolution" was a revolution. When freight collapses, the West's grain can be sold cheaply to the East and Europe for the first time, and the East's factory goods can reach the West cheaply for the first time. A "big ditch" (people mockingly called it "Clinton's big ditch") sewed scattered local economies into one national market.

## 6. Consequences (Short / Medium / Long Term)

- **Short term (1800s-1820s):** the territory doubled, the West was mapped, the "second war of independence" was fought and independence held, and a first national market net was woven with canals and factories. The young nation went from a question mark — "can it even survive?" — to a country that had stood up and begun swelling westward.
- **Medium term (first half of the 19th century):** the market revolution reshaped society — cities grew, wage labor appeared, women entered factories in groups for the first time, and regional specialization took form (North in industry and trade, West in grain, South in cotton). But the same process pushed slavery west with the cotton and pushed Native peoples out of the East. The 36°30′ Missouri line became the starting point for forty years of fights over slavery's expansion, finally igniting the Civil War in 1861.
- **Long term (to today):** these 28 years laid down several tracks that run to the present — the **flexibility of federal power** (buying land, building roads, running banks: government can do more than the Constitution's literal words), the foreign-policy tradition the **Monroe Doctrine** opened ("the Americas are the U.S. backyard"), and a **market economy** that put America on the rails toward the eve of industrialization, from an agricultural republic. The light (growth, opportunity, connection) and the cost (Native dispossession, slavery's westward spread, the cracks of wealth and region) share one body.

## 7. AP DBQ Thinking Practice

- **Stakeholder grid:** Jefferson's government / white farmers heading west / Northeastern factory owners and canal-builders / the Native nations being pushed off / the enslaved carried west / Southern planters / Britain / France — between 1800 and 1828, what did each want, and what cards did each hold? Whose interests briefly overlapped (e.g., western farmers needing the New Orleans outlet ⊥ Jefferson wanting to buy the land)?
- **Continuity & change:** from 1800 to 1828, what changed completely (territory doubled, a national market formed, the party system reshuffled, wage labor appeared), and what stayed the same or got worse (slavery did not fade but spread west, Native peoples' situation kept worsening, "who counts as a full citizen" still excluded women, the enslaved, and Native peoples)?
- **The fight over federal power (key tested angle):** the Louisiana Purchase, the Second Bank of the United States, Henry Clay's American System (federal money for roads and canals) — all ask the same question: can the federal government do what the Constitution did not spell out? This is the verbatim AP point of "strict vs. loose construction." The irony: Jefferson, the champion of strict construction, did the most loose-construction thing of all — buy the land.
- **The two-sided ledger of "westward expansion" (DBQ core argument):** with the same evidence, you can write "the growth of an empire of liberty" OR "expansion at the cost of Native dispossession and slavery's westward spread." What AP graders reward is the argument that **holds both sides at once and does not flatten either.** Evidence: the Louisiana Purchase, Lewis and Clark, Tippecanoe, the Missouri Compromise, the enslaved sold down the river.
- **Compare with the Missouri Compromise itself:** the 36°30′ line of 1820 was a compromise that "froze the disagreement along a geographic line." It bought forty years of peace and turned "where can slavery spread?" into the center of national politics. Did the compromise solve the conflict, or just lengthen the fuse?

## 8. China in the Same Era (Structural Comparison · Rule 0 veto dimension)

In the 1820s, China's Qing dynasty was at the tail end of its "High Qing" peak, at the turn from the Jiaqing to the Daoguang reigns. It was the most populous empire in the world (already past 300 million, heading toward 400 million), with highly developed agriculture, handicrafts, and domestic trade. This section ranks nothing; it simply sets two "wealth-by-canal" economies of the same era side by side.

**A perfect point of comparison: two canals.**

- When Americans cheered the **Erie Canal (363 miles)**, opened in 1825, and called it a wonder, it was in fact only "the second-longest canal in the world" at the time — **the longest was China's Grand Canal** (the Beijing-Hangzhou Grand Canal, over a thousand kilometers, the longest artificial waterway on earth, already connected back in the Sui and Tang dynasties). Both civilizations understood: connect the waterways, and you can move wealth fast and sew scattered places into one whole. On this, China led by more than a thousand years.
- **But what the two canals connected at each end was splitting apart.** The Erie Canal connected, at one end, the western farms of the Great Lakes, and at the other, the roaring **machine factories** beside New England's rivers — it carried not only grain but the seeds of a "market revolution" that pulled labor, machines, and markets together. China's Grand Canal was just as busy, connecting at one end the rice and **handicraft workshops** of the lower Yangtze (Suzhou and Songjiang's silk and cotton were among the world's finest hand-woven textiles) and at the other the northern capital and the grain-tribute system. The lower-Yangtze handicraft industry was extremely developed and exquisite, but it was organized as **workshops plus household labor**, not as the New England kind of **factory** — gathered into one building, driven by water-powered machines, hiring wage workers at scale.
- **Same technology, different roads.** When historians study "the Great Divergence," they find that around 1820 China (especially the lower Yangtze) and Western Europe were originally quite close in markets, population, and accumulation, and both sides had many of the key technologies. What truly split the two roads was not "who was smart and who was dumb," but their different situations (the relative prices of labor and resources, how markets were integrated, where coal and iron were distributed). These differences sent one side toward the factory-and-machine eve of the Industrial Revolution, and the other toward continued intensive cultivation within a highly developed handicraft economy.

**Rule 0**: we cannot say America was "advanced" and the Qing "backward," nor the reverse. The China of the 1820s was a huge, highly mature economy with a canal far longer than America's and handicrafts far finer than America's; America was a young country just beginning to reorganize itself with machines and canals. Setting the two canals side by side, what we see is not a ranking, but **two civilizations standing at the same fork on the eve of industrialization, each walking toward a different direction.** Which road led where would take decades more to reveal — and that is what makes history most fascinating: at the time, not one of them knew the answer.

## 9. What Historians Argue About (Historiography)

- **The "market revolution" school:** Charles Sellers's 1991 *The Market Revolution* defined the core of this era as "how the market economy reshaped ordinary Americans' lives," stressing that it brought both opportunity and new kinds of unfreedom (wage slavery, vulnerability to market swings). This is the main frame APUSH uses for the market revolution.
- **The anti-Whig / Native-agency reading:** rejects the triumphal "freedom marching forward" view of history and writes Native peoples (especially Tecumseh's cross-nation confederacy) as historical subjects with strategy and agency, not as passively vanishing "obstacles." This reading redefines "westward expansion" as "conquest of Native peoples."
- **The slavery's-expansion reading:** recent scholarship (such as Edward Baptist's) stresses that the market revolution and slavery were not opposites but **fed each other** — cotton's profits and the internal slave trade of "selling down the river" were part of the market revolution itself.
- **The political-history reading:** focuses on the collapse and reshuffling of the first party system, treating the "Era of Good Feelings" as a transition in which the old parties dissolved and new ones were born, not a real "peace without parties."
- **A neutral synthesis:** the Early Republic was both an era of a young nation growing, connecting, and widening opportunity, AND an era of Native dispossession, slavery's westward spread, and deepening regional cracks — both true at once; do not make it either/or.

## 10. Common Misconceptions (Myth-Busting)

1. ❌ "The Louisiana Purchase bought empty land." → ✅ Dozens of Native nations lived there; it was their ancestral home. The "purchase" was only a deal between the U.S. and France — Native peoples were never asked.
2. ❌ "Lewis and Clark were lone heroes who got through on their own." → ✅ Without the Shoshone woman Sacagawea as guide and interpreter, and the help of many Native nations along the way, the expedition could not have gotten through.
3. ❌ "America crushed Britain in the War of 1812." → ✅ The peace (Ghent, 1814) basically restored the prewar status quo — neither side gained much. The New Orleans victory people remember was fought after the peace was signed.
4. ❌ "The market revolution made slavery slowly disappear." → ✅ The opposite. Cotton's profits spread slavery west with the cotton, and more people were "sold down the river." The market revolution and slavery fed each other.
5. ❌ "The Era of Good Feelings was truly peaceful, with no party conflict." → ✅ On the surface one party dominated; underneath, the Missouri fight had already split the country in two along a geographic line; old parties were dissolving and new ones being born — far from a real "no-conflict" era.
6. ❌ "The Missouri Compromise solved the slavery problem." → ✅ It only froze the conflict along the 36°30′ line and bought forty years of peace, while making "where can slavery spread?" the center of national politics — finally igniting the Civil War. Jefferson sensed it at the time.
7. ❌ "The moment the Monroe Doctrine came out, America became master of the Americas." → ✅ The America of 1823 had no power to enforce it; what made it work was the tacit help of Britain's navy at the time. It was more a long-term statement of intent.
8. ❌ "Jefferson favored small government, so he would not expand federal power." → ✅ The champion of "strict construction" did the single biggest thing the Constitution never authorized — buy Louisiana. Principle and reality collided right in him.

## 11. Thinking Questions (Open-Ended, No Conclusion)

1. Jefferson argued all his life that "if the Constitution does not say government can do it, it cannot," yet did the single biggest thing the Constitution never wrote — buy half a continent. If it were you, between "holding to your principle" and "seizing a once-in-a-century chance for your country," how would you choose? After a principle is bent by reality once, is it still a principle?
2. "Westward expansion" is almost all good words in the textbook: growth, opportunity, empire of liberty. But the same land "newly gained" was another people's home "being lost." When a nation's growth must stand on another people's loss, how should that "growth" be told to be honest?
3. Tecumseh saw clearly that "if we don't unite, we'll be eaten one by one," and spent his whole life trying to get dozens of nations with old grudges to set aside their feuds and become one community first. He failed. Was it because the idea was wrong, or because there was not enough time and the tide was too strong? Is the person who "sees clearly too early" doomed to be alone?
4. The market revolution crashed freight costs and multiplied opportunity — and also put women into factories, sold the enslaved farther away, and exposed everyone to the economic crisis of 1819. "Being linked on one net" is both a source of prosperity and a source of fragility. Would you trade "each going their own way, slower, steadier" for "linked on one net, faster, but one break hurts everywhere"?
5. In the 1820s, China had a canal several times longer than the Erie Canal — connected over a thousand years earlier — and handicrafts far finer than America's. Yet decades later the two roads split. Setting the two canals side by side, do you see "who is strong and who is weak," or "the same fork, two civilizations each walking toward a direction no one could then guess"?
6. This time you mostly heard the voices of the Native mother being pushed off and the boy sold down the river. How would Jefferson tell this "growth"? How would the white farmer heading west tell it? Whose voice have you not heard yet?

## Appendix A: Key Timeline

- 1801: Thomas Jefferson takes office as the third president.
- 1803: Louisiana Purchase (about 828,000 sq mi, $15 million); Senate approves in October.
- 1804.5.14: Lewis and Clark's Corps of Discovery sets out from near St. Louis.
- 1806.9: Lewis and Clark return to St. Louis.
- 1811.11.7: Battle of Tippecanoe (Harrison vs. Tecumseh's confederacy).
- 1812.6.18: U.S. declares war on Britain; the War of 1812 begins.
- 1813.10.5: Battle of the Thames; Tecumseh killed, the confederacy collapses.
- 1814.8.24-25: British troops burn Washington (including the White House and Capitol).
- 1814.12.24: Treaty of Ghent signed, essentially restoring the prewar status quo.
- 1815.1.8: Battle of New Orleans; Jackson's great victory (after the peace was signed).
- 1816: Second Bank of the United States chartered.
- 1817-1825: Era of Good Feelings; Monroe is president.
- 1819: Panic of 1819, America's first nationwide depression.
- 1820.3: Missouri Compromise (Missouri slave + Maine free + 36°30′ line), signed by Monroe.
- 1823.12.2: Monroe Doctrine announced in the message to Congress (designed by John Quincy Adams).
- 1824: Four-man presidential election, no majority, sent to the House.
- 1825.2: The House elects John Quincy Adams; Clay named Secretary of State, sparking the "corrupt bargain" fight.
- 1825.10.26: The Erie Canal (363 miles) officially opens.
- 1828: Andrew Jackson elected president; the first party system fully reshuffled.
