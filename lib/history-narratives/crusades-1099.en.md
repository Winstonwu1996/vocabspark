# The Crusades · 1099

## 0. Open Your Textbook First (Textbook Anchor)

**Houghton Mifflin Medieval & Early Modern Times · Ch. 10 Lesson 4 The Crusades + Ch. 11 Lesson 1 Muslim Civilization · p.275**

Your textbook will tell you:
- In **1095**, **Pope Urban II** preached at **Clermont** and shouted **"Deus vult"** ("God wills it"), calling for a march east
- The **First Crusade** (1096-1099) captured Jerusalem
- Several more Crusades followed; in **1187 Saladin took Jerusalem back**
- Cultural exchange: **Arabic numerals, algebra, the works of Aristotle** were preserved by the Islamic world and reached Europe through Crusader contact

**What this narrative adds on top of your textbook:**
- **Three-way perspective**: Latin Christendom (Western Europe) / Byzantine Orthodoxy / the Islamic world. The textbook leans Latin. We force the three voices into the same room.
- The full 200-year arc from **1071 to 1291** — your textbook covers a few episodes, not the structural causes
- A **Northern Song China parallel** — why did China never produce a religious war? It's a structural answer, not a cultural one
- The **1099 Jerusalem massacre**: death-toll estimates from 3,000 to 70,000 — historians don't agree, and the disagreement itself teaches something
- **Four schools of historiography** (medieval Christian / Enlightenment critique like Gibbon / Riley-Smith's modern motivation studies / contemporary de-mythologizing)
- **The trap of modern political projection** — why this Topic is harder to keep neutral than Magna Carta
- **Seven common misconceptions** ("noble Saladin vs. evil Crusaders" was invented by Walter Scott in 1825)

The textbook is the skeleton. This is the meat. Read both — that's how you get a complete picture.

## 1. The One-Line Hook

In 1095 a 53-year-old Frenchman shouted from a wooden platform — and within four years, **100,000 Western Europeans had walked 4,000 km** to seize a city. They got there. The cost: **60,000 of their own dead on the road, plus a city massacred**. Two hundred years later, they were chased back out.

— That is the Crusader's view.

From a **Muslim resident of Jerusalem**, the same story sounds different: strangers came over the wall, and in three days your entire family was dead.

From a **Byzantine emperor in Constantinople**, it sounds different again: I asked for a few thousand professional soldiers. Instead I got 100,000 armed pilgrims who looted my villages on the way through.

**One year, three stories.** The 200-year war left no permanent territory. What it left was a wound across three civilizations that has not closed today.

## 2. The Stage: The Eastern Mediterranean in 1099

### Geography (L0)

**The Levant** is a narrow strip of land along the eastern Mediterranean — north end at southern Turkey today, south end at the Egyptian border, the Arabian desert to the east, the sea to the west. **Five modern countries crowd that strip today**: Israel, Palestine, Jordan, Lebanon, Syria. You can imagine how much conflict 900 years ago this corridor was already absorbing.

**Jerusalem** sits in the **Judean Hills** at 750 meters above sea level — a small, dry, hill-country town. In 1099 about **40,000 people** lived there. It had no strategic resources — not a port, not a trade junction. What it had was:
- **Three religions converging on one place**: Judaism (the Western Wall, ruins of the Second Temple); Christianity (the Holy Sepulchre, where Jesus was said to have been buried); Islam (the Al-Aqsa Mosque, where Muhammad was said to have ascended on his Night Journey)
- **Pure symbolic value**: capturing Jerusalem made nobody rich, but it could make 100 million Europeans believe God was on their side

**Paris to Jerusalem**: more than 4,000 km by land. The 1099 route ran Germany → Balkans → Constantinople → Anatolia → Syria → Palestine. **The full march took 2-3 years.** Of every 30,000 who set out, fewer than 10,000 reached the destination — the rest starved, fell ill, were robbed, or were slaughtered by the kingdoms they crossed.

### Climate (L1)

Like Magna Carta's setting, the years around 1100 fell inside the **Medieval Warm Period**:
- **European population doubled** → second and third sons had no land to inherit
- When **Pope Urban II** told the men of Clermont, "Stay in Europe and you keep robbing each other; go east and you can earn land plus salvation," the line landed because **Western Europe really was overpopulated**
- The Middle East was warm and wet too — agriculture was stable, the Fatimid caliphate, Seljuk sultanate, and Byzantium were all strong, producing a multi-polar power struggle

### Society (L2)

In 1099, four highly developed political worlds existed at the same time:

```
 ┌──────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────┐
 │ Latin West (Christian)   │ Byzantine Empire (Orthodox)     │
 │ ~40 million people ·     │ ~15 million people ·            │
 │ feudal agriculture ·     │ Constantinople 500,000 ·        │
 │ 95% illiteracy ·         │ scholarly preservation ·        │
 │ 200+ fragmented duchies  │ pope-vs-emperor structure       │
 ├──────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────┤
 │ Seljuk Sultanate (Sunni) │ Fatimid Caliphate (Shi'a)       │
 │ Baghdad · 1071 broke     │ Cairo · 1099 controlled         │
 │ the Byzantine army       │ Jerusalem                        │
 │ Central Asia to Syria    │ wealthy merchants · scholars ·   │
 │                          │ diplomatic ties to Byzantium     │
 └──────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Key numbers**:
- Constantinople 500,000 + Baghdad 1,000,000 + Cairo 500,000 — **the Islamic world was vastly more urbanized than the Latin West**
- London ~20,000. Paris ~25,000. **Twenty times smaller than Constantinople, forty times smaller than Baghdad.** Western Europe was the village cousin in this trio.
- Scholarship: the Arab world had preserved the complete works of **Aristotle, Euclid, Hippocrates** — every major Greek text. Western Europe had lost most of them. Byzantium kept the originals in Greek.

**This is the part that flips everything**: in economics, culture, and education, **the "advanced civilizations" of 1099 were the Islamic world and Byzantium — not the West**. The Crusades were not "advanced attacking backward." They were the opposite — the poor cousin showing up at the rich cousin's door, swords drawn.

### Institutions (L3) — Three Different Religion-State Relationships

This is the most important layer for understanding the Crusades. The three worlds gave three completely different answers to the question: **Who controls religion — the political ruler, or someone else?**

**① Latin Christendom: church power vs. royal power, two-pole tension**

Just **18 years before** Clermont, in 1077, the **Investiture Controversy** had reached its peak: **Pope Gregory VII** vs. Holy Roman Emperor **Henry IV**. Henry IV walked to the Italian fortress of **Canossa** and stood barefoot in the snow for three days asking forgiveness.

**The takeaway**: the pope and the emperor were two separate powers, pulling against each other. When Pope Urban II launched the Crusades in 1095, **one of his core goals was to make the pope outrank the emperor**. He used "the ability to mobilize armies across borders" to prove who was Europe's real top.

**② Byzantine Orthodoxy: caesaropapism — church and state are one**

The emperor (the Caesar) **was** the highest church authority. He appointed the patriarch. He ran the great church councils. **There was no "pope vs. emperor" tension because they were the same person.**

**③ The Islamic world: caliph + sultan**

In theory the **caliph** is the successor of Muhammad — **both religious and political leader**. But by 1099, real power had split:
- The Abbasid caliph in Baghdad was a figurehead
- The Seljuk **sultan** held real military and political power
- The Fatimid caliph in Cairo claimed rival legitimacy (Shi'a, against Baghdad's Sunni)

**This was the Levant's "power vacuum" in 1099 — the Islamic world was internally fractured, and the Crusade walked into the gap.**

## 3. The Main Characters (Three Perspectives)

### The Latin Christian Side

**Pope Urban II (1042-1099, reigned 1088-1099)**

#### Origins — French nobleman turned monk

Born in 1042 in **Champagne**, France, to a noble family. His birth name was **Odo of Châtillon-sur-Marne**.

At 19 he was supposed to become a knight. **He chose a different road**: the church at Reims. At 30 he entered the **Cluny monastery** — the **strictest, most prestigious monastery in 11th-century Europe**. The rules at Cluny were ferocious: monks prayed eight hours a day, did four hours of physical labor, and could not speak unless reciting scripture.

Cluny produced **five popes**. Urban II was the most influential of them.

In the 1080s he rose to **Cardinal Bishop of Ostia** (a senior cardinal plus bishop of the port city near Rome) — and became **Pope Gregory VII's right-hand man**.

#### Gregory VII's political inheritance

You cannot understand Urban II without Gregory VII — his teacher, his boss, his political model.

Gregory VII (reigned 1073-1085) was **the most radical pope in medieval history**. He did one thing in 1077 that shook all of Europe:

> **He forced Holy Roman Emperor Henry IV to stand barefoot in the snow outside the Italian castle of Canossa for three days and three nights**, begging forgiveness.
>
> An emperor on his knees before a pope. **Europe in 1100 saw that scene exactly once.**

The whole conflict was called the **Investiture Controversy**. The core question: **who appoints bishops — the pope or the secular king?** Gregory VII used the threat of excommunication to grind the emperor into the ground.

But Gregory VII did not end well. In 1084 Henry IV struck back, **marched into Rome**, drove Gregory south, and Gregory died in **exile in 1085**.

> Urban II watched his teacher die — die inside the very dream of papal supremacy he had spent his whole life building.

#### 1088 — Urban II takes a broken throne

In March 1088, at age 46, Urban II was elected pope.

But the situation he inherited was a wreck:
- **Rome was still in imperial hands** — he could not enter the city until 1093. For five years he wandered Europe like a refugee — France, northern Italy, Sicily, Lucca.
- **The Investiture Controversy was unresolved.** Henry IV was still alive and still fighting the pro-pope faction.
- **The 1054 Great Schism between East and West was only 34 years old.** Latin pope and Greek patriarch had excommunicated each other.
- **The papal treasury was empty.** The previous wars had cleaned it out.

**For the first three years of his papacy, Urban II's main job was to keep moving.** He was the first pope in European history to govern out of a traveling cart. From 1088 to 1093, he convened eight church councils — none of them in Rome.

#### 1093 returns to Rome — 1095 Clermont

In 1093 the imperial faction lost ground. **At 51, Urban II finally entered Rome.**

In the city he did two things:
1. **Rebuilt papal authority** — convened a bishops' council to reissue the principle of papal supremacy
2. **Looked for one big event that would put the pope at the center of European attention**

**The opportunity arrived in early 1095.**

In **March 1095**, the **Byzantine emperor Alexios I Komnenos** sent ambassadors to the **Council of Piacenza** asking for help.

The core message:
> "**The Seljuk Turks shattered us at Manzikert in 1071 and now hold 80% of Asia Minor.** We can't hold the line. Could you lend us a few thousand professional mercenaries?"

Alexios wanted **a few thousand soldiers**. Urban II saw something bigger — **Christian unity, plus a papal-led campaign east**. He scaled the request from "borrowed troops" up to "mobilize all of Europe."

#### November 27, 1095 — the Clermont sermon

Eight months of preparation later, on a **Tuesday afternoon, November 27, 1095**, in an open-air square outside the city of **Clermont in central France** —

**Urban II was 53.** He wore the white papal robe and a **mitre** (a bishop's pointed hat). He stood on a wooden platform built for the day. About 2,000 people had gathered:
- 13 archbishops
- 90 bishops
- hundreds of abbots
- and **a large crowd of nobles and townspeople** who had come to watch

He began to preach. In Latin.

There is no recording. Today we read the speech through **four chroniclers who reconstructed it later from memory**.

**The Fulcher of Chartres version** (most often cited; he was present; written around 1106) captures the core points:

> "My brothers — **Christians in the East need our help.**
> **A people who do not know God** has taken their land.
> You knights of France — **stop attacking each other** — go and fight them.
>
> Anyone who joins this expedition: **all of his sins will be forgiven.**
> Anyone who dies on the road: **goes straight to heaven.**"

When he finished, the crowd is said to have shouted: **"Deus vult! Deus vult!"** ("God wills it!") — the cry that would become the rallying call of the First Crusade.

#### Four motives, layered (debunking the "religious passion" single-cause theory)

**Urban II that day was not preaching out of pure religious fire.** He was executing a **carefully designed combination of four motives**:

1. **Answer the Byzantine call for help** — surface justification
2. **Reunify East and West Christianity** — the 1054 schism had bred deep hatred, but military cooperation could thaw it. Urban II wanted to be remembered as "the pope who reunified Christendom."
3. **Drain Europe's militant nobles** — through the 1080s and 1090s, second sons across Europe had no land (Medieval Warm Period population doubling + primogeniture). They were stabbing each other across France and Germany. Send them to the Holy Land. Reduce internal European violence.
4. **Make the pope outrank the emperor** — **this is the core**. If a pope could mobilize 100,000 Europeans on a transcontinental march, and an emperor could not, then the Investiture Controversy had its final answer.

**Motive #4 was Urban II's real political bet.** He was completing **Gregory VII's unfinished dream**: the pope as the highest power in Europe.

#### Plenary indulgence — his invention

The most genuinely **new** thing Urban II said that day was not the idea of crusading (the **Reconquista** in Spain had already used the same template). It was **plenary indulgence**:

> "**Go on this expedition, and every sin you have ever committed is forgiven — at once, completely.**"

This **legal-spiritual instrument did not exist before**. The medieval tradition of penance was incremental: you sinned, you did acts (pilgrimage, almsgiving, long fasting) to slowly reduce time in **purgatory**.

Urban II drew a single line: **going on Crusade = total reset.**

Medieval people **were genuinely terrified of hell after death**. This single line was, in effect, **a free ticket to heaven**.

> ⚠️ **Long causal chain**:
> Urban II's 1095 plenary indulgence
> → 13th-15th century papal abuse — **selling** indulgences to raise money
> → 1517 **Martin Luther** could no longer stand it; he posted the 95 Theses
> → which sparked the **Protestant Reformation**
> → which split Europe into Catholic and Protestant
> → which produced the **Thirty Years' War, 8 million dead, 1618-1648**
>
> **One sentence in 1095. The echo was still shaking Europe five centuries later.**

#### The end — dead 14 days too soon, July 29, 1099

After the 1095 sermon:
- Spring 1096: the **People's Crusade** — 30,000 unarmed pilgrims. They massacred Jewish communities along the Rhine. They reached Anatolia. The Seljuks crushed them in one engagement; almost all died.
- Autumn 1096: the **Princes' Crusade** — 50,000 noble warriors crossed the Bosphorus from Constantinople
- 1097-98: the **siege of Antioch** — eight months. Men chewed boiled shoes. Someone "found" a holy lance.
- **July 15, 1099**: Jerusalem captured. Massacre.

**Urban II died on July 29, 1099, in the Bishop's Palace in Rome. He was 57.**

> ⚠️ **History's coldest irony**:
> The day Urban II died, Jerusalem had already fallen — **14 days earlier**.
> But news from Jerusalem to Rome traveled six to eight weeks. **He died not knowing whether his sermon had succeeded.**
>
> He changed 1,000 years of European history. **He never heard how it turned out.**

#### Neutral framing

> ⚠️ **What you must NOT do**:
> - Paint Urban II as an "evil pope, architect of mass murder" — that is 19th-century Voltaire-style Enlightenment projection
> - Paint Urban II as an "Enlightenment forerunner, defender of civilization" — that is 19th-century European colonial projection
>
> **He acted inside the logic of his own time**:
> - Papal authority should be supreme (Gregory VII's political legacy)
> - Religious universalism (Christianity should unify the world)
> - Drain off the militant nobility (a real social management problem)
>
> **He was not a monster. But he did light the fuse that killed 60,000 Europeans on the road and produced the slaughter of thousands of Muslims.** Both sentences can be true at the same time.
>
> **Historical figures often do world-changing things without knowing whether they were good or bad. Urban II is the case study.**

**Bohemond of Taranto (c. 1054-1111)**

#### The "opportunist" Crusader, archetype

**The First Crusade was not 100,000 devoted believers. It was several different motives bundled together.** Of all the leaders, Bohemond was the **least disguised** about what he wanted: **a piece of land of his own.**

#### Origins: the disinherited Norman eldest son

Born around 1054 in southern Italy. His father, **Robert Guiscard** (Italian for "the Cunning"), was the Norman warlord who had carved out southern Italy by force.

Normally Bohemond, as eldest son, would inherit everything. But in the 1080s **his father remarried**, and the new wife produced **a half-brother, Roger Borsa**. When Robert Guiscard died in 1085, his **will gave the lands to Roger Borsa**. **Bohemond, at 30, suddenly had nothing.**

This was **the first time in his life he was robbed of an inheritance**. It became the engine of every motive that followed.

#### 1081-1085 — fighting Byzantium with his father

For four years, ages 27 to 31, Bohemond rode beside Robert Guiscard sweeping across the Adriatic. Their target: the entire Balkans.

**1081 Battle of Dyrrachium** — Bohemond, age 27, **personally beat Alexios I's Byzantine army** and captured several of his senior officers.

**Alexios remembered Bohemond for the rest of his life.** This is why, 14 years later, when Alexios asked Rome for help, **the man he most dreaded seeing on his doorstep was Bohemond.**

When Robert Guiscard died suddenly in 1085, Bohemond inherited nothing. He went home and fought his half-brother for scraps. Eleven years of small wars yielded only one scrap of southern Italy — **Taranto**. (Hence "Bohemond of Taranto.") **He wanted more. Italy was full.**

#### 1095 — he hears Urban II's call and smells the opening

Early 1096. Bohemond, 42 years old, was besieging a town in southern Italy. He heard about a movement calling knights to head east "to claim land and earn salvation."

**He understood instantly**:
- The East was not full like Italy
- **No one knew Byzantium better than he did** — he had fought it for four years with his father
- He spoke Greek and understood Byzantine military tactics
- The pope's blessing gave him **political legitimacy** — a step up from "looking like a pirate"

He immediately **lifted the siege he was in the middle of** and called every Norman knight in southern Italy to join him. That summer, **Bohemond led 500 knights and 1,000 footmen east**.

**Of all the Crusader leaders, he was the best prepared and the most experienced in war.**

#### Autumn 1096 — Constantinople

Imagine Alexios I's face when Bohemond appeared outside the city walls. This is the man who almost destroyed me in 1081 — and now he's here to "help"?

But Alexios was **shrewd**. He did three things:

1. **Refused to let Bohemond enter the city.** Camp outside.
2. **Buried him in gifts**: gold coins, silk, horses — make robbery pointless
3. **Forced him to swear an oath in front of the entire imperial court**: "Any old Byzantine territory you reconquer will be returned to Byzantium."

**Bohemond swore the oath publicly. He was already planning how to break it.**

#### 1097-98 — Antioch siege, 8 months — his greatest performance

October 1097: the Crusaders reached **Antioch** in northern Syria. Population 500,000. A Seljuk stronghold. Walls 18 meters high.

**Eight months of siege.**

Bohemond did three things:

1. **Military command**. He was the Crusader who understood siege warfare best. He coordinated the staggered assaults and the supply blockade.
2. **Political maneuvering**. He spent the eight months **slowly turning** the other Crusader leaders, repeating: "**Antioch should be mine.** Byzantium has done nothing."
3. **Recruiting an inside man**. Inside the city he found an Armenian guard named **Firouz** (mistreated by his Seljuk master) and **paid him to open a gate**.

**Late on the night of June 3, 1098** — Firouz dropped a rope ladder. Bohemond was the first man up. The walls fell.

The next three days: **massacre at Antioch**. Every Muslim inside, killed.

#### "Prince of Antioch" — public oath-breaking

The day after the city fell, Bohemond **seized the citadel and named himself "Prince of Antioch."**

The other Crusader leaders (especially the Byzantine envoys) said, "**This is Byzantine territory. You swore an oath.**"

Bohemond answered, ice in his voice:
> "**Alexios sent no reinforcements.** We starved for eight months and ate boiled shoes. He failed to perform his side. So I am released from mine."

That is one of history's most famous **oath-breaking defenses**: turn the other party's inaction into the legal ground for your own betrayal.

The **Principality of Antioch lasted 170 years** — but it was **never part of the Kingdom of Jerusalem**. It was **Bohemond's private fief** from the start. That was always the plan.

> ⚠️ **Teaching point**: in the First Crusade, **fewer than half of the men were genuine believers**. Bohemond's type — "religious flag, land hunger" — was at least 30%. Later ages (especially the 19th century) romanticized the Crusaders as devoted holy warriors. **That picture is wrong.** Real First Crusade = half believers + half opportunists + a sprinkling of outlaws. Bohemond is the textbook opportunist. Without his type, you cannot explain why **the Crusader States were already fighting each other from day one**.

#### Aftermath — captured 1100, broken 1107

In 1100 Bohemond was **captured by the Danishmend Turks**. He spent three years in prison. Ransomed in 1103 for **1,300 pounds of gold**.

In 1107 he did something **wild**: he marched **directly against Byzantium proper**. He wanted to finish what his father failed to do in 1081 — take Constantinople itself.

**The campaign was a disaster.** Alexios trapped him at Dyrrachium. The 1108 **Treaty of Devol** forced Bohemond to **formally accept that he was a feudal vassal of Alexios.**

For the first time in 27 years, Bohemond bowed his head. **And it broke him psychologically.**

He died in 1111 at age 57 in southern Italy — **never returning to Antioch** (his nephew took over).

**His life summary**: disinherited at 30 → seized Antioch at 50 → ground into the dirt by Byzantium at 53 → mental collapse, dead at 57.

The Principality of Antioch he founded would last until 1268 — 170 years. But **at his death, Antioch was not his.** He died in a small Italian town called **Canosa**, far from anything he had taken.

**Godfrey of Bouillon (1060-1100)**

#### The "true believer" Crusader, archetype

If Bohemond is the opportunist, **Godfrey is the opposite pole**: this man genuinely went for faith. **He lived like a saint on the Crusader road.** When Jerusalem fell in 1099 and his peers tried to crown him king, **he refused the crown** — and that refusal defined how Christians would remember him for 800 years.

#### Origins: a middling duke from the French-German border

Born 1060 in **Lower Lorraine** (today the borderlands of Belgium, Luxembourg, and western Germany). His father, Eustace II of Boulogne, was **middling nobility** — smaller than Bohemond's "warlord dynasty," but bigger than ordinary knights.

Godfrey inherited the title of **Duke of Lower Lorraine** at 18. But it took him **ten years of war** to make the regional nobility actually accept him as duke. He was not born into authority; he fought for it.

#### The 1090s — he did something strange. Sold everything.

After Urban II's 1095 sermon, **Godfrey, age 35, did something that stunned everyone in his time**:

> **He sold every family castle.**
> **He mortgaged his main residence at Bouillon and all the surrounding estates.**
> **He raised 1,500 silver marks** — and at that time an ordinary knight earned **3 silver marks per year**.
> **That's the equivalent of 500 knights' annual income.**

Why?

**Most direct motive**: his mother **Ida** had grown deeply devout in the 1090s and raised him to be "a knight who lived like a monk." At 35, Godfrey **genuinely believed that going on Crusade was God's work**.

Selling everything meant something specific: **he was not planning to come back.** He would either die on the road or die in Jerusalem. He left himself **no exit.**

This is the exact opposite of Bohemond. **Bohemond traveled light because he wanted other people's land. Godfrey sold his land because he expected to die abroad.**

History gave us **two 35-to-40-year-old men comparing answers**. Same Crusade. **Opposite reasons.**

#### 1096-1099 — the march to Jerusalem

Godfrey set out in 1096 with **40,000 men** — by far the largest Crusader contingent (Bohemond had 1,500). He arrived at the walls of Jerusalem on **June 7, 1099**.

Five weeks of siege. **The city fell on July 15, 1099.**

#### The day the wall fell — first man over, but not the one giving the orders

On the afternoon of July 15, the Crusaders rolled their **siege towers** up against the wall. **Godfrey was among the first men over.** He led troops into the city.

But —

**During the three-day Jerusalem massacre that followed**, with every Muslim inside the city and most of the Jews killed —

**Godfrey was not at the killing.** He had walked **directly to the Holy Sepulchre** (the church built over the supposed tomb of Christ) and **spent the night kneeling in prayer**.

> ⚠️ **Historical complexity**: Godfrey did not participate in the massacre — but he was **the senior military commander**. **He never ordered the killing to stop.**
>
> He is **not a "killer"** and **not "innocent."** He is **the devout commander of a massacring army.** This kind of position was common in the Middle Ages. Morally it is one of the hardest things to handle.
>
> The Arab historian **Ibn al-Athir**, writing 100 years later: "**Their general Godfrey was on his knees weeping in prayer while his soldiers killed 40,000 of us.**"

#### July 22, 1099 — refusing the crown

Seven days after the city fell, the other Crusader leaders **proposed to crown Godfrey King of Jerusalem**. He had sold all his lands, fought hard, prayed devoutly — every box was checked.

**Godfrey refused.**

His reason became one of the most famous lines in medieval Christian history:

> **"How can I wear a crown of gold**
> **where Christ wore a crown of thorns?"**

He agreed to lead the kingdom — but insisted his title be **"Advocate of the Holy Sepulchre"** ("Defender of the Holy Sepulchre"), **not "King."**

That refusal defined his historical image:
- **300 years later**, Dante placed Godfrey **in Paradise** in The Divine Comedy
- **400 years later**, Michelangelo painted his portrait
- **800 years later**, when Belgium became independent in 1830, Godfrey was claimed as a national forefather
- **Today, a statue of Godfrey stands in central Brussels**

#### July 18, 1100 — dead at 40

**One year after refusing the crown**, on July 18, 1100, Godfrey died of illness in Jerusalem. **He was 40.**

Cause of death: probably **typhoid**, possibly **slow poisoning** (one tradition says a Muslim emir sent him poisoned fruit). Historians still argue.

He had no children. The Kingdom of Jerusalem passed to his **brother Baldwin I** — who **did not refuse the crown**. Baldwin took it openly and became **the first formal "King of Jerusalem."**

The purity of Godfrey's refusal — his brother did not hold the line for one year.

> ⚠️ **One of medieval history's most complicated figures**:
>
> Godfrey was **genuinely devout** + **genuinely had a moral compass** (the crown refusal) + **genuinely commanded the massacre army at its peak**.
>
> All three are simultaneously true. **This is one of the hardest personality types in medieval history.**
>
> Modern lenses tend to flatten:
> - "He was a devout hero" (19th-century Belgian nationalist version, plus Romantic painters)
> - "He was a war-crime commander" (20th-century post-colonial critique)
>
> **Both are right.** Historical figures often work this way: a single person is **simultaneously good and evil — and they themselves cannot see the contradiction inside them.**
>
> The night Godfrey knelt in the Holy Sepulchre, **he genuinely felt he was doing God's work.** He was **not a hypocrite.** He simply **had no modern moral tool to look at what his soldiers were doing two streets away.**
>
> **This is the deepest medieval tragedy**: **real faith can let real good people participate in real evil — and the believer themselves never finds out.**

#### Bohemond vs. Godfrey — two souls of one Crusade

| | Bohemond | Godfrey |
|---|---|---|
| Motive | **Land** | **Atonement + faith** |
| Before departure | Recruited troops | Sold everything |
| Antioch | Publicly broke his oath, took the city | Stayed out of Antioch politics |
| Massacre | Commanded the Antioch slaughter | Did not direct the Jerusalem killing — but did not stop it |
| Crown | Self-titled "Prince of Antioch" | Refused the Jerusalem crown |
| Death | Mental collapse, exiled from his own lands | Died of illness, buried at the Holy Sepulchre |
| Memory | A forgotten conqueror | Belgian national hero, Dante's Paradise |

**The First Crusade had 100,000 men.** Bohemond and Godfrey are the **two extremes**. **The real 80% lived somewhere in between** — a little faith, a little land hunger, a little following the crowd. **That is the real Crusade.**

### The Byzantine Side

**Alexios I Komnenos (1056-1118, reigned 1081-1118)**

#### He started the war — but not the way he wanted to

History's irony: **the spark of the First Crusade was not Pope Urban II. It was the Byzantine emperor Alexios.** But Alexios wanted "a few thousand soldiers." What he got was 100,000 men, 200 years of war, and his own empire eventually destroyed by the very people he had asked for help.

#### Origins: a general who took the throne

Born 1056 in Constantinople into the **Komnenos family** — old Byzantine military nobility.

At 24 (in 1081), he did the most common thing in Byzantine history: **a military coup.**

How: the reigning emperor **Nikephoros III** was old and ineffective. Byzantium was being attacked simultaneously by five neighbors (Seljuks, Normans, Pechenegs, Bulgars, Arabs). Alexios at 24 had the highest military reputation. He surrounded Constantinople for four days, broke in, and **forced Nikephoros III to abdicate to a monastery.**

Crowned April 4, 1081.

#### What he inherited

The Byzantium he took over was **in the worst shape in its history**:

- **Army**: 60% had been wiped out at Manzikert in 1071; the rest scattered
- **Treasury**: empty enough that he had to melt church silver into coin
- **Territory**: 80% of Asia Minor — the empire's main farmland and recruiting ground — had fallen to the Seljuks
- **Western front**: Robert Guiscard's Normans had taken Dyrrachium in 1081 and were preparing to march on Constantinople
- **Balkans**: Pecheneg nomads raiding every year
- **Italy**: Bari, the last Byzantine foothold in Italy, fell in 1071

**At 24, Alexios had to save an empire that looked finished.**

#### 1081-1095 — fourteen years of fighting on five fronts

Over the next 14 years, he did several **astonishing** things:

1. **1083-1085 — drove out the Normans**. He allied with Venice (trading commercial privileges for naval power) and pushed Robert Guiscard back into Italy.
2. **1091 Battle of Levounion**. Allied with the Cuman Turks and **almost wiped out the Pecheneg people** — known to history as "the Pecheneg massacre."
3. **Maneuvered against the Seljuks**. Could not retake Asia Minor, but stabilized the border.
4. **Restored the gold coin** — the **hyperpyron** — restoring economic credit
5. **Founded the Komnenos dynasty for 100 years**. The base he laid kept Byzantium alive an extra century.

**From 24 to 38, he held the line.** At 38, in **1095**, he made the decision that would change 1,000 years of European history.

#### The 1095 letter

Internal Seljuk fractures opened in the 1090s. Alexios saw an opening: **retake Asia Minor.**

But he did not have enough troops of his own. So he tried something clever.

> **He wrote to Pope Urban II in Rome**:
> "**Send me a few thousand professional Christian mercenaries** — I'll pay their wages."

The core of the request:
- **A few thousand** (rebellion-suppressing scale, not an army)
- **Professional mercenaries** (not civilians)
- **He would pay** (an employment relationship, not an alliance)
- **Target: Asia Minor** (not Jerusalem — Jerusalem was not on his agenda at all)

**Urban II received the letter in March 1095.**

Over the next eight months, Alexios's "few thousand mercenaries" was **silently rewritten by Urban II into "all of Europe goes east."**

**At Clermont, Urban II did not even mention Alexios.** He swapped "help Byzantium" for "**reclaim the Christian holy sites**." He swapped "few thousand mercenaries" for "**every Christian in Europe.**"

Alexios's letter became **something he himself could no longer recognize.**

#### Summer 1096 — 100,000 people arrive

June-August 1096. Alexios, 38, sat in Constantinople watching **the impossible happen**:

- The **People's Crusade** first wave: 30,000 unarmed pilgrims. Massacred Jews along the Rhine. Looted Byzantine villages on the way through.
- The **Princes' Crusade** second wave: 50,000 fully armed nobles, nominally "allies"
- Plus their families, merchants, priests, drifters — total over **100,000**

This was the moment of **most ironic dilemma**: the army he had requested was now the largest threat at his city walls.

**What he did** (the most brilliant months of his reign):

1. **Ferried them across in waves.** Never let more than 50,000 stand on Byzantine soil at once.
2. **Forced an oath.** Every Crusader leader had to swear, before entering the city: "Any old Byzantine territory I reconquer will be returned to you."
3. **Got them out of his territory fast.** Food + maps + translators + push them across the Bosphorus into Seljuk land. Let them fight Seljuks, not Greeks.
4. **Sent 600 Byzantine soldiers along** to monitor and to receive surrendered cities (he successfully reclaimed parts of Asia Minor this way)

**Result**:
- He did get parts of Asia Minor back (Nicaea 1097, areas around Antioch 1098). **His goal was achieved.**
- The Crusaders did not return all the territory — Bohemond's seizure of Antioch was the headline betrayal
- **Latin and Byzantine Christians built up permanent resentment**, which would explode in **1204 when the Fourth Crusade came back and sacked Constantinople**

#### His ledger vs. the pope's ledger

| | What Alexios wanted | What Urban II wanted |
|---|---|---|
| Force | A few thousand professionals | 100,000-strong popular mobilization |
| Goal | Asia Minor (farmland) | Jerusalem (holy site) |
| Relationship | Employment (he pays) | Papal call (pope leads) |
| Duration | 1-2 years | Permanent |
| Outcome | Recover territory | Pope outranks emperor |

**Their goals were misaligned from day one.** That misalignment exploded **in 1204 when the Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople**. **The army Alexios called for came back 108 years later and destroyed his empire.** One of the longest causal-irony chains in history.

#### 1118 — dead at 62

Alexios died on August 15, 1118 in Constantinople. Age 62. Reigned 37 years.

His final political conflict was over succession. He wanted his son **John II** to take the throne. His wife **Irene** and his eldest daughter **Anna Komnene** wanted to install Anna's husband, Nikephoros Bryennios. **The plot failed.**

> Anna never accepted the loss. **It changed the rest of her life** (see her entry below).

**Verdict on Alexios**: he saved an empire that looked finished — and accidentally summoned the force that would destroy it 200 years later. **He did everything right that he could do. History does not always reward the people who do everything right.**

**Anna Komnene (1083-1153)**

#### The first female historian in the Christian world

Born December 1, 1083, in the **Purple Birthing Room** of the Constantinople imperial palace. The Purple Room was reserved for Byzantine royal use — only **children born while their father was the reigning emperor** could use it. Such children were called **"Porphyrogenita"** ("born in the purple") — born with succession rights.

Anna was **Alexios's eldest daughter**. The day she was born, **Constantinople celebrated for seven days and seven nights** — Byzantine custom held that an eldest daughter's birth foretold the empire's continuity.

Holding the newborn, **Alexios announced**: "**This is my heir.**" He betrothed her at age 4 to a distant cousin, Constantine Doukas.

**Anna was raised from the start as a future ruler**:
- Greek, Latin, classical philosophy (Aristotle, Plato)
- Medicine, astronomy, geometry
- Byzantine law and administration
- At 9, she could recite the entire Iliad from memory

**She was one of the most educated women in 11th-12th century Europe** — better educated than most contemporary European kings.

#### Age 7 — her first loss of the throne

In **1087, when she was 4**, her mother Irene gave birth to a son: **John II Komnenos**.

Byzantine custom: **sons take precedence**. Anna's succession right transferred to her brother instantly.

When she was **7 (1090)**, her mother said, "You and John are the same level of child." But Anna **saw the truth**: she had gone from "the future emperor being introduced to ambassadors" to "the emperor's older sister."

Her fiancé Constantine Doukas died unexpectedly in 1095. She was **widowed at 12**. In 1097 she remarried **Nikephoros Bryennios**, a learned Byzantine general — a political match.

#### 1118 — coup attempt fails

In August 1118, as her father lay dying, **Anna and her mother Irene plotted** to install Nikephoros Bryennios as emperor (bypassing John II).

But **her husband refused**. He said publicly: "I have learning, not ambition."

John II caught wind of the plot, **rushed into the palace, and crowned himself before anyone else moved**. By the time Alexios died, **John II was already emperor.**

**Anna was 35. The coup had completely failed.**

#### Thirty years of confinement — history's greatest "imprisoned" historian

John II did not kill her (he was gentle with his sister) — but he **confined her to the Kecharitomene monastery** in the north of Constantinople. **Anna would never leave that monastery again for the next 30 years.**

Her husband Nikephoros Bryennios died in 1136. **Anna was widowed at 53** for the second time.

It was **after his death + during the final 17 years of her confinement (1136-1153)** that Anna **completed the project her father had asked her to undertake**:

> **The *Alexiad*** — a 15-book Greek history, roughly **400,000 words**.

#### What the Alexiad is

The book records **the 37-year reign of her father Alexios (1081-1118)** — every event, including the **First Crusade — from the Byzantine perspective, first-hand**.

**Why it matters**:

1. **First-hand source**. Anna **personally met** many Crusader leaders (Bohemond, Godfrey, others were received in the imperial palace).
2. **Byzantine perspective**. The Latin chroniclers (Fulcher, William of Tyre) wrote, "We Christians liberated the holy places." Anna wrote, "Those **Latin barbarians** came to our border to plunder." This is one of the earliest **anti-imperial perspectives** preserved in writing.
3. **Female perspective**. Every other major 12th-century European historian was male. Anna is the only woman. **The details she notices** (a person's appearance, their clothes, their manners, their feelings) are details no male chronicler bothered to record.
4. **Scholarly depth**. She quotes Homer, Plato, Byzantine law. **In 13th-century European scholarship, her book stood at the level of Thucydides.**

**Her famous description of the Latins**:
> "**They are like wild beasts — enormous appetites, impatient, untrustworthy.**
> Their women came on the campaign too — **with appetites as large as the men**,
> wielding spears in battle. **In our civilization this is unimaginable.**"

— Anna's 12th-century portrait of "barbarian Western Europeans" is the **exact opposite** of today's Hollywood "civilized Western Europe vs. exotic Byzantine Empire."

The truth in 1095: **Byzantium was a hundred years more civilized than the West.** Anna's perspective is the contemporary fact.

**To read the Alexiad today is to invert the modern Hollywood lens** and see 1095 as it actually was.

#### The fun irony — a woman wrote a classic of historiography while locked up

> ⚠️ **One of history's most beautiful ironies**:
>
> In 1118, Anna at 35 lost her coup and was confined.
> **For 30 years she went nowhere.** Just the Kecharitomene monastery, books, and letters with scholars.
>
> But it was **those 30 years of "deprivation"** that produced the 400,000 words of the Alexiad — **Europe's first national history written by a woman, and the first first-hand Crusade account.**
>
> If she had won the coup of 1118 and become empress, she would have spent her life on court politics, war, and administration. **She would never have had time to write this book.**
>
> Anna's **failed political life** made possible her **successful scholarly life**. **One of history's deepest ironies: some defeats are not losses. They are forced reassignments to longer-lasting work.**

#### 1153 — dead at 70

Anna died at the Kecharitomene monastery in 1153. **Age 70.** Her last recorded words: "**I never stopped writing.**"

She was buried at the monastery — never returning to the palace.

Her brother John II had been dead for 10 years. The reigning emperor was her nephew Manuel I Komnenos. He attended her funeral — **acknowledging her contribution to Byzantium.**

#### Posthumous reception

The Alexiad circulated in the 13th century. The Latin world began translating it in the 14th. **It was truly rediscovered in the 19th century.**

Today the Alexiad is:
- **Required reading for university world history courses** ("If you want the truth about the Crusades, read Anna Komnene")
- **A feminist classic** ("the highest scholarly achievement open to a medieval woman")
- **The Bible of Byzantine studies** (without it, our knowledge of 11th-12th century Byzantium would be cut in half)

**Did Anna know all this in her lifetime?** In the last book of the Alexiad she wrote: "**I hope someone, someday, reads this book and understands my father.**"

She did not know — that 900 years later, **people still read it**.
She did not know — that she would be remembered **even more deeply than her father.**

### The Islamic Side

**Saladin (Salah ad-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub, 1137-1193)**

#### The Kurdish boy who rose to the top

**Born 1137 in Tikrit** (today northern Iraq — and, by coincidence, Saddam Hussein's hometown).

His father, **Najm ad-Din Ayyub**, was a castle warden under the Seljuk sultans. **The family was Kurdish** — not Arab, not Turkish. Kurdish.

In 12th-century Islamic society, **Kurds were second-class** — not as legitimate as Arabs, not as militarily dominant as Turks.

The young Saladin grew up in **Damascus**. He **was not interested in soldiering**. He preferred:
- Reading the Qur'an
- Reading Arabic poetry
- Studying Islamic law

At 15, in the Damascus court, he was thought of as **shy, quiet, bookish**. **No one expected him to become the most famous leader of the Islamic world for the next two centuries.**

#### Age 26 — first campaign, dragged along by his uncle

In 1163, his uncle **Shirkuh** led troops into Egypt to "help" (really, to seize power amid the chaos there). Saladin, 26, was **dragged along by his uncle**. **He did not want to go.** He wrote to a friend: "I am afraid."

But once in Egypt, **he discovered he was good at war**:
- 1167 **Battle of al-Babein** — with 25 cavalry he repelled 2,000 infantry
- 1168 — besieged in Alexandria for 75 days; **personally directed the defense from the walls**

In 1169 his uncle Shirkuh died suddenly.

**Saladin, age 32, was elected vizier of Egypt** — almost by accident, the actual ruler of Egypt.

#### 1171 — ending the Fatimids, a political masterstroke

Egypt was nominally under the **Fatimid dynasty** (Shi'a Islam). But **Saladin was Sunni.**

In 1171 the **last Fatimid caliph died**. Saladin **did not let a successor take the throne**. At Friday prayer in Cairo, he simply **announced**:
> "From today, Egypt's khutbah (sermon) names the **Sunni Abbasid Caliph**."

— **Two hundred years of Shi'a rule in Egypt ended in one Friday.** Saladin reunified Egypt with the Sunni mainstream **without drawing a sword.**

The Abbasid Caliph in Baghdad (the nominal head of the Islamic world) sent a letter naming him **Sultan of Egypt.** Saladin was 34. **He was now Sultan.**

#### 1174-1186 — twelve years to unify the Middle East

Over the next 12 years:
- He marched **north** to Damascus — 1174 absorbed Syria
- Continued north — 1183 absorbed Aleppo
- Marched east — 1186 absorbed Mosul

**The whole Middle East (except the Crusader States) was now his Ayyubid dynasty.**

But he did not attack the Crusaders. **He waited.**

#### July 4, 1187 — Battle of Hattin, a tactics masterclass

In May 1187, Crusader **King Guy of Lusignan** did something foolish: he **broke the truce** by raiding a Saladin caravan. Saladin decided it was time.

**July 4, 1187** — in Upper Galilee, at the **Horns of Hattin** (twin hill peaks):

Saladin used a **brilliant** plan:

1. **Force the Crusaders away from water.** He pretended to attack the city of Tiberias. King Guy decided to "rescue" it — and **30,000 Crusaders left the well-watered Sephoria camp**.
2. **Desert march + burn the grass.** Saladin had **burned all the dry grass along the route** in advance. The Crusader horses were **maddened with thirst.**
3. **Surround them and wait for thirst.** On the morning of July 4, he encircled 30,000 Crusaders **on a waterless ridge.**
4. **Attack in the afternoon.** When the Crusaders **collapsed from dehydration**, he attacked head-on.

**Result**:
- 30,000 Crusaders **almost completely destroyed**. Most died of thirst or in battle.
- **King Guy was captured.**
- **The True Cross** (the supposed wood of Christ's cross) — **the most sacred Christian relic in the medieval world**, venerated by both Latin and Byzantine Christianity — **was captured by Saladin.**
- **Templars and Hospitallers** (the warrior monastic orders) — **all members executed.** Saladin only handed out death sentences to those two orders. Ordinary knights were allowed to ransom out.

#### October 2, 1187 — Jerusalem surrenders

Three months after Hattin, on **October 2, 1187**, Saladin reached the walls of Jerusalem.

The defenders were down to one knight, **Balian of Ibelin**, plus a few thousand civilians. Balian sent a negotiator:

> "**Let us pay ransom and leave** — or we will burn Al-Aqsa, destroy the Holy Sepulchre, kill every one of the 5,000 Muslim prisoners we hold, and the whole city will go up together."

**Saladin accepted.** This was **his decisive moment as a politician.**

The ransom rates:
- Men: **10 dinars**
- Women: **5 dinars**
- Children: **1 dinar**

About **8,000-15,000 people could not pay** and were enslaved. But Saladin simultaneously did three things:

1. **Personally paid 7,000 dinars** to ransom the elderly and the sick
2. **Allowed charitable institutions to ransom thousands more** of the poor
3. **Permitted the poor to leave together as organized groups** (rather than be scattered into slavery)

**No massacre.** A pointed contrast with the Revelation-borrowed "blood up to the horses' bridles" at 1099.

> ⚠️ **Historical detail**:
> 1099 Crusader massacre: **2-3 days**. Christians, Muslims, and Jews all killed, no distinction by sect. A Latin monk, borrowing from Revelation, wrote "blood reached the horses' bridles."
> 1187 Saladin's recapture: **no massacre** — but **also not free passage for everyone.** **Ransom + partial enslavement** is the historical fact.
>
> **Not "slaughter" and not "complete release"** — but a **middle category.**

#### 1189-92 — the famous duel with Richard the Lionheart

The **Third Crusade** (1189-92) — Europe's three biggest kings (Holy Roman Emperor Frederick Barbarossa, French king Philip II, English king Richard I "the Lionheart") joined forces and marched east.

Frederick **drowned** halfway in 1190 — swept away crossing a Turkish river in armor.
Philip II **went home** halfway — domestic instability.

**Richard the Lionheart held on alone**. In 1191 he took **Acre**.

Then for one year — **the legendary duel of Richard vs. Saladin**:

- **Richard, age 32, fighting style: ferocious.** He charged at the front. Legend says he killed nine enemies in a single stroke at Arsuf.
- **Saladin, age 54, style: calm.** He did not charge personally. He **sent his enemy water.** When Richard was wounded at Arsuf, Saladin sent **his personal physician and a basket of fresh fruit.**

**The two never met in person** — only through letters and across the battlefield. But they **respected each other**. Richard said publicly: "**Saladin is the noblest man outside Christendom.**" Saladin said publicly: "**Richard is the most worthy opponent I have faced.**"

#### 1192 — truce, no winner

In September 1192 the **Treaty of Jaffa** was signed:
- **Jerusalem stays under Saladin**
- **Christians may make pilgrimage to Jerusalem** undisturbed
- **Three-year truce**

A **military stalemate** plus exhaustion on both sides — and a treaty with **no formal winner.**

**But really**: Saladin kept Jerusalem. **Goal achieved.** Richard did not retake Jerusalem — but he went home **carrying all of Europe's respect.**

#### 1193 — dead at 56

On March 4, 1193, Saladin died of illness in Damascus. **Age 56.**

Before he died, he did one thing that **shocked the Arab world**: he **gave away his property to the poor**. To his sons he left only: **1 dinar, 47 dirhams, one horse, and his sword.**

The funeral was simple. He was **buried in a plain stone sarcophagus in Damascus.**

> ⚠️ **Fun historical fact**:
> Saladin fought 100+ battles in his lifetime.
> But **on the day of his funeral, his treasury did not have enough to buy a coffin.** His son borrowed the money.
>
> **He unified the Middle East for 12 years, retook Jerusalem, fought Europe's three greatest kings — and died with a net worth of 1 dinar, one horse, and one sword.**
>
> **That fact is still inscribed on his tomb in Damascus today.**

#### Posthumous image — 600 years of romanticization

**Saladin's image in Europe took a strange arc**:

- **1187-1300**: Europeans called him "infidel" — used negatively
- **1300-1500**: Italian writers, including **Boccaccio** in The Decameron, began to **idealize** him
- **1500-1700**: the European Enlightenment **further romanticized** him — Saladin became "the ideal non-Christian"
- **1825**: **Walter Scott** wrote The Talisman — staging "noble Saladin" against "barbaric Crusaders." **This novel created the Saladin of modern popular imagination.**
- **Dante's *Divine Comedy*** (1320): placed Saladin in **Limbo (the first circle of Hell)** alongside **Plato, Aristotle, Homer, and Caesar.** This is the **highest praise a Christian could give a non-Christian** — Dante could not place him in Heaven (Saladin was not Christian), but **he gave Saladin the "noble pagan" position.**

> ⚠️ **Do NOT be taken in by the "noble Saladin" narrative**:
>
> - He was **an effective practitioner of jihad**, not a pacifist. He killed many Christians in battle.
> - He **used violence against Shi'a Muslims** when consolidating Egypt in 1171.
> - He **executed all Templars and Hospitallers** after Hattin.
> - His "tolerance" had **political calculation behind it** — a massacre would have damaged future tax bases and made European retaliation easier.
> - **"Saladin nobler than the Crusaders" is a 19th-century Romantic projection.** Eighteenth-century Britain wanted to bash France and the Catholic Church, and "a noble non-Christian hero" served that political need. Saladin was selected.
>
> **The real Saladin** = **tactical master + skilled politician + relatively restrained ruler**. **Not a saint of mercy.** **Not a barbaric infidel.**

> **Teaching point**: when you see a Walter Scott-style "Eastern wisdom vs. Western barbarism" narrative — that is the **19th-century European reflecting on its own colonial guilt**, using Saladin as a mirror for self-criticism. **It is reading 1187 through 1825 lenses, and it is wrong.**
>
> **The real history**: 1187 Saladin was **an effective ruler in his time** — a bit better than contemporary European kings (no massacre), and a bit worse than contemporary Tang/Song Chinese emperors (China never needed a "we don't massacre" narrative because there were no religious wars to massacre in).
>
> **Each civilization handled its own conditions in its own way.** That is the true Rule 0 neutral perspective.

**Ibn al-Athir (1160-1233)**

Arab historian. He wrote **al-Kamil fi al-Tarikh ("The Complete History")** — a massive work that includes the entire Crusader period from the Islamic perspective. **The most detailed first-hand description of the 1099 Jerusalem massacre comes from him**:

> "They killed more than 70,000 Muslims, including imams, scholars, and ascetics. They sacked the Al-Aqsa Mosque."

His figure of 70,000 is now considered too high (modern estimates range 3,000-10,000). But **his pen preserved the voice of the conquered side.** Without him, our understanding of the Crusades would be severely one-sided.

### Ordinary People (cosplay personas)

**A Norman second son (composite)**

#### You are 25, with no land and no future

You are **Guillaume de Caen** — second son of a middling Norman noble family.

In medieval Normandy the rule was iron: **the eldest son inherits all land, all castles, all titles**. Your brother Robert is four years older — **he inherited everything from your father.**

In 1095 you turn 25. **Your options are narrow**:
- Become a **mercenary knight** (England's wars need cash-paid riders)
- Become a **monk** (your mother wants this; you don't)
- Marry a **woman with inherited property** (but those women have already been claimed by other eldest sons)
- **Marry into another family** — but you give up your name

**There is no "become a lord like Father" option.**

#### November 1095 — you hear someone is preaching at Clermont

Late autumn 1095. You are 25, hunting in Normandy with a cousin. On the road you cross a wandering monk, **Peter the Hermit** — preaching Pope Urban II's message:

> "**All who go east — every sin forgiven.**
> **Anyone who dies on the road — straight to heaven.**
> **The East has empty land — what you take is yours.**"

**Four things detonate inside you at once**:

1. **Atonement**: you once killed a serf during a brawl by accident. **You have carried that for years.**
2. **Land**: "What you take is yours" — for the first time in your landless life, **a path opens.**
3. **Adventure**: Normandy is overcrowded. **You want to see something new.**
4. **Family honor**: if you come back with land, plunder, and stories — **your brother will never look down on you again.**

#### You sell everything — depart spring 1096

March 1096. You are 25. **You sell the two small fields your mother gave you as dowry — raise 5 silver marks.** You buy:
- A **war horse** (5 marks gets you a mediocre horse plus a second-hand chainmail shirt)
- A **Norman longsword** (your father's old one)
- A **round shield** + a **conical helmet** (Norman style with nose-guard)
- **Three months of dried food** (after that, plunder along the way)

You join your cousin Roland and four village boys in the contingent under **Robert Curthose** (William the Conqueror's son), Duke of Normandy. **The Norman column is 50,000 strong**, marching east.

You are 25. **You have never left France.**

#### 1097-98 — eighteen months on the road. Hell.

It takes eight months to cross Europe to Constantinople. **You are now 26.** You cross the Bosphorus into Anatolia. The fighting begins.

For the next 18 months, **you are starving, you are being shot at, and you are burying friends**:

- **June 1097, siege of Nicaea**: your first kill — a Seljuk guard in a white robe. You are 27. **Your hands shake.**
- **July 1, 1097, Battle of Dorylaeum**: of your five village friends, **three die.**
- **Autumn 1097, crossing the Anatolian desert**: half the horses die. **You drink horse blood once.**
- **October 1097, arrival at Antioch**: **eight-month siege.** You eat **boiled shoes, rats, and — in the last month — flesh from corpses.**
- **June 1098, Antioch falls**: **massacre.** You **lose count of how many you kill.**
- **Late 1098 to spring 1099**: **famine and plague.** At 28 you nearly die of dysentery.

Your cousin **Roland starved to death during the Antioch siege in 1098.** When you dug his grave, **your hands shook so hard you could barely hold the spade.**

#### July 15, 1099 — you enter Jerusalem

**Afternoon, July 15, 1099. You are 28.**

You **climb over the Jerusalem wall**. **Three days of massacre.**

You kill:
- A Muslim **old man** praying inside Al-Aqsa
- A Muslim **woman** running past you holding a child — **you cut her down before you saw the child**, and only afterward did you understand what was in her arms
- An **8-year-old Jewish boy** who burst from a corner — you assumed he was Muslim — you struck before realizing his clothes were not Muslim — but you had already moved
- **In total** — **you cannot remember.** Maybe ten. Maybe twenty.

**Evening of the third day** — you sit on the **steps of Al-Aqsa Mosque**. **You are covered in blood.** You cannot tell whose. **Your hands shake — and not from cold.**

#### 1100 — home at 30, a soul of 60

In 1100 you decide to go home. You are not Bohemond — you do not want to settle in the East. **You only want home.**

The journey home takes 8 months. You almost die twice — once in a shipwreck, once in a Seljuk ambush.

Autumn 1100. You are 30. You return to Normandy. Your brother Robert is away. **You wait.** When he comes home, **he does not recognize you** — you are 20 pounds lighter, sun-darkened, with a scar above your left eye.

He recognizes you and weeps. He gives you **one quarter of the family estate** — the Norman tradition for the returning warrior.

But your life is no longer the life you left.

#### The rest of your years — the dreams at night

You live another 35 years in Normandy. You die at 65.

But **for those 35 years, you dream at night**:
- The woman with the child
- The 8-year-old Jewish boy
- Your cousin Roland's face the moment he starved at Antioch

You go to church often. At 35 you ask the priest: "**Urban II said all my sins were forgiven. Is that true?**"
He says: "Yes. **You suffered for Christ.**"
You say: "**Then why do I still dream these dreams?**"
He says: "Because **you are a man with a conscience**. **Men without conscience do not dream like this.**"

The day you die at 65, your grandson asks: "Grandfather, what is the proudest thing you ever did?"
You think for a while and say: "**I came back from Jerusalem alive.**"

— Not pride. Not regret. Just I came back alive. That is all.

> **Walking in Guillaume's shoes**: at 25, three things — atonement, land, an exit — pulled you out the door. **You came back at 30 with a 60-year-old's interior.** You will never be light again.
>
> **Of the 50,000-70,000 Europeans who set out on the First Crusade**, about **60% died on the road**. The 40% who came back were mostly Guillaumes — bodies marked, dreams unmade.

**A Muslim resident of Jerusalem (composite)**

#### You are Ali. Your family has lived in Jerusalem for 200 years.

Your name is **Ali ibn Hassan**. Your family has lived in Jerusalem **since the Arab conquest in 638 — 461 years.** Your great-great-great-great-great-great-... grandfather came from near Mecca. **Thirteen generations were born in Jerusalem and died in Jerusalem.**

In 1099 you are **35**. You are a **coppersmith** — you inherited the shop from your father. You make **oil lamps, teapots, cooking ware**. Your shop is in the small alley next to **Souq al-Qattanin (the cotton market)** — a five-minute walk from Al-Aqsa.

#### Your neighbors — three religions sharing a street

Your neighbors:
- **Left**: a **Jewish shoemaker named Yitzhak** — his great-great-great-grandfather was also Jerusalem-born
- **Right**: an **Eastern Christian (Armenian) carpet weaver named Vartan** — his family came from Armenia in the 9th century
- **Across**: a **Syrian Christian (Greek Orthodox) baker named Yusuf**

**Your four families have shared this street for three generations**:
- When Yitzhak's wife was in confinement after childbirth, **your wife Fatima cooked her meals**
- When Vartan's son married, **you sent a pair of copper teapots** as a gift
- Yusuf's Friday bread — **you save him a loaf for his Sunday morning before church** (he doesn't bake on Sundays)
- Your 6-year-old son **plays daily with Vartan's son**. They speak a mixed **Armenian + Arabic + Greek "street kid language."**

**This is Jerusalem in early July 1099**: **three religions, five languages, 200 years of no major conflict.**

#### June 1099 — fear arrives

In **early June 1099**, the Fatimid governor begins mustering the city's small garrison. The rumor: **the Franj** (Arabic name for the Crusaders) **have crossed Antioch and are coming south.**

In your 35 years, you have **never seen a European**. But your grandfather told you stories about how the Seljuks came after Manzikert in 1071. **Foreign troops usually meant a wave of looting, then they leave. You assume this will be the same.**

**June 7. The Franj arrive outside the walls.** The siege begins.

#### July 1-15 — five weeks of siege

In the city, about **50,000 people**:
- 400-1,000 Fatimid soldiers
- ~40,000 residents — mostly Muslim, several thousand Jews, several thousand Christians

**The Franj attack daily and fail.** On **June 17** they finish building siege towers — assaults grow more effective.

**On July 8** they march **barefoot around the city walls** (imitating Joshua's seven circuits at Jericho). For the first time you understand: "**This group is not here to loot. They have come for the city itself. They want this place.**"

#### July 14 — the wall cracks

Afternoon of July 14: **the south wall breaks under siege-tower impact.** You are at your shop. **You hear the wall come down.** You drop your half-finished copper kettle and run home.

Your 7-year-old daughter Aisha. Your 5-year-old son Tariq. Your wife Fatima. **The four of you hide in the cellar.** Your wife holds the 5-year-old. You hold the 7-year-old. **From the cellar you hear screaming, weeping, and the sound of swords on flesh.**

#### July 15-17 — three days of massacre

For **three days and three nights you stay in the cellar.**

You hear:
- **Across the street**: Yusuf the baker and his entire family killed (you hear his wife scream)
- **Right side**: Vartan the Armenian killed (he shouted "I am a Christian!" — but **Armenian Christianity ≠ Latin Christianity** — the Franj **did not distinguish** — they killed him)
- **Left side**: Yitzhak the Jew and his family — **not slaughtered in the street.** They fled to the synagogue. **The Franj set fire to the synagogue.** **They burned to death together.**

For three days, the bodies pile up around Al-Aqsa. **The Latin monk Raymond of Aguilers later reached for the apocalyptic image of Revelation 14:20 and wrote "blood reached the horses' bridles"** — a phrase of religious purification, not a literal measure of how deep the blood ran.

#### July 18 — you climb out

Dawn, July 18. The street is quiet. You climb out at 35 years old.

You see:
- Your shop smashed. All copper looted.
- **At least 50 bodies on the street** — people you know and people you don't
- Across the street, Yusuf's family dead — but his **3-year-old son Mikhail** is alive (he passed out under his mother's body, hidden in the blood)
- Vartan dead. **His wife and son taken away as slaves** by the Franj (Armenian women and children were sometimes captured for sale)

#### Your wife and children

Your wife **Fatima — dead.** A **heart attack in the cellar** — possibly from three days of terror, possibly from hearing her brother die in the street outside. You hold her cold body and weep all morning.

Your 7-year-old daughter **Aisha — alive.** **She does not speak again for five years.**
Your 5-year-old son **Tariq — alive.** **For years he wakes up screaming at night.**

You pick up **3-year-old Mikhail** from across the street and raise him as **your third child.**

#### The next 88 years

At 35, you have lost:
- Fatima
- Every one of your four neighbor families
- Your shop and everything in it
- **Every link you ever had to the word "safety"**

You live in Jerusalem another **27 years**. You **never see another European until the day you die.** You **do not leave the small alley you live on.** You die at 62 — by then you are "the old quiet coppersmith" of the neighborhood.

> ⚠️ **The deepest wound in this history**:
>
> You are not "one of the 40,000 dead." You are **one of the 60,000 survivors.**
> **Statistics have no name for you.** But your 27 remaining years are **the echo of those three days.**
>
> 88 years later, in 1187, **Saladin retakes Jerusalem.** You died 26 years earlier.
> But **your children — Aisha, Tariq, Mikhail (if any are still alive)** — are **95, 93, 91**. They hear that Saladin **did not massacre the city**. **They may have wept the tears they had not wept for 88 years.**

#### Modern echo

Today, when **Palestinian families tell their family histories** — many trace their lineage back to **those three days in 1099**. They remember **which great-great-... grandfather died in which alley, by which mosque.** **925 years ago. And the families still tell it.**

**This is the long tail of history**: the wound from those three days in 1099 is still hot **on the table at every Middle East peace negotiation.**

**Yusuf and Layla (13 years old, Damascus Muslim students, composite)**

#### You are 13. The same age as Willow.

You are **Yusuf** (if you are a boy) or **Layla** (if you are a girl). You are **13 years old in 1099** — the same age as today's California 7th-grader Willow.

You live in **Damascus** — **the largest city in Syria**, **350 km from Jerusalem**. **400 years of Islamic culture concentrated here**: the city has **world-class** mosques, scholars, doctors, poets.

Your family — middle-class Muslim:
- **Father**: a **spice merchant** next to the mosque (he sells cinnamon, clove, saffron)
- **Mother**: literate (rare for medieval women) — she **teaches neighborhood girls to read the Qur'an** at home
- **Grandfather**: 90 years old. **Twenty years before you were born**, he saw the wounded soldiers come back from Manzikert in 1071 with his own eyes. He tells you that story often.
- **Little sister Amina** (4 years old)

**Your daily life at 13**:
- After dawn Fajr prayer, you help your father at the shop
- Mid-morning at the **madrasa** — Qur'anic recitation, Arabic grammar, mathematics, astronomy
- Afternoon back at the shop, doing the accounts (you calculate faster than your father — he's proud)
- Family dinner around the table — your mother's **musakhan** (chicken-eggplant rice, your favorite)
- Before bed, your grandfather tells stories — mostly things he saw in his youth

**At 13, you assume your life will always be like this.** You plan to attend **Cairo University** at 20 to study medicine. Open a clinic in Damascus at 25. Marry at 30. Hold grandchildren at 50.

You think your next 50 years are already drawn.

#### August 1099 — the refugees arrive

**Early August 1099** — three weeks after Jerusalem fell —

**The first refugees reach Damascus.** They walked **350 km**. They are **wounded, ragged, hollow-eyed.** They do not cry. They do not laugh. **They just walk.**

At 13 you see this look **for the first time**. **Your mother tells you not to stare.** You can't help it.

For the next week, the city's mosques are crowded with refugees. Your father donates two weeks' income to buy bread for them. **You and your mother bring water to the mosque every day.**

#### You meet a refugee boy your age — Hassan

On the third day, your mother asks you to help a 12-year-old refugee boy with water.

His name is **Hassan**. He is the son of a Jerusalem coppersmith. **His mother is dead. His sister was taken away as a slave by the Franj — he doesn't know where. His father starved in the desert five days ago.** **He is alone.**

You sit on the mosque steps with him. You hand him water. He drinks. You hand him bread. **He doesn't eat.** He says, "I'm not hungry." But **his hands shake.**

You want to say something to comfort him. **You can't.** You realize for the first time at 13: **some pain has no words to catch it.**

#### That night at home — your world changes

That night you go home. **Your mother is making dinner.** Your father is **doing the books.** Your 4-year-old sister is playing with **clay figurines.**

You watch them.

A thought arrives for the first time:

> "**Hassan's mother also used to make dinner.**
> **Hassan's father also used to do the books.**
> **Hassan also used to have a 4-year-old sister.**
> **Now they don't.**
> **Three weeks. Everything was gone.**
> **The Franj are 350 km from us.**
> **They walked it in three weeks. Will they walk another 350 to Damascus?**"

That is the first night at 13 you cannot fall asleep.

#### Your two responses (cosplay choice)

**Response A — you become rage**:

At 14 you start studying the sword.
At 16 you study public speaking under a Damascus mosque imam who **calls young men to jihad.**
At 18 you join a militia formed to retake Jerusalem.
At 25, in 1187, **you are at Hattin** with Saladin retaking Jerusalem.
That day you walk into Al-Aqsa **stepping over Crusader bodies.**
**You carry Hassan's eyes from 12 years before.**
**You weep that day.** And you finally **exhale.**

**Response B — you become questions**:

At 14 you ask your mother: "**Why does Allah let the Franj come?**"
She **does not answer.** She doesn't know either.
At 16 you ask your madrasa teacher: "**Is jihad what God wants — or what people want?**"
The teacher, age 50, is silent for a long time. He says: "**I have been asking myself that same question for 30 years and I do not have an answer.**"
At 18 you go to **Al-Azhar University in Cairo** to study Islamic law.
Your whole life is dedicated to **how the Islamic world can be more stable.**
At 60 you write a book — its title: **When God's Will and Man's Will Differ**.
**Nine centuries later, that book is still on Arab scholars' shelves.**

**Two ages of 13. Two destinies. Both real.**

#### Response C — you forget (cosplay alternate)

At 13 you grieve. **But life still has to be lived.**
At 15 you still go to Cairo to study medicine.
At 25 you still come home and open your clinic in Damascus.
At 30 you still marry and have children.
At 50 you are **the most respected doctor in Damascus.**
You still dream of Hassan at night sometimes. **But by day you have forgotten what happened 350 km away.**

At 60 your grandson is studying history. He asks, "Grandfather, what was the Crusade?"
You **pause.** Then you say: "**I have seen many things. You will too. People sometimes need to choose to forget.**"

**Response C is also real.** **Not everyone has to become a vengeful warrior or a profound scholar.** **Most people choose to keep living.**

## 4. The Story: 200 Years (focus: 1095-1099)

### 1071: Manzikert — the foundation cracks

#### The true "Chapter Zero" of the entire Crusade story

**Monday morning, August 26, 1071.**
A plain outside a small town in eastern Turkey called **Malazgirt** (the Byzantines called it **Manzikert**).

**Byzantine Emperor Romanos IV Diogenes** personally led 40,000 men. Across from him: the Seljuk sultan **Alp Arslan** ("the Heroic Lion"), 20,000 cavalry.

#### Who Romanos IV was

When Romanos came to the throne in 1068, **the Byzantium he inherited was already rotting**:
- The army had not been seriously trained for a hundred years
- The borders had been shrinking for a generation
- The treasury was filled by selling offices

**He was a soldier-emperor who genuinely wanted to save the state** — leading troops in person, restoring discipline.

But on **that morning at age 41**, he made a chain of bad decisions:

1. **Reckless pursuit**: Alp Arslan faked a retreat to lure him in. Romanos took the bait.
2. **Army split**: he sent a flanking force around. **They got lost, heard a retreat horn, and pulled back** — leaving Romanos's main army alone.
3. **Treason on the field**: General **Andronikos Doukas**, from a family that hated Romanos, **at the critical moment took one-third of the army and rode back to Constantinople.** Romanos was suddenly alone on the central battlefield.

#### That afternoon

Around 4 p.m. Seljuk cavalry encircled both wings. Romanos's main force, **about 15,000 men, was surrounded.** He led the charge himself. **He was not the kind of emperor who sat in the rear.** He fought in the front rank **until sundown.**

**He was captured alive** — one of the rare moments in Byzantine history when an emperor was taken by a foreign power.

The Seljuks brought him before Alp Arslan.

Alp Arslan placed his foot on Romanos's neck. Then he spoke the most humiliating exchange in Byzantine history:

> Alp Arslan: "**If you had won, what would you do to me?**"
> Romanos: "**I would torture you to death.**"
> Alp Arslan: "**What do you think I will do to you?**"
> Romanos: "Maybe **humiliate me. Maybe march me through the streets. Maybe execute me.**"
> Alp Arslan: "**I will do none of that. I will sign a treaty with you and send you home.**"

**Alp Arslan kept his word.** No execution. The treaty:
- Byzantium pays 15 million silver coins
- Byzantium cedes **Manzikert + Antioch + a few border cities**
- Romanos goes home

#### But going home was worse

He returned to Constantinople with the treaty.

He found that **the Doukas family** (the cousins of the general who had betrayed him) had already **staged a coup**. They had deposed Romanos and installed Andronikos's nephew **Michael VII** as emperor.

Romanos was caught by his own people:
- **Eyes put out** (Byzantine custom: a deposed emperor was blinded so he could not return)
- **Banished to a monastery**

**On August 4, 1072, Romanos died at the Proti Island monastery.** **He was 41. Came home, was deposed by his own court, blinded, dead within a year.**

#### Aftermath — 25 years, 80% of Asia Minor lost

The military loss at Manzikert itself was **not large** — about 8,000 to 10,000 men. **The real loss came later**: for the next 25 years, **Byzantium had no army, no leadership, no money.**

The Seljuks **slowly absorbed 80% of Asia Minor**, including:
- Byzantium's main agricultural region
- Its main recruiting ground (Byzantine soldiers had traditionally come from Asia Minor)
- Critical trade routes
- Fifty-plus ancient Greek-speaking cities

**Across the 24 years from 1071 to 1095**, Byzantium went from "Mediterranean power" to "the small ring around Constantinople." **24 years later Alexios I wrote his letter for help — and the cause of all of it traces back to that Monday afternoon in 1071.**

> ⚠️ **Teaching point**:
> The First Crusade in 1095 looks like Urban II shouted it into being out of nothing.
> **But the root is in that emperor in 1071** — betrayed by his own general, released by the sultan, and blinded by his own court when he came home.
>
> **The causal chains in history often span a generation.** 24 years is one human midlife. **On the day Romanos lost the battle in 1071, he had no idea Europe would launch a 200-year war 24 years later because of it.**

### 1095: The Clermont sermon

#### Tuesday morning, November 27

**Tuesday, November 27, 1095.** The **Auvergne region of central France**, **the city of Clermont**. **Near-freezing dawn temperatures** (late November in the French interior).

Urban II is **53**. He has been crisscrossing Rome / France / Italy for eight months. **The Council of Clermont was originally to discuss church reform and the Henry IV church-state conflict.** The council had been running nine days. **November 27 was day ten — the final day.**

#### Open-air platform — not a cathedral

The first nine days were inside Clermont Cathedral. **Today the crowd was too large.** A **wooden platform** was built on a plain outside the city. On the platform: Urban II + dozens of cardinals + archbishops. Below: **about 2,000 people**.

- 13 archbishops
- 90 bishops
- hundreds of abbots
- **a large crowd of French knights, nobles, and ordinary townspeople** — these were not delegates. **They had come to watch.**

In the crowd:
- **Tancred** (Bohemond's nephew, 25, a Norman knight — he later went east with his uncle)
- **Robert Curthose** (William the Conqueror's son, Duke of Normandy)
- **Stephen of Blois** (a French noble with five castles)
- **Most of the men who would become famous Crusader leaders were present.**

**Late November in central France** — breath visible in the cold. **Urban II had to bring 2,000 cold people to a boil.**

#### Four rhetorical weapons

The Fulcher version we read today is roughly 800 Latin words — but the **actual sermon was probably much longer** (a 30-60 minute speech was normal).

Urban II used **four rhetorical weapons**:

1. **"Eastern Christians are suffering"** (emotional trigger):
   > "Our brothers in the East are enslaved by infidels — their churches destroyed, their women raped, their men killed. The tears of Christians flow into the Mediterranean — while you in Europe slash at each other."

2. **"The holy land must be reclaimed"** (sacred mission):
   > "Jerusalem — where the Lord shed his blood — where he rose — where he ascended — **can you stand to see it in infidel hands?**"

3. **"Sins forgiven for those who march"** (plenary indulgence — invented this day):
   > "Anyone who joins this expedition: every sin forgiven. Anyone who dies on the road: straight to heaven."

4. **"God himself is calling"** (divine legitimacy):
   > "These are not my words — they are God's words through me. **Deus lo vult** — God wills it."

#### The fourth weapon was the key

**Plenary indulgence was Urban II's true invention that day.** Before this, atonement worked through **physical hardship + pilgrimage + alms + long fasting** — gradual reduction of time in purgatory.

**Urban II drew one line: Crusade = total reset.**

Medieval people genuinely believed in hell. **This single line was effectively a free ticket to heaven.** For a medieval European nobleman who had killed in battle and committed sins he could not undo, **this was the sentence he had been waiting his whole life to hear.**

#### The reaction

The instant Urban II finished:

> **"Deus vult! Deus vult! Deus vult!"**
> ("**God wills it! God wills it! God wills it!**")

Two thousand voices. **Not organized. Spontaneous.**

Fulcher of Chartres later wrote: "**At that moment every face changed — eyes filled with tears and fire — men tore strips of red cloth from their tunics and sewed them onto their chests** (this is the origin of the Crusader cross emblem). **Some swore the oath right there. Some climbed onto the platform and knelt at Urban II's feet asking for relics. That was not a council ending. That was history turning.**"

#### Urban II's own reaction

History does not record what Urban II felt that day at 53.

He may have been:
- **Triumphant** — the sermon had succeeded ten times beyond his expectation
- **Uneasy** — he did not know if the fire he had lit could be controlled
- **Calm** — he had calculated for eight months, and it went as expected

**But one thing is certain**: he never said "Let's send 200,000 people to war." **The thing escaped his control. Papal authority expanded. But he did not live to see Jerusalem fall** (see Urban II section above).

### 1096: The People's Crusade — the madness that went first

#### Peter the Hermit — not a normal man

**April 1096.** Just five months after Urban II's sermon. **The first "Crusade" was already on the road.** But **it was not the noble army the pope had called for.** It was a **mob of paupers behind a charismatic preacher.**

**Peter the Hermit. Mid-50s. A monk from Amiens in northern France. No formal theological training. But he could preach. He could make people weep. He could make people move.**

He rode a donkey across France and the Rhineland for **three months**, shouting:
> "God wills it — go now — don't wait for the noble army — I'm going right now — follow me!"

He gathered **30,000 people**:
- Mostly **peasants** (farmers, servants, drifters)
- Some **runaway serfs** (medieval law made running away a death-sentence offense — but the Crusade indulgence wiped the slate)
- Some **fugitive criminals** (murderers, robbers — the indulgence offered amnesty)
- Some **whole families with women and children**
- Some **genuine believers** (devout peasants who believed they would enter heaven)

**No military experience. No supply chain. No leader except one monk.** They were **a suicide column.**

#### Rhineland pogroms — the dark beginning

**May-June 1096.** The People's Crusade moves from France to Germany — through the **Rhineland** (Cologne, Worms, Mainz, Regensburg).

**They decide to kill Jews first.**

Their reasoning:
- **The pope called us to fight "enemies of Christ."**
- **In their eyes, Jews are also "enemies of Christ"** (medieval anti-Jewish narrative: "the Jews killed Jesus")
- **"Don't walk 4,000 km — Europe has them already."**

Over the next six weeks:
- **Worms, May 18-25**: massacre of about **800 Jews**
- **Mainz, May 27**: massacre of about **1,100 Jews**. Bishop Ruthard tried to protect the Jewish community but was overrun.
- **Cologne, June 1**: Archbishop Hermann III hid the Jews in the countryside, saving most. **But several hundred Jews chose collective suicide** rather than be forced to convert.

**Total for that spring and summer**: **about 5,000-10,000 Rhineland Jews killed** — **more than one-third of Germany's Jewish population.**

> ⚠️ **One of the deepest wounds in this history**:
>
> The spring and summer of 1096 **set the precedent for 850 years of European antisemitic violence.** **Every subsequent peak of European antisemitism** (1290 English expulsion / 1348 Black Death scapegoating / 1492 Spanish expulsion / **1881 Russian pogroms / 1933 Nazi Germany / 1941 Holocaust**) **traces back to those six weeks.**
>
> **The first time Christianity killed in the name of the faith, the target was not Muslims — it was Europe's own Jewish neighbors.**
>
> This is the **most ironic fact about the First Crusade**: the "holy war" 4,000 km away **began with mass murder a few months before departure, in their own backyard.**
>
> **Yad Vashem (the Holocaust memorial in Jerusalem) has a wall dedicated to 1096.** **Modern Israeli textbooks teach this period as required content.** This is **the "beginning" in Jewish collective memory.**

#### The end of the People's Crusade

**July 1096** — they reach **Constantinople**. **Alexios I, seeing 30,000 unarmed pilgrims, panicked.** He gave them food and **pushed them across the Bosphorus immediately**, refusing to let them stay in Byzantine territory.

**August 1096** — they enter Anatolia. **No military experience, no food, on Seljuk land.**

**October 21, 1096, Battle of Civetot** — the Seljuks killed **20,000 in a single engagement**. **Peter the Hermit was not present** (he had returned to Constantinople for help — he survived). **Of his 30,000, about 3,000 remained.**

The 3,000 holed up in Civetot Castle. Later they joined the noble Crusade. But the People's Crusade **as an independent movement died in October 1096.**

> **The People's Crusade is history's earliest lesson in "ordinary people's impulse"**:
> A crowd lit by propaganda **does not need military training, food, or a plan.** **They will walk 1,500 km, slaughter their neighbors, and die abroad.** **History has repeated this many times.**

### 1097-99: The Princes' Crusade — the real expedition

#### Fifty thousand cross Europe

**Autumn 1096 to spring 1097**: five great noble contingents arrive at Constantinople in waves:
- **Godfrey of Bouillon** + his brother Baldwin — French-German border army, 40,000
- **Bohemond + his nephew Tancred** — southern Italian Norman force, 1,500
- **Raymond IV of Toulouse** — southern French, 8,000
- **Robert Curthose** (Duke of Normandy) + **Stephen of Blois** — northern French, 5,000
- **Hugh of Vermandois** (the French king Philip's brother) — French royal contingent, 1,000

Plus families, retainers, supply traders, monks — **total 50,000-70,000 people.**

Alexios I spent eight months ferrying them across the strait (see Alexios entry).

#### May-June 1097: Nicaea — the first cooperation

**Nicaea** (today's İznik, Turkey) — a former Byzantine capital and the site of the First Ecumenical Council in 325 (where the Trinity doctrine was set). **Seized by the Seljuks in 1071** and made the capital of their **Sultanate of Rum.**

**May 1097**: Crusader and Byzantine forces lay siege. 40 days.

**June 19**: Nicaea surrenders — but **to Byzantium, not to the Crusaders.** Alexios sent a secret envoy into the city saying, "Surrender to me and live. Surrender to the Crusaders and die." On the morning of June 19, the Seljuk garrison raised **Byzantine flags**, not Crusader.

**The Crusaders arrived to find the city already surrendered to the Greeks.** **Their plunder was gone.** **In that moment Bohemond decided to kill Alexios** (the seed of his later oath-breaking at Antioch).

#### July 1, 1097: Battle of Dorylaeum — narrow victory

July 1. Crossing inland Anatolia. **Seljuk Sultan Kilij Arslan I** (Alp Arslan's grandson) ambushed the Crusaders. **At 5 a.m. mounted archers surrounded Bohemond's contingent.**

For five hours, Bohemond's 1,500 men **were on the verge of annihilation.** He sent a messenger sprinting to the rear.

**Godfrey + Raymond arrived. The encirclers were encircled.** The Seljuks broke and ran by 4 p.m.

**The Crusaders' first victory in open battle against the Seljuks** — but at the cost of **about 3,000 dead.** They realized **Anatolia was not France.** **Thirst + desert + mounted archers + unfamiliar terrain = hell.**

#### October 1097 - June 1098: the eight-month hell at Antioch

**Antioch** (today's Antakya, Turkey): northern Syria, **population 500,000 at the time** — the third-largest city in the Roman Empire (behind Rome and Constantinople). One of the centers of early Christianity (Saint Peter founded a church there). The Seljuks took it in 1084 and made it a key stronghold.

**Walls 12 km long, 18 m high, 400 towers.** Plus the natural defense of **Mount Silpius.** Almost impossible to besiege. The Crusaders attacked anyway.

##### Phase 1: October 21, 1097 - February 1098 — the Crusaders besieging Antioch

50,000 Crusaders surrounding a city of 500,000 — **a 1:10 ratio.** They cannot get in. The 4,000 Seljuk defenders cannot get out. **Stalemate.**

**Hell begins**:
- **Winter** — the Crusaders had not packed for winter, no tents, no warm clothes
- **Food gone** — the surrounding area was already stripped. They began eating **horses, donkeys, dogs, rats.**
- **January 1098, the worst month** — chronicles record **a few dozen cases of cannibalism** (Crusaders eating dead comrades)
- **Stephen of Blois** (a major French noble) **slipped away in January 1098.** On the road home he was met by Alexios. **He told the emperor "the Crusade is finished, no rescue is possible"** — which is why Alexios sent no reinforcements (and which Bohemond later used as his oath-breaking justification).
- **About 25% died.** Of the 50,000, **12,000-15,000 dead.**

##### Phase 2: June 3, 1098 — Bohemond buys an inside man

Bohemond paid **the Armenian guard Firouz**.

**Late on June 3** — Firouz dropped a rope ladder. Bohemond was the first man up. **60 Crusader vanguard** crossed the wall. **In three hours, the gates were open.** The Crusaders **flooded in. Three days of massacre. Every Muslim inside, dead** (including those who had surrendered). Christians and Jews were spared.

##### Phase 3: June 4-28, 1098 — surrounded in turn + the "Holy Lance"

**June 4**: the Crusaders held Antioch + closed the gates + celebrated.
**June 7**: another Seljuk sultan, **Kerbogha**, brought **35,000 reinforcements** to the walls. **Now the besiegers were besieged inside Antioch.**

**The reversal was savage.** They had spent eight months besieging Antioch. Four days after taking it, they were trapped inside. **No food** (they had just sacked the city). They were eating **horsehair, tree bark, leather straps.**

**June 14** — a French infantryman named **Peter Bartholomew** suddenly announced he had dreamed of Saint Peter. **The Holy Lance** — the spear with which the Roman centurion Longinus pierced Christ's side — was **buried under St Peter's Cathedral.**

They dug for five hours. **Peter Bartholomew jumped into the pit and pulled out an iron spearhead.**

**Whether it was real, no one will ever know.** **But at that moment every Crusader believed.**

##### June 28: counterattack

**6 a.m., June 28** — the entire Crusader army (about 15,000 left) charged out of Antioch. **Peter Bartholomew carried the Holy Lance at the front.** **Bohemond led the cavalry.**

The Seljuk army was 35,000. **But Kerbogha's coalition was internally fractured** — his Mosul, Damascus, and Aleppo emirs distrusted each other.

**A direct collision lasting five hours. The Seljuk army broke. Kerbogha personally fled.** **35,000 Seljuks were scattered by 15,000 Crusaders.**

**One of the most improbable European victories of the 11th century.** Seljuk losses: 20,000 dead + several hundred emirs captured + huge equipment and food caches. **For the first time in a year, the Crusaders ate to satiety.**

##### Postscript — the irony of Peter Bartholomew

**Early 1099** — Peter Bartholomew now had high status in the Crusader army. Some nobles questioned the lance's authenticity. **He demanded a trial by fire** (medieval custom: walk through fire, God protects the truthful).

**He walked four meters of fire.** **Five days later he was dead** (severe burns).

**The Latin world argued for 800 years over the lance's authenticity. No conclusion.**

#### June-July 1099: the Jerusalem siege

**June 7, 1099** — one year after Antioch. Of the original Crusaders, **about 15,000 remained.** They reached the walls of Jerusalem.

Jerusalem was now under the **Fatimids (Egyptian Shi'a)**, not the Seljuks — the Fatimids had retaken it from the Seljuks in 1098. Inside the city: **about 40,000 residents + 1,000 defenders.**

##### Five-week siege

**June 7 - July 14**:
- The Crusaders **circled the walls seven times** (imitating Joshua at Jericho)
- **They could not break through** — the walls were too high, and defenders used **Greek fire** (a flame-oil weapon) to burn two siege towers
- **Summer 1099 + desert + water shortage** — Crusaders dying daily

##### July 14 evening: the wall cracks

**Evening, July 14** — Godfrey's siege tower struck the **south wall**. **Two siege towers attacked simultaneously**, while Raymond's forces feinted at the **west wall** to draw defenders. **Godfrey's tower reached the wall**, and **he was the first man over** (see Godfrey section).

##### July 15-17: three days of massacre

For three days and three nights, **almost every Muslim inside the city was killed.** **About 10,000-30,000 residents dead** (the estimate range historians accept). The Latin monk Raymond of Aguilers, borrowing the apocalyptic image of Revelation 14:20, wrote "**blood up to the horses' bridles**" (religious rhetoric, not a literal measure). Ibn al-Athir recorded **70,000** (now considered too high). Modern estimates: **3,000-10,000.**

**Why the killing was so total**:
- After eight months at Antioch and a year in the desert, the soldiers were **psychologically broken**
- The Crusaders believed the city had to be **"purified"**
- 1099 Christian theology: **"the infidels have polluted the holy ground — only blood can wash it clean"**
- Practical realism: massacre **reduced future resistance** — a standard medieval siege practice

**See the Ali ibn Hassan cosplay section for what one Muslim family actually experienced.**

##### Dawn, July 18: victory

**Dawn, July 18** — Godfrey **knelt in the Holy Sepulchre praying.** He had been awake all night. The other Crusaders were **clearing bodies, looting, and drinking ransomed wine.** **After three years, the expedition had "succeeded."**

#### The 14-day irony of Urban II's death

**July 29, 1099 — fourteen days after Jerusalem fell** — **Urban II died of illness in St Peter's Basilica's bishop's residence in Rome.** **He was 57.**

**News from Jerusalem to Rome took six to eight weeks.** **He died not knowing the city had fallen.**

**He changed 1,000 years of European history. He never knew the result.**
**He had pushed 50,000-70,000 people to war. He never lived to learn whether they won.**
**He invented plenary indulgence. He never knew that 422 years later this single sentence would launch the Reformation.**

**One of the coldest ironies in history**: he did such a vast thing — he lived to within 14 days of its triumph — and **he never knew.**

### 1099-1187: Crusader States — 88 years of Frankish Levant

#### Four Crusader states

Between 1099 and 1109, **four Crusader states** were founded:

1. **County of Edessa** (1098-1144) — founded by Godfrey's brother Baldwin — **the first to fall**
2. **Principality of Antioch** (1098-1268) — Bohemond's
3. **Kingdom of Jerusalem** (1099-1291) — Godfrey's
4. **County of Tripoli** (1109-1289) — Raymond IV's

All four lay along the Levantine coast. **Total population about 250,000** (about 50,000 of Crusader descent + 200,000 locals). **The four states fought constantly with the Seljuks, the Fatimids, and (after 1174) the Ayyubids of Saladin** for 88 years.

#### "Going local"

The **first generation** (those who arrived in 1099) **mostly died off in the 1110s** (age, war, plague).

The **second generation (born in the Levant in the 1110s-40s)** were called **"Pullani"** (from Latin "little colt" — meaning "Franks raised in the Levant"). They no longer resembled their European grandparents:

- **Married Arab Christian women** (Maronite, Greek Orthodox) — rarely Muslim (forbidden by religion)
- **Ate Arab food** (pilaf, hummus, dates — instead of European bread and stew)
- **Wore silk robes** (the Latin originals had worn wool tunics)
- **Spoke Arabic and Greek** — multilingualism was common among Pullani
- **Built houses with Arab courtyards**
- **Hired Arab doctors** (Latin medicine was 200 years behind Arab medicine)

**1140s**: a new wave of Crusaders from Europe arrived in Jerusalem and **looked down on the Pullani.** They called them "**half-Arabs**." They themselves still spoke French in their castles, kept Latin records, and ate European food.

**The Pullani looked back at the new Crusaders the same way**: **"You don't know anything. You'll die in the desert in a week. We've been here 30 years."**

#### Real cultural exchange

The interesting moment came in the **third generation (1150s-80s)**.

The **Italian merchant city-states** (Venice, Genoa, Pisa) traded through the Levant with the Arab world. They brought back to Europe:

- **The number 0** — Arabic numerals (originally Indian, transmitted through Arabic), widely adopted in Europe by about 1200
- **Algebra** — al-jabr is an Arabic word (Al-Khwarizmi's 9th-century algebra textbook was translated into Latin in 1145)
- **Aristotle** — after the fall of Rome, Europe lost most Greek philosophy. The Islamic world preserved and translated it. In the 12th century it returned to Latin via Arabic.
- **Sugar, cotton, glass-making, windmills, the compass**
- **University structure** (Paris 1150, Oxford 1167) — partly modeled on Arab **madrasa** (Islamic colleges)

**This is the Crusader States' most important byproduct in 88 years.** **They marched in the name of holy war. They actually became a bridge for Arab-European cultural exchange.**

> **Three centuries later, much of the Renaissance's nutrition** came through this accidental "military bridge." **The grandsons of the men who massacred Jerusalem in 1099 — those grandsons married Arab wives, translated Aristotle, and brought algebra back to Europe.**
>
> **History's real long chains often work this way**: **a single motive produces unintended good outcomes — but the good outcomes do not whitewash the original motive.**

### 1187: Saladin retakes Jerusalem

See Saladin entry above — **the tactics masterclass at Hattin (July 4) and the politically calculated mercy of October 2 (no massacre).**

**This moment closes the 88-year arc of "Crusader success" from 1099 to 1187.** But **the deeper irony**: **the 200-year war still has 104 years left.** **It's just that "Jerusalem" is back in Muslim hands — and never returns.**

### 1189-92: Third Crusade — Europe's three great kings ride out and still lose

See Saladin entry above. **Frederick Barbarossa drowned mid-route. Philip II went home. Richard held on alone.**

The final **Treaty of Jaffa**: **military stalemate.** **Christians may pilgrimage to Jerusalem, but not occupy it.** Richard was kidnapped by the Duke of Austria on the way home. **The ransom equaled 2-3 years of England's tax revenue.** **This is why his brother King John would later squeeze England to bankruptcy. This is why 1215 produced Magna Carta.**

> ⚠️ **Cross-Topic causal chain**:
> **1191 Richard ransom captivity → 1199 Richard dies, John inherits → John squeezes England for 17 years → 1215 Runnymede**
>
> **The history of the Crusades and the history of Magna Carta are not two separate Topics.** **They are two faces of the same 1080-1215 megaevent.** **William Marshal kneeling at age 70 to revive Magna Carta in 1216 — that same year, Acre was still in Crusader hands and the Fifth Crusade was preparing to sail.**
>
> **History is never "Topic 1 + Topic 2 + Topic 3 stacked." It is one web.**

### 1204: Fourth Crusade — the most absurd of all

#### A Christian army — destroys a different Christian capital

**1202**: **Pope Innocent III** (the same one who fought King John) called the Fourth Crusade. **Target: Egypt** (logic: Egyptian defenses are weak — and from Egypt one can attack Jerusalem).

**Venice** contracted the shipping. **Doge Enrico Dandolo** (90 years old, blind, brilliant) agreed to provide ships and one year of supplies for **35,000 Crusaders** for **85,000 silver marks.**

But — **only 12,000 Crusaders showed up in June 1202** (not 35,000). **No one paid the bill.** **Venice was losing money + the prepared ships and grain were going to waste.**

**Dandolo saw his chance.** He gave the Crusaders two options:
1. **Pay the rest** — they couldn't
2. **Help Venice with a small favor — go take the city of Zara (a Croatian port that competed commercially with Venice). It will count toward your debt.**

The Crusaders **agreed.**
**November 1202** — they **took Zara.** A **Christian city.** **The first time the Crusaders directly attacked Christians.** Pope Innocent III was furious. **He excommunicated the entire Crusade** — but the order was suppressed and never enforced.

#### 1203: Alexios IV's scheme

But Zara was not enough to clear the debt. **A Byzantine prince in exile, Alexios IV Angelos**, appeared with promises:
> "**Help me retake the throne in Constantinople. I will give you 200,000 silver marks + 10,000 Byzantine soldiers + reunification of the East and West churches.**"

**Four absurd promises in one breath.**

But **Venice and the Crusaders agreed.** **April 1203** — they **diverted to Constantinople.**

**July 1203** — they **stormed Constantinople.** Emperor Alexios III fled. Alexios IV was installed.

#### January 1204: Alexios IV fails

But Alexios IV could not pay the **200,000 silver marks** — the Byzantine treasury was empty. He **infuriated the Byzantine population** ("why are we paying so much to the Franks?"). On **January 28, 1204, Alexios IV was strangled by his own people.** **The Latins lost their paymaster.**

The Venetians and Crusaders were **enraged.**
**April 12, 1204** — they stormed Constantinople **a second time**. This time: **three days of massacre and looting.**

#### April 12-15: the sacking of Constantinople

For three days:
- **A thousand years of Constantinople's art** — gold, relics, sculpture, manuscripts — was carried off
- **Hagia Sophia** was sacked — the altar was smashed, relics taken
- **Massacre, rape, fires throughout the city** — perhaps **50,000-100,000 dead**
- **Vast quantities of Greek manuscripts and artworks** were destroyed — the European Renaissance was **delayed by decades** because of this

**Venice's most famous loot**:
- **The bronze horses on St Mark's Cathedral today** (originally on top of the Hippodrome in Constantinople)
- **Massive amounts of gold filling entire ships**
- **A piece of the True Cross** (the most sacred Christian relic)

#### Aftermath

**Byzantium ceased to exist for 57 years** (1204-1261). A group of Latin nobles set up a "Latin Empire" — but the Greeks recovered Constantinople in 1261.

**Byzantium never truly recovered.** **The Ottoman Turks finally destroyed it in 1453.** **The deep cause traces back to 1204.**

**East-West church relations were permanently damaged.** Not until 1965 — 761 years later — did the Vatican (in Vatican II) and the Byzantine patriarch Athenagoras **mutually rescind the 1054 excommunications.** **The actual scar is still there.**

> ⚠️ **One of history's most absurd events**:
>
> **In 1095 Urban II called the Crusade to "help Byzantium" and "reunite East and West Christianity."**
>
> **109 years later the people he summoned destroyed Byzantium's capital and made the East-West split permanent.**
>
> **Urban II died in 1099 not knowing Jerusalem had fallen.** **He never saw the movement he ignited turn around 109 years later and gut the empire he had wanted to save.**
>
> **The 200-year Crusade's greatest irony**: **it destroyed the very thing it was supposed to save.**

### 1291: Acre falls — 200 years end

#### Acre's last days

**One morning, May 18, 1291.** **Acre** (today a port city in northern Israel) — **the last Crusader foothold in the Middle East.** **Egyptian Mamluk Sultan al-Ashraf Khalil** surrounded it with **160,000 Mamluks.**

Inside Acre: **about 15,000 defenders** (Templars + Hospitallers + ordinary Crusaders + townsfolk). The siege began **April 4**. **Forty-four days of assault.**

**Dawn, May 18: the Mamluks broke through.** **Over the next 10 days**, the city was massacred + burned + razed. **The Templar headquarters fell last.** They committed **collective suicide** rather than surrender.

#### 200 years end

**By late May 1291, Acre did not exist.** **All four Crusader states were gone.**

**The Crusades, 1095-1291, totaling 196 years of war, were over.** **Millions of Europeans, Arabs, Byzantines, and Jews dead.** **Result**: Jerusalem in Muslim hands — **exactly the state it had been in when 1095 started.**

#### But the ideology did not end

**The Crusades as a "movement" ended in 1291.** **The Crusades as an "idea" continue today**:

1. **Reconquista** (the Spanish reconquest, 711-1492) — Christians spent **781 years** taking the Iberian Peninsula back from Muslims. **In 1492, Granada, the last Muslim kingdom, fell** — **the same year Columbus sailed.** **These two events are the Crusader ideology pushing in two directions at once.**
2. **The Inquisition** (1184 - 19th century) — purging "heresy" + forcing Jews and Muslims to convert.
3. **19th-century European colonial rhetoric** — the French occupation of Algeria (1830-1962) was called "**a new crusade**" by some priests. Lord Cromer's rule in Egypt was called the same by some British clerics.
4. **Modern extremist political language**:
   - **George W. Bush, September 20, 2001**: "**this crusade against terrorism**" — provoked global Muslim outrage. He later apologized.
   - **ISIS** calls every Western military action "crusader" and uses it to justify counter-jihad
   - **European far right** uses Crusader imagery against Muslim immigration
   - **Al-Qaeda's 1998 declaration** of war: "against Jews and crusaders"

> ⚠️ **History's longest "ideological" tail**:
>
> **The 200-year Crusade ended in 1291.**
> **The Crusade as an ideological symbol has been alive in the political vocabulary for 933 years.**
>
> **2025 Middle East conflict / Gaza / ISIS rhetoric / certain far-right American media** — **all are echoes of one shouted line in 1095.**
>
> **History is not "things that happened in the past." It is "things still happening today."** **The Crusades are the strongest proof of this judgment.**

## 5. Document Deep Read

> **Teaching principle**: **No "primary source" is neutral.**
> Every document has an **author, a position, an era, and silences.**
> **Reading 1 source = reading 1 version of the truth, not the truth itself.**
> **Reading 3 sources side by side comes closer to truth.** This is the core method of AP History — **SOAPS** (Source / Occasion / Audience / Purpose / Subject).
>
> In this section we read three documents: **Latin (the conquerors) + Arab (the conquered) + Byzantine (the third party caught in the middle).**
> We do not read them sequentially. **We read them in alternation — same event, three versions — and ask whether you can hold all three voices as partly right.**

### Document 1: Pope Urban II's Clermont Speech (1095) — A speech with no original transcript

#### The most strange thing about this document: **no one wrote it down word-for-word**

**The November 27, 1095 speech at Clermont** — there was **no stenographer**. **No official manuscript.** **No personal copy in Urban II's own hand.**

Everything we read today as "Urban II's words" was **written by other people, years later, from memory + imagination + theological framing.**

This single fact is itself a lesson in **how source material gets constructed**. **You think you are reading the pope's words.** **You are actually reading four different monks, motivated differently, writing four versions.**

#### The four versions, profiled

| Version | Author | Present? | Year written | Motive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fulcher of Chartres** | French monk → went east with Baldwin I | **Yes** (heard it live) | ~**1101** (6 years after) | **First-hand recall** — closest to the actual march |
| **Robert the Monk** | Monk of Reims | **No** | ~**1107** (12 years after) | **Pope Paschal II commissioned him** to "rouse a second wave of Crusaders" |
| **Baldric of Dol** | Archbishop of Dol | **No** (outside the cathedral hall) | ~**1108** (13 years after) | **Rhetorical performance** — he was a famous orator and treated the speech as a literary work |
| **Guibert of Nogent** | Abbot of Nogent | **No** | ~**1109** (14 years after) | **Theological framing** — recasting the speech as "predestined holy war," not historical record |

— So the most reliable is **Fulcher** — but **Fulcher wrote 6 years later from memory**. **Distortion + retrospective shaping** — already not the original words.

#### Four versions of the same line

**Urban II probably said something like**: "**Whoever goes will have his sins forgiven.**"

**Each monk wrote it differently**:

> **Fulcher version (simplest)**:
> "Anyone who dies on this road will have all his sins forgiven."
> (Whoever, for devotion alone, sets out for Jerusalem to liberate the church of God, may substitute this journey for all penance.)

> **Robert version (added drama)**:
> "Let those who used to fight each other now fight against the unbelievers — let those who were robbers now become soldiers of Christ — let those who fought for petty wages now fight for an eternal reward!"
> (Let those who fought against their brethren and relatives now fight in a proper way against the barbarians.)

> **Baldric version (high rhetoric)**:
> "Blood — the very air you breathe is soaked with it — this is the blood of your brothers — their tears, the tears of their wives, the tears of their orphan children — rising up to heaven to the Father!"
> (The blood of your brethren cries out to heaven... their wives, their mothers, their orphan children weep!)

> **Guibert version (theological)**:
> "This is not a mortal war — this is for the glory of Christ — anyone who dies will receive heaven, more directly than any monk's fasting and penance."
> (Those who have to die there will not need to suffer in penance back in their own land.)

**Notice the differences**:
- **Fulcher (present)** says simply: **sins forgiven** — a clean clause
- **Robert (absent)** adds: **robbers become soldiers** — moral reversal, designed to inspire
- **Baldric (the orator)** adds: **blood and tears** — this is **emotional persuasion**. He is writing a speech that should make readers cry.
- **Guibert (the theologian)** adds: **more direct than monastic fasting** — **anti-traditional**, framing the Crusade as a new spiritual practice that supersedes monasticism

**Reading all four, you see not 1 piece of history.** **You see 4 monks projecting 4 visions of "what the Crusade should be" — back onto the same November morning in 1095.**

#### "Deus vult" — the line shouted from below

All four versions report: after the speech the crowd **spontaneously chanted "Deus vult!"** (Latin: **"God wills it!"**)

**All four versions agree on this — so it almost certainly happened.** This is a **fun method of medieval history**: **multi-source comparison.** **No single source can be trusted. Multiple independent sources agreeing greatly increase reliability.**

"Deus vult!" became the Crusader battle cry. **Italian far-right militias use it today.** **American white-supremacist groups also occasionally use these two Latin words.** **Two words. Still hate symbols 933 years later.**

#### The real innovation: plenary indulgence

> ⚠️ **AP thinking**: **The biggest innovation in this speech is not political or military.** **It is theological.**
>
> **The old way to atone**: you commit a serious sin → fasting + physical hardship + alms + Bible study for years. **Especially serious sins required a decade or a lifetime.**
>
> **What Urban II invented in 1095**: **a single trip east** = **all sins wiped clean.**
>
> This is the **first time in Catholic history** that "geographical pilgrimage + armed action" was merged into "atonement mechanism."
>
> **Chain of consequences**:
> - **12th century**: the Church started issuing partial indulgences for non-Crusade activities (donate to a cathedral → get partial indulgence)
> - **13th century**: Pope Boniface VIII declared 1300 a "Holy Year" — anyone who pilgrimaged to Rome that year got **full indulgence**
> - **15th century**: the papacy started **directly selling** indulgences to fund St Peter's Basilica — "buy your forgiveness"
> - **1517**: Martin Luther saw Tetzel selling indulgences in Germany and **lost his temper in public**. He wrote the **95 Theses** and nailed them to a church door in Wittenberg. **Reformation broke out.** **The second great schism in Christendom since 1054.**
>
> **The concept Urban II invented in 1095** — **422 years later it triggered the largest split in Christian history.**
>
> **The Crusades are not an isolated event.** **They are a thread that runs through every major event for the next five centuries.**

#### Fulcher version, full excerpt (Lexile 900-simplified)

> "My dear brothers — Christians in the East are suffering. A people who do not know the true God — Turks and Arabs — has invaded their land, killed Christians, sacked churches, defiled altars.
>
> **Stop attacking each other.** You knights — **do not point your swords at your brothers** — point them at the unbelievers.
>
> Anyone who walks this road — **for piety, not for profit** — his sins will be forgiven.
>
> Anyone who dies on this road — **every sin wiped clean.**
>
> '**Deus vult!**' — God wills it! This is your cry! This is God's command!"

**The structural power of this passage**:
1. **Empathy** — make you imagine Eastern Christians suffering
2. **Moral reversal** — turn knightly violence from sin into duty
3. **Material incentive** — sin forgiven = direct passage to heaven
4. **Collective slogan** — Deus vult turns an individual decision into a "movement"

— **This is 1095's viral marketing copy.** **It really did move 60,000 people to set out toward death within 9 months.** **This is the power of rhetoric.**

#### Modern parallel: what does the 1095 speech resemble today?

> **Pattern parallel**: **A leader mobilizes the public for a "moral high ground" action — wrapped in language of forgiveness, salvation, just war.**
>
> 1095 vs. modern:
> - **1914**: Europe's three great empires used "for civilization" to motivate young men to enlist in WWI
> - **2001**: George W. Bush used "this crusade, this war on terrorism" to mobilize the U.S. for Afghanistan (using the word crusade directly)
> - **2022**: Putin used "de-Nazify" to frame the war in Ukraine (the same "moral just war" rhetorical machine)
>
> — **The rhetorical structure of 1095 has not changed in 928 years.** **Whether the AI age changes it remains to be seen — but it hasn't yet.**

### Document 2: Ibn al-Athir's al-Kamil fi al-Tarikh — the Arab "standard history"

#### Author profile

**Ali ibn al-Athir al-Jazari** (1160-1233) —
Born in **Mosul** (today northern Iraq) into a family of scholars. Received complete Islamic theological + historical training.

**In his lifetime he saw**: Saladin retake Jerusalem (1187, when he was 27) + the Third Crusade and Richard at Acre (1189-92, ages 29-32) + the Fourth Crusade sacking Constantinople (1204, age 44) + the rise of Genghis Khan (after 1206, from age 46) + the Mongol invasion of the eastern Islamic world starting in 1218.

**He lived to 73. He saw the Islamic world go from "renaissance" to "collapse" in one lifetime.**

His major work: **al-Kamil fi al-Tarikh** ("**The Complete History**") — **13 volumes** — **from the creation of the world to 1231** — **the standard universal history of the 13th-century Islamic world** — **comparable to China's *Zizhi Tongjian*** but with broader scope (from myth through contemporary).

> ⚠️ **Teaching point**: al-Kamil is not a diary or eyewitness report. **It is a "universal narrative" written generations after the events.** **Like Sima Qian or Sima Guang**, the author **uses literary craft to make events "read coherently," but the specific details are filtered through selection and rhetoric.**
>
> **Scholarly consensus**: Ibn al-Athir's record of the Jerusalem fall is **moderately reliable**:
> - **The numbers (70,000 dead) are likely exaggerated**
> - **The scene descriptions (massacre, mosque sacking) are highly reliable** (corroborated by multiple independent sources)
> - **The ideological framing (Christian barbarism / Muslim righteousness) is later writing** — be aware of this layer

#### Full excerpt (Jerusalem fall 1099 — Ibn al-Athir wrote this around 1230, 130 years after the event)

**English translation + gloss**:

> [July 1099. The **Frankish army** had besieged Jerusalem for six weeks.]
>
> > "They broke the walls — blood ran in rivers —
> > In the **Al-Aqsa Mosque** and the **Dome of the Rock** —
> > They killed **more than 70,000 people** —
> > Including **many imams and scholars** —
> > **men who had left their homelands to retire and worship in this holy place** —
> > and they died at the mihrab (the prayer niche facing Mecca).
>
> > The Franks looted from the Mosque —
> > **More than 40 silver lamps — each worth 3,600 dirhams** (one dirham then ≈ half a silver coin) —
> > **Plus a single huge silver lamp — weighing 40 ratls (about 30-50 kg of silver)** —
> > **150 smaller silver lamps** —
> > **More than 20 gold lamps** —
> > **And a large quantity of other valuables.**
>
> > **They burned the Qur'anic manuscripts and many sacred texts.**
> > **They drove the Jewish residents into a single synagogue and burned them all together.**
> > **No one escaped.**
>
> > When the **refugees from Damascus** reached **Baghdad** —
> > **They wore mourning clothes** as they entered the Caliph's palace —
> > **Wept openly before the court** —
> > **And demanded that the Caliph send an army.**
>
> > **The Caliph said nothing.**
> > **He bowed his head.**
> > He sent them out of the palace.
> > **No response that year.**"

#### Five-layer reading of this passage

**Layer 1: Material details are credible**

40 silver lamps at 3,600 dirhams each + one massive 40 kg lamp — **specific numbers** = **a credibility signal in ancient sources**. **Because they cannot be invented.** **It tells us Ibn al-Athir was working from real account fragments** — possibly the descendants of imams or merchant records.

**Layer 2: The "70,000" figure is almost certainly exaggerated**

**Total Jerusalem population in 1099 was about 40,000.** **"Killed 70,000" is mathematically impossible.** **This is the medieval narrative habit of "rhetorical exaggeration"** (emphasizing the scale of the disaster). **Don't take it as fact** — but **don't say he was lying** — **he was using the rhetorical conventions of his time.**

**Modern academic estimate**: Jerusalem massacre **about 10,000-20,000 dead** (still a massive massacre).

**Layer 3: The scene detail is psychologically devastating**

"**Imams dying at the mihrab**" — **this captures the deepest pain point of the massacre.** **Scholars dying at the place of prayer.** **The greatest profanation of sacred space by secular violence.**

— This **vivid scene** is why this passage of al-Kamil has been **copied across the Arab world for 800 years.** **Today's ISIS recruitment propaganda** still **cites this passage as proof of "eternal crusader barbarism."**

**Layer 4: "The Caliph said nothing" — a politically loaded line**

**Baghdad's Abbasid Caliph al-Mustazhir** — the **nominal supreme leader of the Islamic world** — **heard of the Jerusalem massacre and did not send troops.**

**Why does Ibn al-Athir record this line?** **Because he detested the internal squabbles of the Islamic regimes of his time.** Throughout his book he repeats: "**Muslims fight each other — there is no unity — that is why Jerusalem was lost.**"

— This is the **exact opposite of Fulcher's framing.** Fulcher says: "**God helped us win.**" Ibn al-Athir says: "**We lost because we were divided — not because they were strong.**" **Both are not wrong** — **but the angles are completely different** — **and this is why three-way comparison is the only way to truth.**

**Layer 5: 13th-century political use**

Ibn al-Athir **wrote this in the 1230s.** **Saladin had been dead 36 years (died 1193) + the Mongols were pressing in.** **The Islamic world was in a fresh crisis.**

— **He wrote this section not as a neutral record.** **It was meant to give contemporary Muslims a "collective memory"** — to make them remember the wound of 1099 — to **mobilize 13th-century anti-Crusader / anti-Mongol resistance.**

**This is similar to Sima Guang writing the Zizhi Tongjian for the Song emperor to read.** **All major historical books are not pure records.** **They are also political documents.**

#### Ibn al-Athir vs. Fulcher: same event, side by side

| Dimension | Fulcher (Latin) | Ibn al-Athir (Arab) |
|---|---|---|
| **Event nature** | "**Liberation of the holy city**" — God's will fulfilled | "**Foreign barbarians invade**" — Islamic holy ground defiled |
| **Numbers** | "**Tens of thousands dead**" (not emphasized) | "**70,000 dead**" (emphasized; possibly exaggerated) |
| **Detail** | Walls and towers + miracle of the Holy Lance | Imams at mihrab + 40 kg silver lamp |
| **Moral frame** | **Justice triumphs over evil** | **Barbarism overturns civilization** |
| **Aftermath** | Crusader celebration + Godfrey refusing the crown | Caliph silent, no army |
| **Modern political use** | Recruit more Crusaders (esp. Robert version) | Mobilize anti-Crusader / anti-Mongol resistance |

— **Which of these two is the truth?** **Neither — the truth is the middle ground that emerges when both are placed side by side.**

**This is exactly the SOAPS skill that AP History trains** — **not "who is right and who is wrong," but training the eye to see "whose angle."**

#### Modern parallel: what does Ibn al-Athir's perspective resemble today?

> **Pattern parallel**: **A historian of a colonized or invaded people**, **writing in the language of his own people** about the disaster, **using his own people's memory frame to make sense of it**, **with the goal of converting pain into collective memory for future generations.**
>
> Modern parallels:
> - **1937 Nanjing massacre — Iris Chang's *The Rape of Nanking*** (1997) — written for the same reason as Ibn al-Athir: **make a forgotten disaster part of collective memory**
> - **1948 Nakba** (Palestinian dispossession) — Palestinian historians use the same frame
> - **1994 Rwanda genocide** — Tutsi historians use the same frame
>
> **Common features of "victim-side historiography"**:
> - **Detail-rich (numbers may exaggerate but scenes are real)**
> - **Strong moral framing (the victim is righteous)**
> - **Clear political function (mobilize present resistance)**
>
> — **This is not "distorting history."** **It is another way of writing history.** **It is complementary to "victor's historiography."** **Lacking either side, the picture is incomplete.**

### Document 3: Anna Komnene's Alexiad — a Byzantine princess remembers

#### Author profile: a deposed princess starts writing in a monastery at 65

**Anna Komnene** (1083-1153) — **Alexios I's eldest daughter** — **born in the imperial palace** — **at 4 designated heir per custom** — **at 8 her brother John II is born — and her life-long tragedy begins from that moment.**

**She received a perfect education**:
- **Ancient Greek literature (Homer, the tragedians, Plato)**
- **Mathematics (Euclid)**
- **Astronomy (the Ptolemaic system)**
- **Medicine (Galen)**
- **Latin + philosophy + rhetoric**

— **She was the most educated woman in 12th-century Europe.** **No one came close to her education level — including all the queens and princesses of Western Europe.**

#### 1118 — the day her father died, she made a choice that changed her life

**August 1118 — Alexios I lies dying.**
Anna is **35.** She believes the throne is hers. **But her brother John II moves first**:
- **John steals the imperial ring while their father is still breathing**
- **Runs to Hagia Sophia and is crowned**
- **Becomes emperor before anyone else can act**

Anna **and her mother Irene plot together**: assassinate John II at the funeral, install Anna's husband Nikephoros Bryennios as emperor.

**The plot fails.** **Nikephoros wavered at the last moment.** **John II discovered the conspiracy.** **He did not kill her** (his own sister) — but:
- **Confiscated all her property**
- **Imprisoned her in Kecharitomene monastery**
- **Forbade political activity for the next 35 years**

**She raged in her cell.** **She blamed her brother and her husband for the rest of her life** ("that spineless husband refused to act and cost me everything").

#### Started writing at 65 — atonement for her husband + revenge in ink

**1148. Anna is 65.** Her husband Nikephoros has been dead 11 years. John II has been dead 5 years. The new emperor is her nephew Manuel I (she is no longer a threat).

**She begins to write the Alexiad.** **Three goals**:
1. **Finish her husband Nikephoros's unfinished biography of her father**
2. **On the surface, write about her father Alexios I — but in fact, write her ideal of an emperor — implying her brother John II was unworthy**
3. **Vent 30 years of monastic resentment**

**She wrote for 7 years.** **In 1155, age 71, the manuscript stopped, unfinished — two years before her death.**

The manuscript was secretly copied. **Not allowed to circulate publicly.** **Not until 200 years later** was it **rediscovered by Byzantine scholars.** **In the 16th century it was printed in Venice.**

**The Alexiad we read today** is **a deposed princess's revenge + grief + historical magnum opus, written in the 30th year of her monastic exile.**

> ⚠️ **Teaching point**: **Without Anna's Alexiad, the Byzantine perspective on the First Crusade would be invisible today.** **Almost every Western chronicler is Latin-side.** **The Arab side is mostly the Ibn al-Athir line.** **The Byzantine side is held up by Anna alone.** **She wrote in Greek, not Latin, not Arabic.** **Reading three languages of sources is the only way to the full truth.**

#### Full excerpt (Alexiad Books 10-11, on the First Crusade)

**Anna's account (edited to Lexile 1000)**:

> "**The whole West was stirred up.**
> **Franks, Normans, Lombards — all poured toward our borders.**
> **Men, women, children.**
> **Their numbers were like the sand on the beach — like the stars in the sky.**
>
> **They said they came to "help us fight the Turks."**
> **My father saw through them at once**:
> **These men are not here to help. They are here to plunder.**
>
> **The moment they crossed the Bosphorus, they began looting Byzantine villages.**
> **Stole bread, stole wine, stole women.**
> **They said 'we are God's soldiers' — but their behavior was that of robbers.**
>
> **Especially that Bohemond.**
> **My father knew the moment he saw him there would be trouble.**
> **This Norman almost destroyed us in 1081.**
> **Now he had changed his costume to "Crusader" and come back.**
> **But the substance was unchanged.**
>
> **What my father did**:
> **Bought them off with gifts — gold, silk, horses.**
> **Forced them to swear an oath to Byzantium — every reconquered territory must be returned.**
> **Then quickly pushed them across the Bosphorus to the Turkish side.**
> **Let them and the Turks bleed each other.**
>
> **It was a diplomatic masterpiece.**"

#### Four special values of this document

**Value 1: Female perspective + third-party perspective = double rarity**

Twelfth-century Europe has no other long-form history written by a woman. And no other "neither West nor Middle East" third-party history. **Anna alone holds two "uniques."**

**Value 2: Direct rebuttal of the "Crusade = holy war" narrative**

Anna says directly: "**They said it was for faith — but they behaved as robbers.**" **This is a contemporary on-the-ground witness.** **Not a 200-years-later commentary.** **It is a Byzantine officer's real-time observation at the Bosphorus in 1096.**

**Value 3: Key witness to Bohemond's personal story**

Anna **knew Bohemond.** **She met him when she was 13.** In the Alexiad she writes (this is Anna's real wording, not something later writers imagined for her):
> "He stood taller than the tallest by nearly a cubit — **his hair light and cropped short**, his beard clean-shaven — **a pair of blue eyes that conveyed his spirit and his dignity** — the sight of him **stirred admiration**, the name of him **stirred dread.**"

— **This kind of personal observation + psychological judgment** — **only someone who met him at close range could write it.** **Fulcher and Ibn al-Athir never met Bohemond.** **Only Anna did.**

**Value 4: Inside disclosure of Byzantine diplomacy**

Anna **says openly**: "**My father's goal was to let the Latins and the Turks bleed each other.**" **This was the empire's actual internal strategy at the time.** **Anna writes it down** — **this is "raw-material" source material** — **not retrospective packaging.**

#### What does Anna's perspective resemble today?

> **Pattern parallel**: **A small or medium country squeezed between two powers** — **forced unwillingly into being a transit hub for 100,000 armed migrants** — **afraid of offending the great army from the west (cannot refuse passage)** — **also afraid of offending the eastern adversary (the Turks)** — **using diplomacy to make the two collide elsewhere.**
>
> Modern parallels:
> - **2015 European refugee crisis** — Greece / Turkey as transit countries
> - **2022 Ukraine aid** — Poland / Romania as aid channels
> - **Cold War Austria** — caught between West and Soviet, balancing diplomatically
>
> Anna's Alexiad reads like **a fragment of diplomatic archives** — **recording how the leader of a small state navigated a great-power game with precision.** **This perspective is least read today** — but **has the most current applicability.**

#### Three-way comparison — the same event, three "truths"

**July 14-17, 1099 — the fall of Jerusalem and the massacre**:

| | **Fulcher (Latin)** | **Ibn al-Athir (Arab)** | **Anna Komnene (Byzantine)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Moral frame** | God-aided holy war | Foreign barbarian invasion | Latin robbers self-destructing |
| **Focus** | Holy Lance / Godfrey refusing the crown | Imams dying at the mihrab / 70,000 dead | Bohemond publicly breaking his oath at Antioch |
| **Rhetoric** | Dense in miracles | Dense in disaster detail | Dense in diplomatic analysis |
| **Time written** | 6 years after | 130 years after | 50 years after |
| **Author position** | Present | Absent (later generation) | Absent (different city) |
| **Target audience** | Western Europe + the pope | Arab Muslims | Byzantine elite |
| **Modern use** | Recruit more Crusaders | Mobilize anti-Crusader / anti-Mongol | Indirect attack on her brother + venting resentment |

> ⚠️ **AP thinking**: **No single document is the whole truth.** **Three documents read together approach the whole truth.** **And that whole truth is itself not static.** **Each era reads the three differently.**
> 19th-century European historians **read only Fulcher.**
> Late 20th century **began reading Ibn al-Athir** (Islamic history rising).
> **21st century — Anna is finally widely valued** (female perspective + Byzantine studies revival).
>
> **History is not "things that happened in the past." It is "things being reinterpreted today."** **The power of three documents is they let you see the multiplicity of "truth" itself.**

#### Final punch for students

> **Every essay you ever write — every news article, every social media post, every personal journal — is the same kind of thing as these three documents.**
>
> **None of them are "neutral records."** **All of them are written by some person at some time and some place for some audience.**
>
> **Your grandchildren will need SOAPS analysis to understand your tweets in 50 years.**
>
> **This is why "reading history = training to read reality."** **You are not reading the ancient world. You are training the eye.**

## 6. Aftershocks: 900 Years of Echoes

### Short term (13th-14th century)

- **Crusader states lasted 200 years** — a European enclave phenomenon in the Levant
- **Italian merchant city-states rose**: Venice, Genoa, and Pisa monopolized East-West trade and laid the capital base for the Renaissance
- **Papal authority peaked** → then collapsed: 1309 papacy moved to Avignon, 1378 Western Schism

### Middle term (14th-17th century)

- **Black Death 1347**: the East-West trade routes built during the Crusades served as the highway for plague transmission
- **Scholarly preservation flowed back**: Aristotle, Avicenna, Averroes — preserved by the Arabs — were translated into Latin via Spain and Sicily, becoming the core of 12th-13th century scholastic philosophy (Aquinas)
- **Reconquista completed 1492**: same year Columbus sailed — **New World colonization was understood as "a new Crusade,"** combining missionary work + reconquest + economic interest
- **Reformation 1517**: Luther opposed papal indulgences — and indulgences were exactly what Urban II invented in 1095 for Crusaders
- **Battle of Lepanto 1571**: European fleet defeated the Ottoman navy, framed by contemporaries as "Christianity's triumph" — Crusader ideology continuing

### Long term (18th century onward)

- **18th-century Enlightenment**: Voltaire and Gibbon framed the Crusades as "ignorance + barbarism + religious fanaticism" — beginning de-sacralization
- **19th-century European colonialism**: British colonial administrators (such as Lord Cromer in Egypt) used "civilizing mission" rhetoric; some Catholic missionaries compared colonization directly to "a new Crusade" — French Algeria was called this by some priests
- **Late 20th century**: Jordan's King Hussein called the founding of Israel "a new Crusade"; Arab nationalism used the Crusades as an anti-colonial metaphor
- **September 20, 2001**: George W. Bush said "this crusade, this war on terrorism, is going to take a while" — global media criticized the word choice
- **Modern**: ISIS calls every Western military action "Crusade" to legitimize counter-jihad; some Western far-right groups use Crusader imagery to legitimize anti-Muslim politics

**The point**: the Crusader war ended in 1291. **But "Crusade" as an ideological symbol is still alive in political language 900 years later.** **This is an extreme case of historical memory diverging from historical fact.**

### Three-way interest analysis

| Side | What they wanted in 1095 | What they actually got 1099-1291 | Long-term legacy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pope Urban II / Roman Curia | Pope above emperor + church reunion + less internal European fighting | Papal power did peak (13th c.); but East-West split deepened; European noble warfare actually intensified | Papal peak followed by decline; 1517 Reformation smashed indulgences |
| Byzantium | A few thousand professional mercenaries to recover Asia Minor | Recovered parts of Asia Minor at terrible cost; 1204 the Crusaders sacked the capital; 1453 empire ends | East-West church split permanent; "European vs. Byzantine" cultural tension persists in Eastern Europe |
| Western European nobles (second sons) | Land + atonement + a way out | Four Crusader states formed; 70% of departures died | "Go find land" mentality continues into the Age of Exploration |
| Western European commoners (poor) | Pilgrimage + emotional momentum | People's Crusade nearly all dead; survivors returned with nothing | "Religion + violence + migration" — a frightening template |
| Seljuk / Arab Muslims | Hold the holy sites + repel the invasion | 1099 lost Jerusalem; 1187 retook it; 1291 total victory | But the "Muslims under attack" collective memory persists |
| Rhineland Jews | No voice | 1096 People's Crusade massacred them; medieval European antisemitism templated | The starting point of 850 years of antisemitic history |
| Jerusalem's three-faith residents | Status quo | 1099 massacre broke the model of multi-faith coexistence | "The holy land must belong to one faith" mentality continues today |

**AP usage**: In a DBQ, **cover at least 3 sides** + add one sentence about a "silent" side like Rhineland Jews or Jerusalem civilians, and you immediately earn the sophistication point.

### The Annales 5-layer causation

```
L0 Geography: narrow Levantine corridor + concentrated holy sites (Jerusalem/Bethlehem)
   + pilgrimage routes + spice route terminus
   ↓
L1 Climate: Medieval Warm Period → Western European population doubles → no land for second/third sons
   ↓
L2 Society: Western Europe ~15M rural population + Italian merchant cities rise + Eastern spice market +
        Islamic world more urbanized than Western Europe
   ↓
L3 Institutions: Papal authority at peak (Pope vs. Emperor 1077 Canossa was 18 years earlier) +
        feudal chivalry (the "expedition = atonement" tradition) +
        Islamic world fragmented (Sunni vs. Shi'a; Seljuk vs. Fatimid) +

   ↓
L4 Events: 1071 Manzikert → 1095 Clermont sermon → 1099 Jerusalem captured →

```

**Core insight**: 1099 is not "sudden religious fanaticism." It is **the joint action of 4 structural conditions + 100 years of institutional evolution**, ignited at exactly the right moment by Urban II's sermon. **Without those four foundation layers, no sermon could have lit the fire.**

### Continuity vs. Change

| Continuity | Change |
|---|---|
| Three-faith reverence for Jerusalem | A new "religion-military-migration" template is born |
| Byzantine decline (already underway) | Western Europe begins moving from "rural feudalism" to engagement with the Mediterranean economy |
| Arab-Islamic cultural advantage (continues to 14th c.) | Latin Christians develop "the East = infidel" binary |
| Pope vs. emperor tension | Pope's mobilization capability briefly strengthens papal authority |

## 8. Contemporary China: A Completely Different Religion-State Relationship

In 1099, China was in the late reign of **Emperor Zhezong of the Northern Song (1085-1100)**. Place 1099 Western Europe, the Islamic world, and China side by side:

| Dimension | Western Europe 1099 | Islamic world 1099 | Northern Song China 1099 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Political structure | Pope + Emperor + 200 duchies (fragmented) | Caliph + Sultan (multi-polar) | **Emperor sole authority** (centralized) |
| Religion-state relationship | Church vs. royal power (two-pole tension) | Caliph nominally combines religious + political | **Imperial power absorbs religion** (Buddhism, Daoism subordinated) |
| Main conflict | Church-state conflict + cross-civilizational religious war | Sunni vs. Shi'a + repelling Crusaders | **Bureaucratic factional disputes** (New Policies vs. Old Policies) |
| Major event of the year | 1099/07 captures Jerusalem | 1099/07 loses Jerusalem | 1099 Northern Song factional struggle escalates (Shaosheng era — Zhang Dun rules and persecutes Yuanyou party) |
| Who can mobilize war | Pope + king | Caliph + Sultan | **Only the emperor** (officials can faction-fight but not raise armies) |

**Key insight**:
- **Western Europe could have a Crusade because the pope, an independent religious authority, possessed military mobilization capability.**
- **The Islamic world could have jihad because the very concept of caliph fuses "religious + political."**
- **China could not have a religious war because imperial power had already fully absorbed religious authority. Buddhist and Daoist hierarchs were appointed by the emperor. No independent religious authority existed that could "summon an army."**

**So**: while Pope Urban II was summoning 100,000 men at Clermont, the contemporary Emperor Zhezong's biggest political event was **factional struggles among bureaucrats** — a debate inside the civil service over **whether the New Policies of Wang Anshi were good for the country.**

**Two paths solving the same problem (how to govern a great state) with completely different answers**:
- The West: religious authority + political authority kept separate + balancing each other (extreme version produces religious war)
- China: religious authority absorbed into political authority (result: no religious war — but also no religious autonomy)

**AP perspective**:
- ❌ Don't write "China was more advanced than the West because no religious wars"
- ❌ Don't write "the West was more advanced than China because of religious freedom"
- ✅ Do write "the two systems gave different answers to 'how independent can religious authority be?' — each with its own costs and benefits"

> **AP sophistication move**: when asked "Why did China never have a Crusade-style religious war?", the highest-scoring answer is not "because China was a Confucian/heathen state" — **it is "because Chinese institutional design left no independent religious authority that could mobilize an army." That is a structural difference, not a cultural one.**

### ⚠️ Each path's costs — must be seen symmetrically

It is easy to read this section and conclude "the Chinese path was costless." **It was not.** Both paths had costs:

**The cost of China's "imperial power absorbs religion" path**:
- When the emperor judges a religion is a threat, **no independent authority can balance it**
- Tang Wuzong's 845 Buddhist persecution (4,600 monasteries destroyed + 260,000 monks forcibly returned to lay life)
- Northern Song Emperor Huizong's suppression of the Fang La rebellion
- Ming-Qing prohibitions on folk religions (White Lotus, Eight Trigrams)
- → **"Religion absorbed = religion cannot protect itself"** — the side effect

**The cost of the West's "church-state separation" path**:
- When both church and king claim universal authority, **no shared higher arbiter exists**
- 1095-1291 Crusades (200 years of armed pilgrimage)
- 1517-1648 Reformation wars (a century of civil war + the Thirty Years' War)
- 1572 St Bartholomew's Day Massacre (in one night, 5,000-30,000 Protestants killed in France)
- → **"Religion stays independent = religious conflict is unregulated"** — the side effect

**Each path prevented the other's pain — and each paid costs the other did not.** This is not "which is right and which is wrong" — it is different trade-offs under different geographies, histories, and institutional combinations.

## 9. What Historians Argue About (Historiography)

The key to understanding the Crusades is not "what happened" — it is "**how 900 years of people have read it.**"

### Four major schools

**① Medieval European holy-war narrative (1099-1700)**

The Crusaders are God's warriors. Urban II is a hero. The 1099 massacre is recorded but interpreted as "God's judgment." This narrative supported European Christian identity for 700 years.

**② Enlightenment critique (Voltaire, Gibbon, 18th-19th c.)**

Edward Gibbon's Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire framed the Crusades as "religious fanaticism + barbarism + self-destruction." Voltaire called them "an outburst of stupidity." **This reading dominated 19th-20th century Western academia.**

**③ Walter Scott romanticization (19th-century popular culture)**

The novelist Walter Scott in The Talisman (1825) created the "noble Saladin vs. zealous Crusader" binary. **This is pure literary projection, not history** — but the image lives on in Hollywood films.

**④ Riley-Smith revisionism (1980s-)**

Jonathan Riley-Smith (1938-2016) in The First Crusaders (1997) reconstructed the **actual motivations** of Crusade participants by studying wills, church archives, and noble genealogies:
- Most nobles **truly believed in indulgence**, not opportunism
- Setting out was **economically irrational** (sold estates to outfit + 70% death rate)
- The "religious zeal vs. economic self-interest" binary is wrong — **for medieval people, the two were one thing**

**Riley-Smith's insight**: you cannot judge medieval people through a modern secular framework. **Their faith was not a hobby — it was the foundation of their entire worldview.**

**⑤ Contemporary de-mythologization (2000s-)**

Christopher Tyerman's God's War (2006) and similar works. They acknowledge:
- The Crusaders were neither heroes nor demons
- They were the joint product of medieval European institutions + mentality + economic pressure + international structure
- **Any modern political symbolic use of them (West / ISIS / Arab nationalism) is misuse**

### Why this Topic is especially hard to keep neutral

Unlike Magna Carta, which is mostly an English-American story, the Crusades involve **geopolitics still ongoing today**:
- Modern Israel-Palestine conflict often traced by both sides to the Crusades
- "9/11" Bush "crusade" remark provoked global Muslim reaction
- European far right uses Crusader imagery against Muslim immigration
- ISIS uses "crusader" to justify anti-Western violence

**So when you (Willow / a student) read this Topic, be on guard**:
- Anyone who says "the Crusades were a defense of Western civilization" — that's 19th-century colonial rhetoric
- Anyone who says "the Crusades were the West's original sin / eternal imperialism" — that's contemporary political simplification
- The truth is: **1099 was the product of a specific time, set of institutions, and mentality that allowed an 1095 sermon to mobilize people. It is not proof of any "civilizational essence."**

> **AP training**: when you write a Crusades essay, **acknowledging this Topic's political sensitivity is itself sophistication.** For example: "This essay analyzes the Crusades as a historical event, not as a metaphor for contemporary East-West relations." That single line earns nuance points.

## 10. Seven Common Misconceptions (Cleanup)

| Misconception | Reality |
|---|---|
| Crusade = Christian defense against Muslim invasion | Partly true but oversimplified. Urban II also wanted: church reunification + outlet for nobles + boost papal authority. **Compound motives, not single defense.** |
| Noble Saladin vs. evil Crusaders | Walter Scott 19th-century novel projection. Saladin was an effective military leader, not a pacifist. Crusaders ranged from genuine believers (Godfrey) to opportunists (Bohemond). |
| 1099 killed 70,000 people | Ibn al-Athir's contemporaneous figure. Modern estimate **3,000-10,000**. Still a massive massacre — but for AP, do not directly cite 70,000; use the academic range. |
| All Crusaders were motivated by faith | Faith + land + atonement + escape + commerce + imitation. Multiple motives. **Bohemond self-titled "Prince of Antioch" en route — naked land hunger.** |
| The Crusades were Western Europe vs. Middle East | **The Fourth Crusade in 1204 sacked Constantinople — Christians attacking Christians.** This is the most absurd truth of all. |
| The West got all the cultural exchange benefits | Yes — **but only in a 12th-13th century window.** The West "received" Arabic-preserved Greek classics (Aristotle, Euclid, Hippocrates), Arabic medicine, algebra, and decimal numbers. Without this contact, the European Renaissance would have been thinner. |
| The Crusades ended in 1291 | The war ended. **The ideology did not.** Reconquista (1492) / colonial era / modern political language — **"crusade" still active in Western political vocabulary 900 years later.** |
