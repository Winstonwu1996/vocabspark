# Contemporary United States · 1980-Present

> This file is the factual foundation for the storyboard. It follows the
> 12-section + 4-appendix structure in AUTHORING_PIPELINE.md §4.
>
> **⚠️ Rule 0 neutrality is the first red line of this course (one-vote veto).**
> This is the most delicate topic in the set — it is living, still-arguing
> politics. Every president, every party, every movement is a **subject we
> study**, not a **side we join**. Whenever something cannot be said without a
> partisan slant, the supporters' view and the critics' view must sit side
> by side, and the student weighs them. The tone model is the College Board
> APUSH Period 9 CED: deliberately analytical, deliberately balanced.

---

## 0. Textbook Anchor

- **APUSH Period 9 (1980-Present)** — the final stretch of the AP US History
  timeline, CED Topics 9.1 through 9.7. The exam spine: the conservative
  turn (Reagan), the end of the Cold War, globalization and economic
  restructuring, the tech revolution, 9/11 and the War on Terror, the 2008
  financial crisis and Great Recession, demographic change, and political
  polarization.
- **A blind spot for Chinese parents**: Chinese school curricula barely cover
  the United States after 1980. Many students have only scattered images of
  names like Reagan, Clinton, Obama, and Trump — and no through-line. This
  course gives that line: what the last 40 years of economic and social
  restructuring actually did to the country.
- **Why this topic is unusually hard**: it is so recent that the adults in a
  student's own home may be arguing about these very events. **Because it is
  alive, it must be taught as history** — give the angles and both sides of
  the evidence; do not give a verdict.

## 1. One-Sentence Anchor (three views, side by side)

From 1980 to today, the United States went through a quiet, enormous
restructuring. Factories left small towns one by one, and money and jobs
flowed toward a new economy — finance, tech, globalized services. The Cold
War was won, and the Soviet Union dissolved. Then 9/11 and two long wars
pulled the country through twenty years. In 2008 a financial wave wiped out
half of millions of people's retirement savings. The same history, landing on
different people, becomes a completely different story.

- Some people **rode the up escalator of the restructuring**: they left a
  manufacturing town for the new economy, and their houses kept getting
  bigger. To them, these 40 years were 40 years of opportunity.
- Some people were **caught in the middle**: a military veteran who enlisted
  after 9/11 and served in Iraq or Afghanistan. He fought for this country
  and came home to find it arguing about everything — including whether the
  war he fought was worth it, with both sides certain.
- Some people stood on the **receiving end**: a working-class family in a
  manufacturing town. The plant closed, the work moved offshore, 2008 hit
  like a fist, the opioid crisis followed, and Main Street went dark store by
  store. The bill for 40 years of restructuring landed on their kitchen
  table.

On the edge of the same town stands one **shuttered factory**. To the three
people it is three different things. The one who rode the escalator left it
behind (relief). The veteran came home to find it finally torn down (a period
at the end of a sentence). The receiving-end family watched its lights go out,
shift by shift (a long goodbye).

## 2. The Stage (L0 geography / L1 climate / L2 society / L3 institutions / L4 events)

- **L0 geography**: The center of gravity is the *Rust Belt* — from
  Pennsylvania, Ohio, and Michigan to Indiana and Wisconsin, an old
  industrial band around the Great Lakes. In the mid-20th century it was the
  heart of steel, cars, and appliances. The contrast is the Sun Belt in the
  South and West, plus the finance and tech corridors on the two coasts — the
  places that rose during the restructuring. This course uses a **fictional,
  composite small town** as the receiving-end stage. It is not tied to any
  real place.
- **L1 climate / scene**: Rust Belt winters are long and gray. A typical
  industrial town's skyline is mills by the river, iron bridges, smokestacks.
  In 1980 those stacks still smoked. By the 2010s many had rusted red-brown,
  windows broken, weeds at the door — this is the **shuttered factory on the
  edge of town** that runs through the whole course.
- **L2 society**: In 1980, American manufacturing could still give a
  high-school graduate a **middle-class life with benefits** — decent hourly
  pay, health insurance, a pension, one paycheck for a whole family. Forty
  years later, in many small towns those jobs are gone, and the service jobs
  that remain often pay less and carry no benefits. Meanwhile the country's
  population kept changing: more diverse, more urban, with immigration a
  recurring national argument.
- **L3 institutions**: Around 1980, American economic thinking took a turn.
  One mainstream story (often called the conservative turn) argued for tax
  cuts, deregulation, and letting markets — not government — lead.
  **Supporters** say this released economic energy and ended the 1970s
  stagflation. **Critics** say it widened inequality and weakened protections
  for ordinary workers. Those two views have argued from 1980 to today, and
  this course does not pick a side for the student.
- **L4 events**: The 40-year spine — Reagan takes office (1981) and cuts
  taxes; the Cold War ends (Berlin Wall 1989, USSR dissolves 1991);
  globalization speeds up (NAFTA 1994, China joins the WTO 2001); the internet
  and smartphone revolution; 9/11 (2001) and the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq;
  the 2008 financial crisis and Great Recession; Obama's 2008 election as the
  first Black president; rising inequality and polarization; the spreading
  opioid crisis. The next section unfolds this line beat by beat.

## 3. The Main Characters

> Three views (the riser / the caught-in-the-middle mediator / the
> receiving-end commoner). All three are **composite characters** — each
> stands for a type of person, not one real individual, and does **not** map
> onto any real politician. Students never role-play a president.
> User-specific details use placeholder syntax; no real name or town is
> hard-coded.

### Lens A — The One Who Rode the Up Escalator (composite · actor) — "Winner of the Restructuring"

He is a composite, standing for the kind of person who moved upward after
1980. The setup: born around 1962 in a Rust Belt factory town, with a father
who worked at the plant on the edge of town. He was good at school, went to
college in the late 1980s, and studied something the new economy could use —
finance, computers, or globalized business. After graduating he did not go
home; there were no jobs waiting for him there. He went to a big city and
joined a bank, or a software company, or a firm that moved its production
overseas.

For the next thirty years he climbed with the rhythm of the era: the 1990s
internet boom, the 2000s global supply chains, the 2010s smartphone economy.
His income, his house, his kids' schools — each stepped up. From his angle,
these 40 years were **40 years of opportunity**. He proved a worker's son
could trade one life for another through education and movement.

But the climb had a shadow. The small town he left, and the plant where his
father worked, fell apart during the very same years he was rising. Some of
the money he made came from exactly this — moving production to a cheaper
place. **His gain is real, and the cost of his gain is real** — both are true
at once. This view lets the student stand inside the riser and see the logic
of globalization and the tech economy, and the shadow under its feet.

### Lens B — The Veteran Caught in the Middle (composite · mediator) — "The One Who Stood on Every Side"

He is a composite, standing for the generation of American veterans who
enlisted after 9/11 and served in Iraq or Afghanistan. The setup: born around
1985 into an ordinary American family. On September 11, 2001, he was still in
high school or had just finished. Like many in his generation, the attack
changed him. He enlisted, and was sent to Afghanistan (the war began October
2001) or Iraq (the war began March 2003).

What he saw on the front was hard to explain at home. He saw the war's
**meaningful side** (comrades, discipline, the concrete duty of protecting the
people next to him) and its **heavy cost** (casualties, length, goals that
kept changing). **Those who supported the wars** called them counterterrorism,
protecting American safety. **Those who opposed them** said the wars ran too
long, cost too much, and rested on contested reasons. He heard both voices —
because he stood right in the middle of them.

Back home, he found the country arguing about everything — about whether the
war was worth it, about politics, about each other. He became the kind of
person who **sees every side and fully belongs to none**. When he came home,
the plant on the edge of town was being torn down. This view lets the student
stand where he stands, to understand a history where mission and cost live in
the same body, and a country pulling apart.

### Lens C — The Receiving-End Working Family (composite · commoner, DEFAULT) — "Where the Restructuring Landed"

This is a composite family, standing for the countless working households in
Rust Belt factory towns. The setup: the story starts in 1980. The father works
at the plant on the edge of town — a job you could get with a high-school
diploma that still supported a whole family: decent hourly pay, health
insurance, a pension. The mother works in town. The family owns its house; the
kids go to the local school. In 1980 Main Street is busy, and the factory's
lights burn around the clock in three shifts.

Over the next forty years, this family is the **receiving end** of the
restructuring. First the plant lays people off, then it moves the production
line overseas, then it closes entirely — they watched the factory's lights go
out, shift by shift. Then the 2008 crisis lands another blow; house,
retirement account, and job take the hit together. Later the opioid wave
arrives, and people in the small town begin to die from it. On Main Street the
stores close one by one.

But this family is not only a story of loss. **They also adapt, hold on, and
rebuild** — someone goes to community college to retrain, someone opens a
small business, some move away and some stay to keep the town alive. The
receiving end does not mean the losers; they are where these 40 years of
restructuring actually came down to earth.

> **Cross-view ending for the default view (hard rule)**: When this view ends,
> the course must say plainly — you have heard only **one side**. That same
> shuttered factory is relief to "the one who rode the escalator" and a period
> at the end of a sentence to "the veteran." Another view will **challenge or
> add to** the judgment you hold right now. The Learning Receipt must ask:
> **whose voice have you not heard yet?**

## 4. The Story (plot beats)

> Timeline + turns + irony. Every beat sets the supporters' view and the
> critics' view side by side, with no verdict.

1. **1981: The conservative turn opens.** In August 1981 Reagan signed the
   Economic Recovery Tax Act, a large tax cut (the top income tax rate fell
   from 70% to 50%, and after the 1986 tax reform to 28% — done in two steps,
   across two terms). Deregulation moved alongside it. **Supporters** say this
   sparked the economy and ended the 1970s stagflation. **Critics** say it
   started inequality widening and weakened worker protections. Both views
   start running from this year.

2. **The 1980s: factories begin to leave; globalization rehearses.** Rust Belt
   plants lay off and move out across the decade. The receiving-end family
   feels the ground shift for the first time. Meanwhile a new economy rises on
   the coasts. The same act — moving production to a cheaper place — is
   efficiency and opportunity to the riser, and lost livelihoods to the
   receiving end.

3. **1989: the Berlin Wall falls.** On the night of November 9, 1989, crowds
   pushed open the wall that had split East and West Berlin for 28 years. This
   is the turning image of the Cold War winding down.

4. **1991: the Soviet Union dissolves.** In December 1991, Gorbachev resigned
   on December 25, and the Soviet Union formally dissolved on December 26. The
   roughly 40-year Cold War ended, and the United States became the world's
   only superpower. **One view**: this was the victory of free markets and
   democracy. **The other view**: after the victory, America fell into a new
   uncertainty about what role it should play in the world.

5. **1994: NAFTA takes effect.** On January 1, 1994, the North American Free
   Trade Agreement took effect, sharply lowering trade barriers among the US,
   Canada, and Mexico. **Supporters** say it lowered prices and grew the
   economy. **Critics** say it sped up the outflow of manufacturing jobs. Rust
   Belt towns felt both of those views in their own lives.

6. **The internet and smartphone revolution.** Personal computers and the
   internet spread in the 1990s; in June 2007 the first iPhone went on sale,
   opening the smartphone age. Technology reshaped the economic map — it pushed
   wealth and jobs further toward the tech corridors, and further away from the
   old manufacturing towns.

7. **2001: China joins the WTO.** On December 11, 2001, China formally became
   a WTO member. From then on, the American and Chinese economies were deeply
   intertwined. The factories America closed and the factories China opened
   were two ends of the same supply chain (see §8).

8. **2001: 9/11.** On September 11, 2001, hijackers struck the World Trade
   Center towers in New York and the Pentagon; about 2,977 people died. It is
   the deepest collective wound in contemporary America. That October 7, the
   US sent forces into Afghanistan.

9. **2003: the Iraq War.** In March 2003 the US sent forces into Iraq. The
   stated reason (weapons of mass destruction) later drew enormous dispute.
   **Supporters** at the time called it an extension of counterterrorism and
   security. **Critics** said the reason was false, the war too long, the cost
   too heavy. The veteran's view sits exactly between these two.

10. **2008: financial crisis and the Great Recession.** On September 15, 2008,
    the investment bank Lehman Brothers went bankrupt, setting off a global
    financial wave. In the Great Recession (December 2007 to June 2009) the US
    lost about 8.7 million jobs, and unemployment hit 10% in October 2009. The
    receiving-end family's house, retirement account, and job took the hit
    together. **One view** puts the bill on Wall Street's greed and missing
    regulation; **the other view** puts it on over-borrowing and policy
    incentives. The two ledgers have never been reconciled.

11. **2008: Obama is elected the first Black president.** On November 4, 2008,
    Obama was elected, and on January 20, 2009, he was inaugurated as the first
    African American president in US history. For many people, this was a
    historic step for the country on race; the moment is written into
    contemporary America's collective memory.

12. **The 2010s to today: inequality, polarization, the opioid crisis.** The
    restructuring's bill keeps coming due: economic inequality widens; political
    polarization deepens, and two parties and two Americas find it harder to
    talk; the opioid crisis runs from 1990s prescription drugs (OxyContin,
    released by Purdue in 1995-96), to heroin around 2010, to fentanyl after
    2013 — and from 1999 to 2020 about 565,000 Americans died of opioid
    overdoses, with Rust Belt towns among the hardest hit.

**Irony**: the same globalization made the riser's good years and hollowed out
the town where he was born; the Cold War was "won," yet twenty years later the
winner fell into unprecedented division at home; the loudest tech and finance
boom and the quietest small-town decline are two faces of the same 40 years.

## 5. Source Close-Reading (primary sources)

> Original + a high-school-readable version + multiple views. This section
> deliberately picks texts **both sides quote**, to demonstrate Rule 0.

### A. Reagan's First Inaugural Address (January 20, 1981)
> Original: "In this present crisis, government is not the solution to our
> problem; government is the problem."
> Simple version: "In this crisis right now, government is not the cure for
> our problem — government is the problem."
> Multiple views: **Supporters** treat this line as the program of the
> conservative turn — less regulation, let the market lead. **Critics** say it
> was exactly this "government is the problem" thinking that later weakened
> protections for workers and small towns. The same sentence is used by both
> sides as opposite evidence. This course does not judge whether the line is
> right — it asks the student to see **why both sides quote it**.

### B. Reagan at the Berlin Wall (June 12, 1987)
> Original: "Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!"
> Simple version: "Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!"
> Reading: Two years later, in November 1989, the wall did open. **One view**
> credits the fall to American diplomacy and pressure; **the other view**
> stresses the power of ordinary people in Eastern Europe and the Soviet
> Union's own reforms and troubles. Big events rarely have a single cause —
> which is exactly what AP DBQ training asks students to see.

### C. A Typical "Plant Closing Notice" (a WARN notice — generic, not a specific company)
> Background: US law (the WARN Act, 1988) requires large employers to give
> notice before a plant closing or mass layoff. A closing notice is, to a
> receiving-end family, one page that shakes the ground.
> Simple version: "Notice: this plant will permanently close on [date], and
> the related jobs will be cut."
> Reading: there is nothing fancy here. One cold notice is what 40 years of
> restructuring looks like when it lands on a family's table. In the riser's
> world this is called "industrial transition" and "efficiency"; in the
> receiving-end world it is called "my dad lost his job." The same event, two
> vocabularies.

### D. The National Mood After 9/11 (September 2001)
> Background: after the attacks, the country had a rare stretch of unity, with
> flags up on every block.
> Reading: that unity was soon pulled apart again by later war decisions
> (especially the 2003 Iraq War). **Those who supported sending troops** and
> **those who opposed it** each cited 9/11 — one saying "so we must strike
> first," the other saying "so we must be careful and not get stuck." The core
> tension of the veteran's view is right here: he went to war for a country
> that had been united, then split.

## 6. Impact (short / medium / long term)

- **Short term (1980s-90s)**: the conservative turn reshaped the tax and
  regulation framework; the Cold War's end left the US the only superpower;
  globalization and the internet began rearranging the economic map.
- **Medium term (2000s)**: 9/11 and two wars defined a generation's coming of
  age; after China's WTO entry, US-China supply chains locked together; the
  2008 crisis broke the longest postwar boom, shrinking the wealth of millions
  of families.
- **Long term (2010s to today)**: economic inequality and regional split (the
  rising corridors vs the declining towns) hardened; polarization deepened; the
  opioid crisis left hundreds of thousands dead and countless broken families;
  immigration and demographic change became a continuing national argument.
  These 40 years of restructuring are **not yet settled** — they are still the
  bedrock geology under every argument in American politics today.

## 7. AP DBQ Thinking Practice

- **Stakeholder grid**: winners of the restructuring (finance/tech/globalized
  services) / Rust Belt working families / veterans / immigrants / the base of
  each party — what did each **gain, lose, and how does each tell this
  history**?
- **Continuity & Change**: from 1980 (manufacturing middle class, Cold War,
  leftover heavy regulation) to today (service/tech economy, unipolar then
  multipolar, deregulation then a renewed argument) — what changed (industry,
  world order, technology)? What stayed (the promise of opportunity and
  mobility, the tension between paper equality and real-life gaps)?
- **Causation (multiple causes)**: was the loss of manufacturing jobs the fault
  of globalization, of automation/technology, or of policy? Mainstream research
  usually says **layered causes** — the very point of DBQ training is to avoid
  single-cause thinking.
- **Comparison**: America's 1980 restructuring ≈ China's 1978 reform and
  opening — two halves of the same globalization story (see §8). Putting these
  two side by side is this course's strongest DBQ exercise.

## 8. China at the Same Time (structural comparison — Rule 0 one-vote-veto dimension)

America's great restructuring around 1980 and China's **reform and opening**
starting in 1978 are not two unrelated stories — they are **two halves of the
same globalization story**.

- **Two ends of one supply chain**: in December 1978, the Third Plenum of the
  11th Central Committee launched reform and opening, with Deng Xiaoping as
  chief architect. After that, factories rose along China's coast one by one.
  During the very same years, Rust Belt factories closed one by one. **The
  factories America closed and the factories China opened were often two ends
  of the same supply chain** — one side's "industry moving out" was the other
  side's "industry moving in."
- **2001, a key node**: China joined the WTO in December 2001, and the two
  economies grew deeply intertwined. The decade-plus that followed was the
  period when the two economies were tied most tightly, and it also set the
  stage for the US-China trade frictions of the 2010s.
- **Many Chinese American families are inside this arc themselves**: within
  this globalization, many families moved from China to the United States.
  Their own migration history sits exactly inside these 40 years of
  restructuring. For the Chinese students studying this course, this is not
  someone else's history.

Rule 0: this is a **juxtaposition of two transformations**, not a **contest of
who won**. We cannot say America "declined" and China "rose," and we cannot say
the reverse. Each side answered its own situation, each with its own costs and
gains. Seeing it as two faces of one coin — that is the point of this section.

## 9. What Historians Are Arguing About (historiography)

- **Was the restructuring progress or loss?** One school stresses that the turn
  after 1980 released economic energy and lifted overall living standards and
  the technology frontier; another stresses that its cost fell on the working
  class and manufacturing regions and was the start of widening inequality.
  Both read different slices of the same data.
- **Why did manufacturing jobs vanish?** One school points to globalization and
  trade (jobs outsourced); another points to automation and rising productivity
  (machines replacing people, unrelated to trade). Recent research tends toward
  "both, hard to separate."
- **How to judge the post-9/11 wars**: from "necessary counterterrorism" to
  "overreach that cost too much," scholars and the public are still arguing —
  this is historiography too close to settle.
- **The roots of polarization**: driven by economic inequality? amplified by
  media and technology (social media)? or deeper cultural and identity splits?
  No consensus.
- **A neutral synthesis**: the hardest thing about contemporary history is that
  **it is not over**. We are inside it, without the distance of time. The honest
  move is not to pretend there is a verdict, but to lay several explanations
  side by side and admit we cannot yet see the whole.

## 10. Common Misconceptions (the cleanup zone)

> These "misconceptions" are deliberately written as traps you can fall into
> from **either direction** — they correct factual accuracy and single-cause
> thinking, and **assume no political answer**.

1. ❌ "Manufacturing jobs vanished, all because of one trade deal (or one
   president)." → ✅ Mainstream research says it was globalization, automation,
   and policy layered together; single-cause blame is a DBQ trap.
2. ❌ "Reagan's tax cut took the top rate from 70% to 28% in one step." → ✅ It
   was two steps across two terms: down to 50% in 1981, then to 28% after the
   1986 tax reform.
3. ❌ "The Cold War was won by American pressure alone." → ✅ The Soviet Union's
   own reforms and troubles and the power of Eastern European people were all
   key — many causes together.
4. ❌ "America stayed united after 9/11." → ✅ The brief national unity was soon
   torn into two sides again by later war decisions (especially Iraq 2003).
5. ❌ "The 2008 crisis was only Wall Street's fault (or only the borrowers'
   fault)." → ✅ Regulation, financial innovation, incentives, and individual
   behavior were all involved; each side keeps its own ledger.
6. ❌ "In globalization America lost everything and China won everything (or
   the reverse)." → ✅ It was two ends of one supply chain, each with gains and
   losses, not a zero-sum scorecard.
7. ❌ "The opioid crisis was just drug users' personal choice." → ✅ It began
   with the mass marketing of prescription drugs in the 1990s and is a public
   health crisis of medical, regulatory, and economic factors.

## 11. Open Questions (no conclusions)

> These questions deliberately **assume no correct political answer**. Each can
> be answered seriously by both the supporting and the opposing side.

1. If an economic restructuring makes the whole country richer but concentrates
   the bill on certain towns and certain workers — is the restructuring "worth
   it as progress" or "an unfair cost"? What is your reason for your side, and
   how would the other side push back on you?
2. The same shuttered factory is relief to "the one who rode the escalator" and
   a long goodbye to the receiving-end family. When two people feel the
   complete opposite about the same thing, and both are real — how should
   history record it?
3. The veteran caught in the middle saw both the meaningful side of the war and
   its heavy cost. If you came home and heard the two sides arguing over
   "was it worth it," how would you speak?
4. Twenty years after the Cold War was "won," the winner fell into unprecedented
   division at home. A country can find it harder to unite when there is no
   outside enemy — what does that tell you?
5. The factories America closed and the factories China opened are two ends of
   the same supply chain. If you had to explain one end to a person who has only
   seen the other end, where would you start?
6. Around you today, are there two worlds as far apart as "the rising corridor"
   and "the declining town"? Which one are you in, and can you see the other?
