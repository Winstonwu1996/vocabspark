# Mesopotamia & Sumer · The First Cities

> **Lock status**: narrative kernel v0 — pending review
>
> **CA HSS anchor**: Grade 6, Standard 6.2 (Mesopotamia / Origins of Civilization)
> **Through-line**: the first cities, the first writing — and the first time people were sorted into "the recorded" and "the recorders"
> **Difficulty**: 3/5 (many new terms, deep-time dates, one big counter-intuitive idea)
> **Expected duration**: 30-40 min

---

## 0. Open Your Textbook First (Textbook Anchor)

> This belongs to **CA HSS Grade 6, Standard 6.2 — Mesopotamia.** It is the **first real "civilization" unit** in sixth-grade world history. Almost every World History textbook opens with Mesopotamia, because it is the cleanest classic case of the **"urban revolution"** and the **building blocks of civilization.**
>
> Your textbook will ask you to know:
> - The two rivers — **Tigris** and **Euphrates** — and **irrigation farming.**
> - **City-states**, **cuneiform** (the first writing), and the **ziggurat** (a temple tower).
> - Hammurabi's law code.
>
> **What your textbook may NOT make clear** (this is why we need this lesson):
> - **Writing was invented to keep accounts, not to write poetry.** About **85-90%** of the earliest clay tablets are administrative records — grain, sheep, workers, debts.
> - **Sumer was not one big empire.** For most of this period it was a set of **competing, often warring city-states.** The first empire came only around **2334 BCE** with **Sargon.**
> - **A ziggurat is NOT a tomb.** It is a stepped platform for a temple — don't confuse it with the Egyptian pyramids.
> - **The AP angle:** every "building block" of civilization solved an old problem AND created a new inequality. Sumer is the first time humans were systematically sorted into "those who are recorded" and "those who record them." This lesson holds both the greatness and the cost — it does not pick a final verdict for you.

## 1. One-Sentence Anchor (Many Factions at Once)

Around **3500 to 3100 BCE**, on a hot, dry, almost rainless plain between two great rivers — in what is now southern Iraq — humans built the world's first true **city**: **Uruk** (OO-rook). At its peak it may have held about **40,000 people** inside the city, making it the largest gathering of humans on earth at the time. On that same land, people did one more thing that changed the world: to keep track of who had paid how much grain, and how much barley sat in the temple storehouse, they pressed signs into wet clay tablets with a reed. This was humanity's **first writing** — **cuneiform** (kyoo-NEE-uh-form).

But "the first city" was squeezed out of several groups whose lives could not have been more different:

- **the temple priests and managers (en / ensi)** — they ran the biggest "company" in the city: the temple. A temple was not only a place of worship. It was the storehouse, the bank, the workshop, and the landlord, all in one. They invented writing because they had to keep accounts.
- **the lugal (LOO-gahl, "big man" → king)** — at first a temporary war leader, then someone who held power tighter and tighter until it became hereditary kingship. **Gilgamesh** (GIL-guh-mesh), in legend, was an early lugal of Uruk.
- **the people who could write (dub-sar, "scribes")** — the tiny few who mastered the hundreds of signs. To write was to control records, orders, and laws — one of the highest forms of power at the time.
- **the majority in the fields and temple workshops** — the people who grew the barley, dug the canals, wove cloth, and carried bricks. Their labor fed the city and raised the temple tower. Most of them appear in history, if at all, as a single line on a ledger — "received this much grain" — or are not written down at all.

Far away, on the other side of the earth, the Yellow River valley had **no cities and no writing** yet. China's earliest state-level society (Erlitou) would not appear for about another **1,500 years**, and China's first certain writing (oracle-bone script) not until about **1250 BCE**. Two great river civilizations, one earlier and one later, each walked the road "from village to state" on its own. The moment the reed pressed the first line of accounts into wet clay, the person written down and the person doing the writing began to live in two different worlds.

## 2. The Stage (Geography / Climate / Society / Institutions / Events)

- **Geography**: **Mesopotamia** is Greek for "the land between the rivers" — the plain between the **Tigris** and the **Euphrates**, roughly today's Iraq. The earliest cities clustered in the south, in **Sumer**: **Uruk**, **Ur**, **Eridu** (the city said to be oldest), **Lagash**, **Nippur**. This land had **no stone, no large trees, almost no metal ore** — only mud, reeds, river water, and a blazing sun. As you'll see, what this land *lacked* shaped what the Sumerians *invented.*
- **Climate**: Extremely hot and dry — too little rain to grow crops. But the two rivers flooded each year, leaving rich silt. The catch: the floods came at the wrong time (late spring, not planting season) and were unpredictable. So farming here was an engineering problem from day one — **no canals, no food.** Endless barley fields, a grid of irrigation canals, sun-bleached mud-brick walls, and at the center a temple tower (**ziggurat**) built higher and higher like a man-made mountain — that was the skyline of Sumer.
- **Society**: Around 3100 BCE Uruk may have held about **40,000 people** inside the city, and **80,000-90,000** counting its surroundings — the largest city in the world. Surplus grain fed a whole class of **people who did not farm**: priests, scribes, craftsmen, soldiers, merchants, rulers. This was the first time humans had such fine **division of labor** — and the first time there were such clear **social classes**: a few who lived in temples and palaces, were recorded and remembered; a majority in the fields and workshops, appearing on the tablets only as a ration figure.
- **Institutions**: Sumer was not one country. It was a cluster of competing, sometimes warring **city-states** — each with its own patron god, its own temple, its own ruler. The strongest early institution was the **temple economy**: the temple, owned in name by the city's god, in fact controlled vast land, thousands of workers, and huge storehouses — handing out rations, collecting tribute, organizing irrigation. Later, worldly kings (lugals) grew more powerful than the priests. Later still came written law — a line that runs all the way to Hammurabi's code around **1792-1750 BCE** (a separate lesson).
- **Events**: There is no single "one night it all began" — civilization grew slowly over centuries. But there are landmark moments: Eridu founded around **5400 BCE** (the Ubaid period); the Uruk urban revolution peaking around **3500-3100 BCE** with an explosion of population; **the birth of cuneiform around 3350-3200 BCE** (first as *proto-cuneiform*, almost entirely accounts); and around **2334 BCE**, **Sargon** conquering the city-states and building humanity's first known **empire** (the Akkadian Empire).

## 3. The Main Characters

> Three perspectives (actor, mediator, receiving-end), including at least one powerless party.

### The temple scribe (actor) — a literate manager in a Uruk temple storehouse

A bridging character, but the role is fully real — he stands for the most important and most underrated group in Sumer: **the people who could write (dub-sar, scribes).** He has no name, because he stands for a class (and very few real scribes left their own names). What is certain is his situation: he sits in a mud-brick room beside the Uruk temple storehouse, a pile of wet clay tablets at hand, a sharpened reed (a **stylus**). What he does looks ordinary to us now, but it was world-changing then: pressing signs into clay to record things like "Kushim received this much barley, to be stored for this many days." Some of the earliest tablets humanity has ever found record exactly this kind of account.

He is not a poet or a philosopher. He invented and used writing for one purpose at first: **to count things** — how much barley, how many sheep, how many workers, who owed what. About **85-90%** of the earliest tablets are this kind of administrative record. But because he could record, count, and write down orders and contracts, he became the brain of the city machine. This perspective shows you a counter-intuitive truth: **writing was not born to write poems or stories — it was born to keep accounts, to manage, to serve power.** Poetry (like the later Epic of Gilgamesh) was written down only centuries after writing matured.

### The lugal / early king (mediator-actor) — the "big man" between priests and army

A historical role (anchored on the Gilgamesh legend, with a fully real basis). **Lugal** literally means "big man." In the earliest Sumer, the priests (en) ran the city. But city-states fought over water and land, and war needed a commander — that commander was the lugal. The problem: once you taste temporary military power, it is hard to hand back. The lugal slowly turned from "wartime commander" into "hereditary king," building a palace beside — and eventually above — the temple.

Uruk's legendary king **Gilgamesh** stands right at this turning point. Later epics made him a hero who was two-thirds god and one-third human, who raised great walls for Uruk. Peel away the legend, and he stands for a real historical process: **power moving from the temple (priests) to the palace (king).** This perspective shows you a mediator-and-power-grabber: he had to balance priests, army, and the people of the city, while pulling more and more power into his own hands. He is the key figure at the moment when "the state" came into being out of nothing.

### The temple field laborer (receiving-end · DEFAULT) — a person whose name appears on a ledger exactly once

A bridging character with a fully real role (this is the DEFAULT perspective). He has no exact name — because for the vast majority of such people, **history left only one line on a ledger: how much grain they received.** What is certain is his situation: a laborer owned by the temple. He might be bent over in a barley field cutting the heads of grain, or digging a deep irrigation canal under a scorching sun, or in a workshop pressing mud bricks and weaving wool cloth. His ration is barley, handed out by the day and written on the scribe's tablet — a tablet he himself cannot read, even though it decides how much he eats.

What do the "building blocks of civilization" mean to him? **City** — he lives inside the walls, but they were not built for him. **Surplus grain** — he grew it, but most of it went into the temple storehouse; he got only a ration. **Writing** — it recorded his labor but did not belong to him; he is the one written about, not the one who writes. **Division of labor and class** — he was sorted into the very bottom layer. This perspective is the heart of this lesson. We are used to calling Sumer "the glorious beginning of civilization." But the other face of civilization is this: it is the first time humans systematically sorted people into "those who are recorded" and "those who record them." From this position, you ask an AP-level question: **a city's greatness — paid for with whose labor, and whose being-forgotten?**

## 4. The Story (Plot Beats)

1. **First, the water problem (c. 5400-4000 BCE, the Ubaid period).** The southern river plain was hot and dry, but the soil was rich and the rivers flooded. The earliest villages (like Eridu, where the king list says "kingship first descended from heaven") discovered: if people dug canals together, this land could feed far more people than anywhere else. Irrigation farming is the start of everything — it created **surplus grain.**
2. **Surplus grain → people who don't farm (c. 4000-3500 BCE).** With extra food, you can feed people who never go into the fields: priests, craftsmen, soldiers, managers. This is the first time humans had fine **division of labor** and clear **classes.** The temple became the city's largest institution — owned in name by the god, in fact controlling fields, people, and storehouses.
3. **The city explodes: Uruk (c. 3500-3100 BCE).** Uruk grew like a snowball — at its peak about **40,000 people** inside the city, the largest city in the world. Huge temple complexes rose, and the **city-state** took shape — one city, one patron god, one ruling staff. This is the textbook's **"urban revolution."**
4. **To keep accounts, writing is invented (c. 3350-3200 BCE).** The temple had to manage thousands of workers and mountains of barley — too much to hold in memory. So someone began pressing signs into wet clay to keep accounts: **proto-cuneiform** was born. Of the earliest thousands of tablets, **85-90% are administrative records.** Writing was first a **management tool**, not literature.
5. **The logic of the tablet (c. 3200-2900 BCE).** The signs got simpler with use, from little pictures into wedge strokes pressed by a reed (cuneiform = Latin for "wedge-shaped"). It first recorded numbers and goods, then slowly could record language, names, orders, contracts — and whoever had that skill held power. Scribes became an elite profession.
6. **From priest to king (c. 3000-2350 BCE).** City-states fought over water and land. The wartime commander, the lugal, held power tighter, going from temporary to hereditary, his palace rising beside the temple. The Gilgamesh legend is anchored on this turn from "temple power" to "kingship."
7. **The first empire: Sargon of Akkad (c. 2334 BCE).** **Sargon**, from the north, conquered the Sumerian city-states and, for the first time, gathered many cities under one ruler — humanity's **first known empire** (the Akkadian Empire). He also institutionalized rule: appointing governors, standardizing measures, using the Akkadian language.
8. **The first author with a name: Enheduanna (c. 2285 BCE).** Sargon's daughter **Enheduanna** (en-hed-oo-AH-nah) was appointed high priestess of the moon god at Ur. She wrote hymns to the goddess Inanna — and signed her own name. She is widely regarded as **the first author in history whose name we know.** Note the contrast: for centuries since its birth, writing had been an anonymous accountant's tool. Only now did a **specific person** use it to say, "I wrote this."
9. **The Epic of Gilgamesh is written down (matured after c. 2100 BCE).** The story of Uruk's half-god king, passed down orally for a long time, was finally carved into tablets by scribes and became one of humanity's earliest great works of literature — with friendship, the fear of death, and a great flood (far older than the Biblical flood). Writing had finally grown from ledger into literature.
10. **The soil strikes back: salinization (over centuries).** Here is a cruel irony: the irrigation that made Sumer strong was also slowly destroying it. In a hot, dry climate, canal water evaporated and left salt in the soil year after year (**salinization**), and barley yields fell. This is one structural reason some city-states later declined and populations shifted — the very technology a civilization rose on carried the seed of its own trouble.

**An irony to keep:** humans invented writing not to be immortal, but to keep track of "who owed the temple how much barley." One of the first names writing ever "remembered forever" (like *Kushim*) was very likely not a king or a poet, but **an accountant managing a storehouse** — humanity's earliest "famous name" was a bookkeeper.

## 5. Reading the Documents (Primary Sources)

### A. One of the earliest tablets: Kushim's barley account (c. 3300 BCE, Uruk)
> Content (scholarly reading): a proto-cuneiform tablet records a transaction of barley, with the sign "Kushim" beside it. Most scholars think Kushim was probably an institutional title or the person handling the account.
> Plain reading: one of the earliest names humanity ever "wrote down" was not a king or a hero, but a name attached to a barley account — very likely a person who managed a storehouse.
> Many-sided reading: this single piece of clay is the whole lesson in miniature. **The one who writes** (the scribe) uses it to manage; **the ones written about** (the laborers paying and receiving grain) mostly left not a single line; and history's first "famous name" turns out to be an accountant. From the moment writing was born, it stood on the side of the managers.

### B. The Sumerian King List (many copies, earliest traceable to after c. 2100 BCE)
> Content (paraphrased spirit): "Kingship descended from heaven; it first descended at Eridu." The list names king after king, the earliest written as reigning for tens of thousands of years.
> Plain reading: this is the Sumerians' own official story of "how we got kings" — kingship was not won by people, it was given from heaven by the gods.
> Reading: notice that this is **not objective history — it is political propaganda.** Calling kingship "heaven-given" makes the current ruler's power seem natural and beyond challenge. To read it well, you read out "who is speaking, and what do they want you to believe" — exactly the core skill AP asks of you with sources. (You may compare with China's "Mandate of Heaven" in §8 — but the two arose independently.)

### C. Temple ration tablets (administrative documents, common type in the 3rd millennium BCE)
> Content (type description): great numbers of tablets record the daily/monthly barley, oil, and wool rations the temple handed out to workers, listing head counts and amounts.
> Plain reading: the temple was like a giant employer, handing out rations every day and recording it all — but it recorded "how much was given," not "who this person was, or how they lived."
> Reading: this is the documentary foundation of the receiving-end perspective. For the vast majority of ordinary Sumerians, the **only** way they entered the historical record was as a number on a ration list. Their labor was recorded precisely; their lives were not recorded at all.

### D. A fragment of the Epic of Gilgamesh (mature text c. 2nd millennium BCE, from older oral roots)
> Content as passed down (paraphrased spirit): after his friend Enkidu dies, the hero Gilgamesh, terrified of death, travels far to seek immortal life — and fails. His final insight is that humans must die, and what one can leave behind is the city walls one has built.
> Plain reading: over four thousand years ago, people were already in pain over "why do we die, and what does life mean" — and were writing poems about it.
> Reading: this shows that once writing matured, it was no longer just a ledger — it began to carry humanity's deepest fears and questions. But don't forget the order: **first the ledger, then centuries later the epic.** Literature is a civilization's luxury; accounts are its foundation.

## 6. Consequences (Short / Medium / Long Term)

- **Short term (within the 3rd millennium BCE):** city-states, the temple economy, cuneiform, and written administrative systems spread across Mesopotamia, and Sargon built the first empire. A whole toolkit for "how to organize thousands of strangers to live together" was invented — this is the prototype of "the state."
- **Medium term (the ancient world):** cuneiform became the international writing system of the Near East for over two thousand years, borrowed by the Akkadians, Babylonians, Assyrians, and Hittites. Sumerian inventions — base-60 counting (our 60-minute hour and 360-degree circle come from here), the wheel, the plow, the tradition of written law, city planning — were inherited and reshaped by civilization after civilization. Hammurabi's code (c. 1792-1750 BCE) is a famous link in this "written law" chain.
- **Long term (to today):** Sumer set up a human way of living that lasts to this day — **cities + writing + states + classes.** We live in cities, use writing, pay taxes, and are defined by records (school files, accounts, ID cards); the seed of that logic is on Uruk's tablets. Its bright side: humans could, for the first time, cooperate at scale, accumulate knowledge, and pass it across generations. Its cost side: humans were also, for the first time, systematically sorted into "the recorded, ruled majority" and "the recording, ruling few." The light and the cost have shared one body since the very first tablet.

## 7. AP DBQ Thinking Practice

- **Stakeholder grid:** priest (en) / king (lugal) / scribe (dub-sar) / merchant / craftsman / temple laborer / slave / rival city — in the urban revolution, what did each group gain and lose from "cities + writing + states"? Whose labor fed whose power?
- **Building blocks of civilization · causal chain (key tested angle):** lay out the causal chain with Sumerian evidence — irrigation → surplus grain → division of labor → classes → writing → the state. Which link is the "necessary condition"? Would the story differ without the hidden threat of salinization? AP rewards arguments that distinguish "correlation" from "causation."
- **Whom did writing serve? (core argument):** "Writing began as a tool of management and power, not literature." Argue it with evidence: 85-90% of early tablets are administrative; Kushim was an accountant, not a poet; the epic came centuries after the ledger. This counter-intuitive claim can score high.
- **Continuity & change:** from Uruk around 3500 BCE to Sargon's empire around 2334 BCE — what changed (temple power → kingship → empire; city-states → empire), and what stayed the same (the temple economy, the managerial nature of writing, the laborer's situation at the bottom)?
- **A source is propaganda:** the Sumerian King List calls kingship "heaven-given." Read it critically: a source often tells you not "what happened," but "what its writer wanted you to believe."

## 8. China in the Same Era (Structural Comparison · Rule 0 veto dimension)

When Uruk became the world's first great city around 3500-3100 BCE, and cuneiform was born around 3350-3200 BCE, the Yellow River valley had **no** cities or states and **no** writing yet. China's earliest state-level society — the **Erlitou** (UHR-lee-toh) culture — appeared only around **1900-1500 BCE** (some dating places it around 1750-1530 BCE), roughly **1,500 years after Uruk**; it is often seen by Chinese scholars as the material counterpart of the legendary **Xia dynasty.** China's earliest **certain, undisputed** writing — the Shang dynasty's **oracle-bone script** — appeared only around **1250 BCE.**

Structural comparison (side by side, no praise or ranking):
- **Two independent river civilizations:** both the Mesopotamian and the Yellow River valleys grew from villages into states beside great rivers, on farming surplus, on their own. There was almost no contact between them. Scholars widely agree that **writing was invented independently at least three times in the world** (the Near East, China, and Mesoamerica) — Chinese characters and Sumerian cuneiform differ in both principle and form, and are **separate, independent inventions**, not one copying the other. This is the strongest evidence of "different roads, same destination": in two places that never spoke to each other, humans each arrived at "cities + writing + states."
- **Irrigation, alike and different:** Sumer almost never rained — **no canals, no food** — so irrigation was a matter of life and death, and it bred the threat of salinization. The Yellow River valley had monsoon rains, so early farming leaned more on rainfall and small-scale water control, and depended on large irrigation less absolutely than the two rivers did. Both "relied on a great river," but answered it differently.
- **What writing was for:** Sumerian cuneiform was born as the **temple's ledger** (managing grain, managing people). China's earliest certain writing, oracle-bone script, was mainly **divination records** — the Shang king asking ancestors and spirits: should we go to war, will it rain, will the king's toothache heal? One began serving **economic management**, the other serving **communication with spirits and royal power.** Two writing systems, two original purposes, reflecting two societies' different chief concerns.
- **"Where does kingship come from?":** the Sumerian King List says "kingship descended from heaven"; China later developed the idea of the **"Mandate of Heaven"** (heaven grants a ruler the right to govern). The two **arose independently**, yet both answer the same ancient question — by what right is it *you* who rules? Setting them side by side is not to claim one influenced the other, but to see this: facing the same problem of power, different civilizations came up with answers alike in shape but separate in origin.

Rule 0: we cannot say the two rivers were "earlier, therefore more advanced," nor China "later, therefore backward," nor flip it to elevate China. "Early" and "late" are only time, not rank. Each river civilization answered its own environment (one extremely arid, one monsoon-fed), each with its own wisdom and its own hidden risks. This section only sets side by side two independent human paths "from village to state" — and the most interesting thing is exactly this: **they never copied each other, yet arrived at a similar destination.**

## 9. What Historians Argue About (Historiography)

- **Childe's "urban revolution":** the archaeologist V. Gordon Childe proposed the "urban revolution" in the mid-20th century, listing several markers of civilization (cities, writing, monumental buildings, classes, surplus food). This "checklist" framework is still the teaching mainstream, but is also criticized as too rigid.
- **What was the driver — irrigation or something else?:** an early "hydraulic hypothesis" (Wittfogel) argued that the need to manage large-scale irrigation produced the centralized state. Later archaeology made many scholars doubt this — early irrigation may have been small-scale and community-run, so the state may have been a *result* of, not a precondition for, irrigation. The direction of causation is still debated.
- **Temple vs. palace vs. private economy:** early scholars (like Deimel) once thought the temple owned almost everything (the "temple-state" theory). Later research found private land and royal economies also mattered. Whether early Sumer was "the temple ran everything" or a mix of economies is still being revised.
- **Who were the Sumerians, and where from?:** the Sumerian language is unrelated to its neighbors (a "language isolate"), and the origin of the Sumerian people is still a mystery. The "Sumerian problem" has been argued for over a century.
- **A neutral synthesis:** Sumer was both a great leap in human cooperation and knowledge, AND the starting point of systematic inequality. To tell it only as "the glorious first," or only as "the beginning of oppression," is too one-sided — both are true at once.

## 10. Common Misconceptions (Myth-Busting)

1. ❌ "Cuneiform was made to write poems, stories, and myths." → ✅ It was **made to keep accounts** — 85-90% of early tablets are administrative records (grain, sheep, workers, debts). Literature like the Epic of Gilgamesh appeared only **centuries after** writing matured.
2. ❌ "Sumer was one unified great empire." → ✅ For most of its history it was a set of **competing, often warring city-states** (Uruk, Ur, Eridu…), each with its own god and ruler. The first empire waited until around 2334 BCE with Sargon.
3. ❌ "A ziggurat is a tomb, like the Egyptian pyramids." → ✅ A ziggurat is **not a tomb.** It is a stepped temple platform, with a shrine on top for the city's patron god — a religious and economic center, not a burial place. Don't confuse it with Egyptian pyramids.
4. ❌ "Writing was invented in Egypt/China first, then spread everywhere." → ✅ Scholars widely agree writing was **invented independently at least three times** (the Near East, China, Mesoamerica). Sumerian cuneiform and Chinese oracle-bone script are **separate, independent inventions**, different in principle and form.
5. ❌ "Irrigation only had benefits — it was pure progress." → ✅ Long irrigation in a hot, dry climate caused **salinization** — the soil grew saltier and yields fell, one structural reason some city-states later declined. The technology carried the seed of its own trouble.
6. ❌ "Sumer was a purely glorious beginning of civilization." → ✅ It was also the **first time humans were systematically sorted into the recorded and the recorders** — the first clear classes and slavery. The light and the cost have shared one body since the first ledger.
7. ❌ "Gilgamesh was entirely a real historical person." → ✅ He is in the Sumerian King List and **may** have had a real basis, but the surviving epic (half-god, seeking immortality) is literature and legend, not to be read as archival fact.
8. ❌ "Mesopotamia is the same thing as Sumer." → ✅ Mesopotamia is the whole "land between the rivers" (geography); Sumer is the **southern** region where cities first appeared (culture). Later came Akkad, Babylon, Assyria, and others.

## 11. Thinking Questions (Open-Ended, No Conclusion)

1. Humans invented writing not to write poems or remember heroes, but to keep track of "who owed the temple how much barley." One of the first names ever "remembered forever" was very likely an accountant (Kushim). Does that change how you understand what "writing" is?
2. Among the building blocks of civilization — city, writing, surplus grain, division of labor — each solved an old problem AND created a new inequality. If an invention both makes the many live better AND lets the few rule the many more completely, how would you judge it?
3. The laborer whose name appears once on a ration tablet, and the scribe who wrote that tablet, lived in the same city but in two different worlds. By whose measure should a city's "greatness" be judged?
4. The Mesopotamian and Yellow River valleys had almost no contact, yet each arrived independently at "cities + writing + states." Does this suggest the path of human civilization is some kind of necessity, or a pile-up of countless coincidences?
5. The irrigation that made Sumer strong was also slowly destroying it with salt. A technology that is both the cause of a civilization's rise AND the seed of its decline — can you think of any similar example today?
6. This time you mainly heard the position of the nameless laborer in the field. How would the scribe tell the story of writing's invention? How would the lugal tell the story of the city walls and kingship? Whose voice have you not heard yet?

## Appendix A: Key Timeline

> Note: prehistoric/ancient dates are mostly scholarly estimate-ranges, not exact years. This lesson uses the mainstream "middle chronology."
- c. 5400 BCE: Eridu founded (Ubaid period), the place where kingship "first descended" in legend.
- c. 4000-3500 BCE: surplus grain feeds non-farming people; division of labor and classes appear; the temple becomes the core institution.
- c. 3500-3100 BCE: the Uruk urban revolution peaks — about 40,000 people inside the city, the world's largest.
- c. 3350-3200 BCE: proto-cuneiform is born, the earliest tablets almost all administrative accounts.
- c. 3300 BCE: the Kushim barley-account tablet (one of the earliest names ever written down).
- c. 3200-2900 BCE: signs simplify into wedge strokes; cuneiform takes shape; scribes become an elite profession.
- c. 3000-2350 BCE: lugal (king) power shifts from temple to palace; the turning point anchored by the Gilgamesh legend.
- c. 2334 BCE: Sargon of Akkad conquers the Sumerian city-states, founding humanity's first known empire (Akkad).
- c. 2285 BCE: Enheduanna (Sargon's daughter, high priestess of the moon god at Ur) signs her poems — seen as the first named author in history.
- c. 2112-2004 BCE: the Third Dynasty of Ur (Ur III), a Sumerian cultural revival, builds great ziggurats (such as Ur's great tower).
- c. 1792-1750 BCE: Hammurabi rules Babylon and issues his law code (a link in the written-law tradition, a separate lesson).
- Over centuries: irrigation causes salinization, a structural backdrop to some city-states' decline.
- — Comparison — c. 1900-1500 BCE: China's Erlitou culture (earliest state-level society). c. 1250 BCE: China's oracle-bone script (earliest certain writing).

## Appendix B: Core Vocabulary

- **Mesopotamia** — Greek for "the land between the rivers" (roughly today's Iraq)
- **Sumer** — the southern region of the two rivers where cities first appeared
- **Tigris / Euphrates** — the two great rivers
- **Uruk** — the world's first great city
- **Ur / Eridu** — major city-states (Eridu the oldest in legend)
- **city-state** — a political unit of one city, one patron god, one ruler
- **cuneiform** — wedge-shaped writing (Latin for "wedge"), pressed into clay with a reed
- **proto-cuneiform** — the earliest stage, almost entirely accounts
- **ziggurat** — a stepped temple platform (NOT a tomb)
- **temple economy** — the early dominant economy where the temple ran land, people, storehouses
- **en / ensi** — priestly ruler / city-state leader; **lugal** — "big man" → king
- **dub-sar** — scribe (a literate elite); **stylus** — the reed pen
- **salinization** — irrigation-caused salting of the soil, lowering yields
- **urban revolution** — Childe's framework of markers of civilization
- **Gilgamesh** — legendary king of Uruk / hero of the epic
- **Enheduanna** — the first author in history known by name
