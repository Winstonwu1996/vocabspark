# The Spanish-American War & American Empire · 1898

> **Lock status**: narrative kernel v0 — pending review
>
> **AP anchor**: APUSH Period 7 (1890-1945), Topic 7.3 (The Spanish-American War)
> **Through-line**: how a republic founded against empire became an overseas empire
> **Difficulty**: 4/5 (a fierce national debate + a forgotten, very bloody war)
> **Expected duration**: 35-45 min

---

## 0. Open Your Textbook First (Textbook Anchor)

> This belongs to **APUSH Period 7 (1890-1945)** — Topic 7.3, **The Spanish-American War.** It opens the whole period.
>
> Your textbook will tell you:
> - **The USS Maine exploded in Havana, Cuba, on February 15, 1898**, and soon the U.S. went to war with Spain.
> - **The war was short** — about ten weeks. The U.S. won easily.
> - **The U.S. gained Cuba, Puerto Rico, Guam, the Philippines** — plus it annexed Hawaii the same year.
>
> **What your textbook may NOT make clear** (this is why we need this lesson):
> - **1898 turned the U.S. from a land nation into an overseas empire.** A country founded in 1776 *against* empire suddenly ruled millions of people who never voted for it.
> - **Americans fought bitterly over it.** The AP test does not ask "did the U.S. win?" It asks how Americans **debated** empire: the case FOR (destiny, naval security, the China market, a "civilizing mission") vs the case AGAINST (it breaks the principle of "consent of the governed," it betrays 1776, and it carried racism).
> - **The "splendid little war" was followed by a forgotten, far bloodier one.** The **Philippine-American War (1899-1902)** killed over 4,200 U.S. troops, about 20,000 Filipino fighters, and **at least 200,000 Filipino civilians.**
> - **The China connection is real and close to home.** The **Open Door Policy** (1899-1900) was about the "China market" — at the exact moment foreign powers were carving up China and the **Boxer Rebellion (1900)** broke out. For many Chinese-American students, that carved-up China is where their own ancestors lived.

## 1. One-Sentence Anchor (Many Factions at Once)

At 9:40 p.m. on **February 15, 1898**, the U.S. battleship **USS Maine** sat in the harbor of Havana, the capital of Spain's colony of Cuba. Two explosions tore through it. The ship sank. More than 260 American sailors died. No one could say right away who did it — but the next morning, New York newspapers ran giant headlines blaming Spain. A few months later the U.S. went to war with Spain. A few months after that, Spain's empire was finished, and the U.S. held **Cuba, Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines** — plus **Hawaii**, which it annexed that same year. A republic that always said it stood *against* the empires of Europe had become an empire stretching across the Pacific. But this turn was squeezed out from groups who completely disagreed:

- **Expansionists** — naval strategists, newspaper bosses, missionaries, factory owners wanting new markets. They said this was America's "destiny": the navy needed Pacific coaling stations (fuel stops for ships), factories needed markets in China and Latin America, and civilization needed to be "carried" outward.
- **Anti-imperialists** — from the writer **Mark Twain** to the steel tycoon **Andrew Carnegie** to the politician **William Jennings Bryan.** They said: a nation built on "all men are created equal" and "governments get their power from the **consent of the governed**" was *betraying its own founding* by ruling people who did not consent.
- **Cuban revolutionaries** — they had already fought Spain for years for independence. The U.S. came to "help" — and then attached strings to Cuba's freedom after the war.
- **Filipinos** — they thought the U.S. came to free them from Spain. Instead they found they had been "sold" from one empire's hands into another's — and so they fought again, in a far bloodier war.

Far away, on the other side of the earth, the tempting "China market" was being carved up by the great powers: Germany took Jiaozhou (jyow-JOH), Russia took Port Arthur, Britain took Weihaiwei (way-hai-WAY), France took Guangzhouwan. In 1900 a group of Chinese — the **Boxers** — struck back at this carving-up in the fiercest way they could. The U.S. arrived one step late. It could not grab a "sphere of influence," so it invented the **Open Door**: instead of seizing one piece of China, *demand that everyone be allowed into all of China to trade.* That explosion in Havana harbor and the guns outside Beijing are two ends of the same story.

## 2. The Stage (Geography / Scene / Society / Institutions / Events)

- **Geography**: The 1898 stage spanned two oceans. **The Caribbean**: Cuba (a Spanish colony, just 90 miles from Florida) and Puerto Rico. **The Pacific**: the Philippine Islands (a Spanish colony, but on the other side of the earth, near China), Guam (a small Spanish Pacific island), and Hawaii (an independent kingdom in the middle of the Pacific, a coaling station on the way to Asia). The map itself tells the story: the U.S. went from a nation guarding its own continent to one reaching far into two oceans.
- **Scene**: This was an age of **new technology.** Steel warships had replaced wooden sailing ships — the U.S. had just built a modern steel navy (the "New Navy") in the 1890s. The telegraph carried news across the country in hours. **Cheap mass newspapers** (one or two cents each) fought for readers with shocking headlines and pictures — this was **yellow journalism** (sensational, exaggerated reporting). In Cuba, Spain crushed the rebellion by herding civilians into **reconcentration** camps (forced relocation zones), where huge numbers died of disease and hunger. American papers magnified these horrors, and they lit up public anger.
- **Society**: In the 1890s the U.S. had just "closed" its western frontier (the 1890 census said the frontier line was gone). Industry was booming and producing more goods than Americans could buy, so many believed the U.S. needed overseas markets. At the same time, a "ladder of civilization" idea (shaped by Social Darwinism — the false claim that some races are "more advanced") was popular. It was used **both** to argue "America has a duty to civilize 'backward' peoples" **and** to argue "we should not make people of a 'different race' into Americans." The same racism powered both sides, pointing opposite ways.
- **Institutions**: America's founding document is the Declaration of Independence — "all men are created equal," and governments get their "just powers from the **consent of the governed.**" That sentence is the center of every 1898 debate: how can you believe in "consent" and still rule Cubans and Filipinos who never consented? On the eve of war, Congress passed the **Teller Amendment**, stating the U.S. had **no intention of annexing Cuba** — a product of anti-imperial worry. But after the war, the **Platt Amendment (1901)** gave the U.S. the right to step into Cuba's affairs and keep a naval base there. The system pulled itself back and forth between "freedom" and "empire."
- **Events**: Feb 15, 1898 — the Maine explodes. April — the U.S. goes to war with Spain. May 1 — **Dewey** destroys Spain's Pacific fleet at **Manila Bay.** July — the land battle at **San Juan Hill** in Cuba (Roosevelt and the Rough Riders win fame). August — a ceasefire; the war against Spain lasted only about **ten weeks** (Secretary of State John Hay called it "a splendid little war"). December 10 — the **Treaty of Paris**: Spain gives up Cuba, hands Puerto Rico and Guam to the U.S., and "sells" the Philippines for **$20 million.** February 1899 — the **Philippine-American War** begins; it runs to 1902. 1899-1900 — Hay sends the **Open Door Notes.** In one year, the U.S. became an empire.

## 3. The Main Characters

> Three perspectives (actor/driver, lonely mediator, receiving-end), including at least one powerless party.

### Theodore Roosevelt (actor / driver of empire) — 1858-1919 · from Navy desk to San Juan Hill, pushing America into empire

A real historical person. When the war began in 1898, he was **Assistant Secretary of the Navy.** He was the hottest-blooded believer in the idea that "America must become a sea power." He read **Mahan**, whose book argued a nation's greatness depends on a strong navy and overseas bases — and he believed it completely. There is a famous detail: one afternoon in February 1898, while his boss, Navy Secretary John Long, was out of the office, Roosevelt fired off a telegram ordering Dewey's fleet in Asia to be ready for war and to watch the Spanish fleet at Manila. That one order helped make the victory at Manila Bay possible.

When war came, he would not stay at a desk. He quit his post and personally raised and led a volunteer cavalry unit, the **Rough Riders** — cowboys, miners, and Ivy League athletes all mixed together. In July 1898, in the fighting on the San Juan ridge in Cuba, he led the charge (the real assault, on Kettle Hill / San Juan Hill, also relied on the heavy fighting of the Black 9th and 10th Cavalry — but the newspaper cameras went almost entirely to Roosevelt). He later said, "San Juan was the great day of my life." This made him a national hero. Within two years he was vice president, and after McKinley was assassinated in 1901, he became president.

This perspective puts you inside a *driver of empire.* He sincerely believed expansion would make America stronger, manlier, greater. He also sincerely carried his era's sense of racial superiority — the belief that "civilized" nations had a duty to manage "backward" ones. He is not a cartoon villain. He is a man whose ideals, ambition, courage, and the prejudices of his time were all mixed together. Understanding him is how you understand why so many respectable Americans truly embraced empire in 1898.

### Mark Twain (lonely mediator / the conscience that said no) — 1835-1910 · America's most-loved writer stands up and says "this is not us"

A real historical person. Samuel Clemens, pen name **Mark Twain**, wrote *Tom Sawyer* and *Huckleberry Finn.* In 1898 he was perhaps the most-loved American writer in the world. Here is the hard part: when the war began, even *he* supported it at first — he thought the U.S. was going to help Cubans and Filipinos win their **freedom.** That is exactly what made 1898 so difficult: even the clearest minds were, at first, convinced by the story "we are going to liberate others."

But when the truth came out — that the U.S. was not helping the Philippines become independent, but **taking over** the Philippines and crushing Filipinos who refused to be ruled — Twain turned. He became one of the sharpest voices of the **American Anti-Imperialist League (founded 1898).** He wrote stinging satire (writing that mocks to expose wrongdoing), suggesting a new version of the American flag: paint the white stripes black and replace the stars with skulls and crossbones. His "mediator" role was not between two armies. It was between America's conscience and America's ambition. He loved his country — and *because* he loved it, he could not stand to watch it betray its own founding vow of "all men created equal" and "consent of the governed" by becoming an empire.

This perspective shows you that anti-imperialism was not "unpatriotic." It was another kind of patriotism — it asked, "Who are we, really? We once threw off British rule; how can we now rule others?" The anti-imperialists were not all pure (some opposed empire out of racism, not wanting "non-white" people as citizens). Twain's value is that he argued mainly from principle. AP graders love exactly this complexity: even one side has different motives inside it.

### A young person in a Filipino village (receiving-end · DEFAULT) — a young Filipino "handed over" twice, on the island of Luzon

A bridging character, with a fully real role (this is the DEFAULT perspective). He has no exact name, because history left no names for most of the hundreds of thousands of Filipino civilians who died in this war — and that, exactly, is part of the problem. What is certain is his situation: a young man in a village on **Luzon** (loo-ZON), the main Philippine island. He had grown up under more than three hundred years of **Spanish** colonial rule. From 1896 on, Filipinos were already fighting Spain for independence. In 1898 he heard that a new great power — the United States — had sailed its fleet into Manila Bay and sunk the Spanish fleet in a few hours. At first the village cheered: they thought America had come to drive out Spain and let them rule themselves.

Then the truth came out, piece by piece. In December 1898, in the Treaty of Paris, Spain **sold the Philippines to the U.S. for $20 million** — and not one Filipino was ever asked. Their leader, **Emilio Aguinaldo** (ah-gee-NAHL-do), had already declared Philippine independence and set up a republic. But the U.S. did not recognize it. In February 1899, U.S. and Filipino forces opened fire, and the **Philippine-American War** began. This young man learned that he had not been *freed* — he had simply been handed from the Spanish empire's hands into the American empire's hands.

The next three years were a hell. Filipinos could not win a straight battle, so they fought a guerrilla war (small hit-and-run attacks). U.S. forces fought back by burning villages, herding civilians into camps (concentration), and even using the "water cure" (a form of torture) to force confessions. In this war, over **4,200 U.S. troops** and about **20,000 Filipino fighters** died — and **at least 200,000 Filipino civilians** died, most of them from the famine and disease the war brought (including a cholera epidemic near the end). This perspective puts you in the most overlooked position. In American textbooks, 1898 is often told as "a splendid little war + America rises." But from this village, it was a longer, bloodier, nearly forgotten war. "Were we freed, or were we conquered?" — that question is the heart of this lesson.

## 4. The Story (Plot Beats)

1. **Background: a closed frontier + a steel navy (1890-1897).** The 1890 census declared the western frontier "closed." U.S. industry made more than Americans could buy, and looked for overseas markets. The sea-power thinker Mahan argued a great nation needs a strong navy and overseas bases. The U.S. built a modern steel navy, and its appetite for expansion grew at the same time.
2. **The Cuban revolt + yellow journalism (1895-1898).** Cubans rose again against Spain in 1895. Spain crushed them with reconcentration (herding civilians into camps), where many died of disease and hunger. Hearst's *New York Journal* and Pulitzer's *New York World* used shocking stories (yellow journalism) to magnify the horrors, stir American anger, and sell papers.
3. **The de Lôme letter + the Maine explosion (February 1898).** On Feb 9, a private letter from Spain's minister to the U.S., de Lôme, was stolen and printed — it mocked President McKinley as weak. The nation was outraged. Just 6 days later, on the night of Feb 15, the **USS Maine** exploded and sank in Havana harbor; over 260 sailors died. The cause could not be determined at the time (a 1976 U.S. Navy study concluded the most likely cause was a fire in the ship's own coal bunker igniting the ammunition, **not** Spain). But papers and the public were sure it was Spain. "Remember the Maine!" became the war cry.
4. **War begins (April 1898).** Pushed by public opinion and Congress, McKinley demanded Spain leave Cuba. War broke out in April. Congress also passed the **Teller Amendment**, stating plainly that the U.S. had **no intention of annexing Cuba** and would let Cuba be free after the war — meant to answer the charge that this was just a land grab.
5. **Manila Bay (May 1, 1898).** On the other side of the earth, Dewey's Asiatic Squadron surprised the Spanish fleet at Manila Bay and destroyed it in hours — **with not a single American killed.** A war about Cuba fired its first shot in the Philippines — because Spain's fleet was there, and the U.S. had long eyed that Pacific stepping-stone toward the China market.
6. **The Cuban land war · San Juan Hill (July 1898).** U.S. troops landed in Cuba. Roosevelt's Rough Riders, together with the Black 9th and 10th Cavalry, won the bloody fight on the San Juan ridge. Spain's Caribbean fleet was then destroyed. The war was quick — but far more U.S. troops died of yellow fever and typhoid than of combat.
7. **Hawaii annexed in the rush (July 7, 1898).** The war made the value of a "Pacific coaling station" obvious. Back in 1893, with U.S. warships behind them, a group of American businessmen had overthrown Hawaii's Queen **Liliʻuokalani** (lee-lee-oo-oh-kah-LAH-nee). In July 1898, Congress annexed Hawaii by a **joint resolution** (the Newlands Resolution — needing only a simple majority, getting around the two-thirds vote a treaty needs) — even though more than half of Native Hawaiians had signed a petition against it.
8. **The "splendid little war" ends + the Treaty of Paris (Aug-Dec 1898).** A ceasefire came in August; the whole war against Spain lasted about ten weeks. On December 10, the **Treaty of Paris** was signed: Spain gave up Cuba, handed Puerto Rico and Guam to the U.S., and "sold" the Philippines for **$20 million.** Overnight, the U.S. had overseas possessions.
9. **The great empire debate (1898-1899).** Whether to keep the Philippines set off a fierce debate. Expansionists spoke of destiny, markets, missions, and naval security. The **Anti-Imperialist League** (Twain, Carnegie, Bryan, and others) said: ruling people who refuse to be ruled betrays the founding principle of "consent of the governed." In February 1899, the U.S. Senate approved the Treaty of Paris **by just one vote**, keeping the Philippines.
10. **The Philippine-American War (1899-1902).** In December 1898, McKinley issued his "Benevolent Assimilation" proclamation, claiming the U.S. came to bring "justice." But Filipinos would not accept being taken over. Fighting broke out in February 1899, and the **Philippine-American War** began. Aguinaldo was captured in 1901. This war was far bloodier than the "little war" with Spain: over 4,200 U.S. troops, about 20,000 Filipino fighters, and **at least 200,000 Filipino civilians** died. The U.S. declared the war over in July 1902.
11. **The Open Door Notes (1899-1900).** The U.S. had arrived one step late to empire and could not grab a "sphere of influence" in a China being carved up by other powers. Secretary of State **John Hay** sent the **Open Door Notes**: instead of one piece of land, he demanded that all nations let everyone trade equally throughout China. During the 1900 Boxer crisis, Hay sent a second note, adding a call to preserve "the territorial and administrative integrity" of China. It sounded like "protecting China," but the real base was "keeping America's chance to enter the whole China market."
12. **From a land nation to an overseas empire (frozen after 1898).** After 1898, the U.S. was an empire spanning the Caribbean and the Pacific, ruling millions of non-voting people. The Platt Amendment (1901) made Cuba a protectorate in all but name. The question "Is the U.S. an empire — and should it be?" never really left American politics again.

**An irony to keep:** A nation founded in 1776 *against* imperial rule, on the rule that "the governed must consent," became, in 1898, an empire that ruled others. It went to war under the banner of "helping Cubans and Filipinos win freedom" (the Teller Amendment, Benevolent Assimilation) — then crushed the very people who truly wanted freedom but did not want to be ruled by America.

## 5. Reading the Documents (Primary Sources)

### A. The line from the Declaration of Independence (1776 — the anti-imperialists' weapon)
> Original: "Governments are instituted among Men, deriving their just powers from the consent of the governed."
> Plain reading: "Governments exist for the people, and their rightful power comes from the **consent** of the people they rule."
> Many-sided reading: this is America's own founding vow. The anti-imperialists aimed it back at the 1898 U.S. government — if you rule Cubans and Filipinos who never consented, are you not betraying your own first principle? The same sentence used in 1776 to throw *off* an empire was used in 1898 to argue *against* America becoming one. The power of words is that they turn back to question the people who wrote them.

### B. The Teller Amendment vs the Platt Amendment (1898 vs 1901 — the system pulling at itself)
> Content: The **Teller Amendment (1898)** stated the U.S. claimed "no disposition or intention to exercise sovereignty" over Cuba and would leave it self-governing after the war. The **Platt Amendment (1901)** kept the U.S. right to step into Cuba's affairs, limit its treaties and debts, and keep a naval base (Guantánamo).
> Plain reading: when the war started, America swore "we don't want Cuba"; three years later, it made a law saying "but Cuba's big decisions are ours."
> Reading: put side by side, these two documents are the split mind of 1898. America wanted to keep the self-image of "liberator, not conqueror" (Teller) and also could not let go of the influence it had just gained (Platt). Understanding this contradiction is understanding what a "reluctant empire" means.

### C. McKinley's "Benevolent Assimilation" proclamation (December 21, 1898)
> The core (widely accepted quote): "The mission of the United States is one of benevolent assimilation, substituting the mild sway of justice and right for arbitrary rule."
> Plain reading: "America's mission is 'kind absorption' — replacing Spain's harsh rule with gentle, fair government."
> Reading: this is empire's "good-intentions version" — we are not here to conquer you, we are here to *bring* you better government. The problem: Filipinos were **never asked.** A proclamation that promised "justice and right" was immediately followed by a war that took at least 200,000 Filipino civilian lives. The gap between the language of good intentions and the reality of empire — this document is its clearest sample.

### D. The logic of the Open Door Notes (1899-1900, John Hay)
> Content: Hay's notes asked the powers to promise that, inside each one's "sphere of influence" in China, merchants of all nations would get equal trading rights (same tariffs, ports, railroads). A second note (1900) added a promise to keep China's "territorial and administrative integrity."
> Plain reading: America told the powers — you have already carved China into "spheres," but please let **everyone** (including us) trade throughout all of China; don't shut the door.
> Reading: this was "the latecomer's clever move." America had no army to seize a piece of China, so it changed the rules: don't take one piece, keep the *whole door open.* On the surface it looked like "protecting China from being carved up." Underneath, it was protecting American merchants' chance to enter the whole China market. For China, this was neither invasion nor gift — China was never asked. China was simply the door being "opened."

## 6. Consequences (Short / Medium / Long Term)

- **Short term (1898-1902):** Overnight the U.S. became an empire with overseas possessions — Cuba (protectorate), Puerto Rico, Guam, the Philippines, Hawaii. Roosevelt became famous from the war and was president by 1901. For Spain it was the end of empire (the loss of its last major colonies). For the Philippines it was another war, three years long, costing at least 200,000 civilian lives.
- **Medium term (first half of the 20th century):** 1898 set the pattern of America "reaching overseas." The Platt Amendment let the U.S. dominate Cuba for decades (the Guantánamo base is still in use today). Puerto Rico is still an "unincorporated territory" of the U.S. — its people are U.S. citizens but cannot vote for president — an unsolved "knot of empire" left from 1898. The Philippines did not become independent until 1946. The Open Door became the long-term frame of U.S. policy toward China, drawing America step by step into East Asia's power struggles, on a path that led to its later clash with Japan in the Pacific.
- **Long term (to today):** 1898 is a permanent crack in America's identity — a republic founded on "anti-empire, consent of the governed" ruled, for the first time on a large scale, foreign people who did not consent. "Is the U.S. an empire, and should it be?" has been argued from 1898 to every overseas military action since. At the same time, 1898 first truly placed the U.S. on the world stage, planting the seed of its later rise to superpower. The glory and the cost share one body.

## 7. AP DBQ Thinking Practice

- **Stakeholder grid:** expansionists (navy / press / factory owners / missionaries) / anti-imperialists (principle-based vs race-based) / Cuban revolutionaries / Filipino independence fighters (Aguinaldo) / the McKinley government / Spain / a China being carved up / the great powers — what did each want, and what cards did each hold around 1898? Whose interests briefly overlapped (e.g., the U.S. and Cuban/Filipino rebels agreed on "beat Spain" but completely disagreed on "who gets the colonies after")?
- **The empire debate (the key tested angle):** the case FOR expansion (destiny, naval security, the China market, a civilizing/missionary mission) vs the case AGAINST (it breaks "consent of the governed," it betrays the 1776 anti-empire tradition, and it carried racism) — argue with the evidence: the Teller Amendment, the Anti-Imperialist League, Benevolent Assimilation, the Philippine-American War. Note: **the anti-imperialists had different motives inside their own camp** (Twain argued from principle; others did not want "non-white" people as citizens). This "split inside one side" is what AP graders reward most.
- **Continuity & change:** from "continental expansion" (Manifest Destiny / westward expansion / the 1890 closed frontier) to "overseas empire" (1898) — what continued (the urge to expand, confidence in a "civilizing mission," racial attitudes toward Native and conquered peoples) and what broke (ruling, for the first time, large numbers of non-citizens across an ocean; the first large-scale break with "consent of the governed")?
- **Compare with 1776 and Reconstruction:** the U.S. in 1776 threw off imperial rule, and in 1868 the 14th Amendment extended citizenship to formerly enslaved people; yet in 1898 it ruled overseas territory people who could not vote (Puerto Ricans still cannot). The same "freedom/citizenship" language ran in different directions at home and abroad — an excellent cross-topic comparison.

## 8. China in the Same Era (Structural Comparison · Rule 0 veto dimension · the heart of this lesson's "China bridge")

> This is this lesson's "China bridge." The Open Door is not an abstract policy — its stage was the China being carved up by the great powers, the China where many Chinese-American students' own ancestors lived.

1898 was the 24th year of the Guangxu reign of China's Qing dynasty. After a crushing defeat by Japan in the 1894-1895 war, the Qing was badly weakened and had lost prestige. The powers smelled blood and began what is called the **"scramble for concessions"** (the carving-up of China):

- **The carving-up, on the ground (1897-1898):** Using the killing of two German missionaries in Shandong as a pretext, Germany seized **Jiaozhou Bay** (Qingdao) and claimed Shandong as its sphere; Russia leased **Port Arthur and Dalian** and reached into the northeast (Manchuria); Britain leased **Weihaiwei** and claimed the Yangtze valley; France leased **Guangzhouwan** with a sphere in the southwest; Japan eyed Fujian. In just a year or two, China's coastline was cut into "leased territories" and "spheres of influence." This was the real shape of the tempting "China market" Americans talked about — **others had already started carving it on the table, and the U.S. arrived one step late.**
- **This is where America's "Open Door" came from:** The U.S. had just gotten its footing in the Philippines in 1898 (the Philippines being exactly that Pacific stepping-stone near China), yet could not grab a piece of China. So in 1899-1900, Secretary of State John Hay sent the **Open Door Notes** — not asking for a "sphere," but demanding that all nations let **everyone** trade throughout all of China. For China, this was not protection and not invasion: China was never asked. China was simply the door being "opened."
- **The Boxers (1900) · China strikes back:** In 1900, a group of people in north China called the **Boxers** struck back in the fiercest way they could against this carving-up and the foreign power (including missionary activity) that came with it. They besieged the foreign legation district in Beijing. Eight nations — Britain, France, Germany, Russia, the U.S., Japan, Italy, and Austria-Hungary — formed the **Eight-Nation Alliance**, about 45,000 troops, and stormed Beijing in August 1900. **The U.S. sent troops too** — the first time U.S. forces set foot on Chinese soil to "protect American interests," in line with the Open Door logic. Afterward, the 1901 **Boxer Protocol** forced the Qing to pay an indemnity of 450 million taels of silver (paid over 39 years) — more than the Qing government's annual tax revenue.

Structural comparison (side by side, no ranking):
- **The people at the two ends of "the market":** in Washington and New York, the "China market" was a field of opportunity on a map, an outlet for factories' surplus goods; in Shandong, in Zhili, the "China market" was your own land being taken, a foreign flag flying over your harbor, your old way of life shaken — living, breathing people. The same word, two completely different experiences at its two ends.
- **Two peoples being "opened / ruled":** Filipinos were "liberated into a possession," Chinese were "opened into a market." Between 1898 and 1900, the two hands of American overseas empire pressed down — one on the occupation of the Philippines, one on the door of China. For many Chinese-American students, this section is not distant history: the carved-up, "Open Door" China of those years is the very China their ancestors lived in.

Rule 0: we cannot say the powers (including the U.S.) were "advanced" and the Qing "backward," nor the reverse, nor can we simply write the Boxers as heroes or as a mob — the Boxers were both a real resistance against invasion AND a movement with violence against foreigners and Chinese Christians; both are true at once. The Open Door, too, cannot be written simply as "America protected China" or "America invaded China" — its base was commercial opportunity; in effect it both limited the most naked carving-up AND never treated China as a party with a say. This section only sets the two ends of one "empire/market" logic, on two sides of the earth in one year, side by side.

## 9. What Historians Argue About (Historiography)

- **The "reluctant / accidental empire" view:** an older view holds that the U.S. was "swept" into empire by yellow journalism, the Maine, and a moment of public feeling — not by design. It stresses chance and public pressure.
- **The "market expansion / Open Door" interpretation (e.g., William Appleman Williams):** holds that 1898 was no accident but the necessary result of an industrial U.S. systematically searching for overseas markets — the Open Door is the program of this "market empire," not a chance act of goodwill.
- **The cultural / racial reading:** stresses how racism, Social Darwinism, the "civilizing mission," and "the white man's burden" drove both expansionists and some anti-imperialists — empire was not only economics and military, but a set of ideas about "who deserves self-rule."
- **The Filipino / postcolonial reading:** shifts the focus from "America's rise" to the **conquered**, stressing that the scale and cost of the Philippine-American War (at least 200,000 civilian deaths) were long downplayed in the U.S. mainstream story, and arguing this was a forgotten colonial war, not a "minor episode."
- **A neutral synthesis:** 1898 was both a moment when the U.S. was pushed by public feeling and opportunity, AND the result of long-building industrial, naval, market, and racial forces; it was both sincerely seen as a "mission of liberation" AND truly caused occupation and mass death. Multiple motives are true at once; do not make it either/or.

## 10. Common Misconceptions (Myth-Busting)

1. ❌ "Spain blew up the Maine, so the U.S. went to war." → ✅ The cause was **never determined** at the time; papers and the public assumed Spain. A 1976 U.S. Navy study concluded the most likely cause was a **fire in the ship's own coal bunker** igniting the ammunition, not Spain. The Maine was a trigger and a catalyst for public opinion, not a proven Spanish attack.
2. ❌ "The Spanish-American War was about Cuba, so it was mostly fought in Cuba." → ✅ The first big victory, on May 1, was in the **Philippines** (Manila Bay), because the U.S. had long eyed that Pacific stepping-stone to the China market. This "war about Cuba" had a larger Pacific/China ambition from the start.
3. ❌ "The U.S. went to free the Filipinos." → ✅ The U.S. **took over** the Philippines (buying it from Spain for $20 million in the Treaty of Paris) and crushed Filipinos who wanted independence; the Philippine-American War (1899-1902) took **at least 200,000 Filipino civilian** lives.
4. ❌ "The Spanish-American War was a quick 'splendid little war.'" → ✅ The war against **Spain** was indeed only about ten weeks; but the **Philippine-American War that followed ran three years** and was far deadlier. Seen together, 1898 was not "little" at all.
5. ❌ "San Juan Hill was Roosevelt and the Rough Riders alone." → ✅ The Black 9th and 10th Cavalry fought and bled in the same battle, but the press cameras went almost entirely to Roosevelt; their part was long buried.
6. ❌ "The Open Door was America protecting China." → ✅ Its core was protecting **American merchants' access to the whole China market;** it never asked China, which was the object being "opened," not a partner being protected. It did, in effect, slow the naked carving-up — but the motive was commercial gain.
7. ❌ "Hawaii joined the U.S. peacefully and willingly." → ✅ In 1893 Queen Liliʻuokalani was overthrown by American businessmen backed by U.S. warships; at annexation in 1898, more than half of Native Hawaiians had signed a petition **against** it.
8. ❌ "The anti-imperialists were all noble humanitarians." → ✅ The Anti-Imperialist League included both people who argued from principle (Twain) and people who opposed annexation out of **racism** (not wanting "non-white" people as U.S. citizens). One camp, but not one motive.

## 11. Thinking Questions (Open-Ended, No Conclusion)

1. The U.S. was founded in 1776 on "throwing off imperial rule" and "government by the consent of the governed." In 1898 it ruled Filipinos who did not consent. Can a nation believe in a principle and break it at the same time? When principle and interest collide, is the principle changing, or are people choosing when to use it?
2. McKinley said America's mission was "benevolent assimilation," to *bring* Filipinos better government. If a powerful nation truly believes it is helping you, but never asks whether you want it — is there any real difference between "kind rule" and just "rule"?
3. The same words — "China market," "opening," "opportunity" — meant a field of chances to a New York factory owner and your own land being taken to a farmer in Shandong. When you hear "market," "open," "opportunity," do you ask: open *for whom*, and pried open *against whom*?
4. American textbooks often tell 1898 as "a splendid little war + America rises," while the Philippine-American War (at least 200,000 civilian deaths) gets a single line. In any history, what decides whose story is remembered and whose is forgotten?
5. Among the anti-imperialists, some acted from principle (Twain) and some from racism. If two people do the same "right" thing, but one's motive is noble and one's is ugly — is the thing still "right"? Does the motive matter, or the result?
6. This time you mostly stood in a Filipino village to hear this story. How would Roosevelt tell that day at San Juan Hill? How would the New York newspaper boss tell the Maine explosion? How would a farmer in Shandong tell the "Open Door"? Whose voice have you not heard yet?

## Appendix A: Key Timeline

- 1890: U.S. census declares the western "frontier" closed; Mahan's *The Influence of Sea Power upon History* (1890) urges a strong navy + overseas bases.
- 1893.1.17: Hawaii's Queen Liliʻuokalani overthrown, with American businessmen and U.S. Marines behind the coup.
- 1895: Cuba rises again against Spain.
- 1898.2.9: The de Lôme letter is printed, mocking McKinley; national outrage.
- 1898.2.15 (9:40 p.m.): USS Maine explodes and sinks in Havana harbor; 260+ sailors die. (A 1976 Navy study leans toward an internal coal-bunker fire, not Spain.)
- 1898.4: U.S. goes to war with Spain; Congress passes the Teller Amendment (no intention to annex Cuba).
- 1898.5.1: Dewey destroys Spain's Pacific fleet at Manila Bay, with not one American killed.
- 1898.7.1: The San Juan / Kettle Hill land battle; the Rough Riders + the Black 9th/10th Cavalry win.
- 1898.7.7: McKinley signs the Newlands Resolution, annexing Hawaii.
- 1898.8: Ceasefire with Spain (the whole war ~10 weeks, "a splendid little war").
- 1898.12.10: Treaty of Paris — Spain gives up Cuba, cedes Puerto Rico and Guam, sells the Philippines for $20 million.
- 1898.12.21: McKinley issues the "Benevolent Assimilation" proclamation.
- 1899.2.4: The Philippine-American War begins.
- 1899.2.6: The U.S. Senate ratifies the Treaty of Paris by just one vote (keeping the Philippines).
- 1899.9.6: John Hay sends the first Open Door Note.
- 1900: The Boxer Rebellion; the Eight-Nation Alliance (including U.S. troops) storms Beijing in August; Hay sends a second Open Door Note (adding "China's territorial and administrative integrity").
- 1901: The Boxer Protocol — the Qing pays an indemnity of 450 million taels of silver; the U.S. passes the Platt Amendment (the right to intervene in Cuba).
- 1901.3.23: Aguinaldo is captured.
- 1902.7.4: The U.S. declares the Philippine-American War over.

## Appendix B: Core Vocabulary

- **USS Maine** — the U.S. battleship that exploded in Havana harbor on Feb 15, 1898; the war's trigger
- **yellow journalism** — the sensational, exaggerated newspaper style of Hearst / Pulitzer
- **de Lôme letter** — a private letter by Spain's minister mocking McKinley, stolen and printed
- **"Remember the Maine!"** — the pro-war slogan
- **Teller Amendment (1898)** — stated the U.S. had no intention to annex Cuba
- **Platt Amendment (1901)** — gave the U.S. the right to intervene in Cuba and keep a base (Guantánamo)
- **Battle of Manila Bay (May 1, 1898)** — Dewey destroys Spain's Pacific fleet
- **Rough Riders** — the volunteer cavalry led by Roosevelt
- **Treaty of Paris (Dec 10, 1898)** — Spain cedes territory and sells the Philippines to the U.S.
- **Anti-Imperialist League (1898)** — Twain, Carnegie, Bryan, and others opposing annexation of the Philippines
- **Benevolent Assimilation** — McKinley's phrase justifying the takeover of the Philippines
- **Emilio Aguinaldo** — leader of the Philippine independence movement / republic
- **Open Door Policy (1899-1900)** — demanding all nations get equal access to the whole China market
- **consent of the governed** — the anti-imperialists' core principle weapon
- **reconcentration** — herding civilians into camps, used by Spain in Cuba, then by the U.S. in the Philippines
- **Boxer Rebellion (1900)** — a north-China uprising against the carving-up and foreign power
- **Eight-Nation Alliance (1900)** — including U.S. troops, stormed Beijing
- **scramble for concessions (1897-1898)** — the powers seizing leased territories and spheres of influence in China
